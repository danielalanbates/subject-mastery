# Gravity and Planetary Orbits

Imagine you are whirling a heavy rubber ball on a sturdy string in a wide circle above your head. You feel a steady, inward pull on your hand. If you let go of the string, the ball does not curve away or fly straight out from your hand. Instead, it flashes off in a straight line along the direction it was moving the exact moment you released it. Planets and moons trace out vast circular or slightly stretched oval paths through empty space without any strings attached. That invisible bond is gravity, acting across millions of miles of the vacuum of space.

Sir Isaac Newton realized that the exact same force pulling an apple down from a tree branch also reaches out to hold the moon in its monthly circuit around the earth. He formulated the law of universal gravitation, which states that the gravitational force between two point masses equals the gravitational constant multiplied by the product of the two masses, divided by the square of the distance between their centers. Put into words, if you double the mass of one object, the gravitational pull doubles. But if you double the distance separating the two objects, the gravitational force drops not by half, but by a factor of four. That inverse square relationship explains why gravity weakens so rapidly as celestial bodies move apart.

To understand how this force creates an orbit, think about firing a cannon horizontally from the peak of an extremely tall mountain that reaches far above the atmosphere. If the gunpowder charge is small, the cannonball drops quickly and hits the ground a short distance away. If you add more gunpowder, the ball flies farther before gravity pulls it down to the earth. Now imagine launching that cannonball with tremendous speed. As the ball curves downward toward the ground, the surface of the earth curves away beneath it at the exact same rate. The cannonball is essentially falling continuously toward the center of the earth, but it moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing the ground entirely. That perpetual falling motion is what an orbit actually is.

Let us walk through a practical numerical example using a satellite orbiting near the surface of the earth. Suppose we want to find the orbital speed of a satellite skimming just above the atmosphere, where the orbital radius is roughly six point four times ten to the sixth meters, and the mass of the earth is about six times ten to the twenty-fourth kilograms. We set the gravitational force on the satellite equal to the mass of the satellite times its velocity squared, divided by the orbital radius. Notice that the mass of the satellite appears on both sides of the equation and cancels out entirely, meaning a heavy satellite and a light satellite travel at the exact same speed at that altitude.

We solve for the velocity by multiplying the gravitational constant by the mass of the earth, dividing by the orbital radius, and then taking the square root of that result. Using the standard gravitational constant of six point six seven times ten to the negative eleven newton square meters per kilogram squared, the calculation yields a speed of about seven thousand nine hundred meters per second, or seven point nine kilometers per second. That is about twenty-three times the speed of sound in air, a staggering velocity required to keep the satellite from falling back to the ground.

Johannes Kepler had mapped out the geometric patterns of planetary paths decades before Newton explained the physical cause behind them. Kepler observed that planets sweep out equal areas of space in equal amounts of time, meaning a planet moves faster when it is closer to the sun and slower when it is farther away. Furthermore, Kepler's third law states that the square of a planet's orbital period is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbital path. Newton showed that his law of gravitation naturally produces those exact planetary rules, including that third law relationship. The path is typically an ellipse, which is a flattened circle with two focal points rather than one single center. The sun sits at one of those foci, drawing the planet inward during its sweep through the solar system.

It is worth noting that this classical description treats gravity as an instantaneous pull across empty space. While Newton's equations remain remarkably accurate for almost all everyday space navigation and satellite placement, twentieth century physics later revealed that gravity travels at the speed of light through warped space and time. Even with that modern refinement, the fundamental mechanics of orbital balance remain unchanged. As we turn our attention next toward fluids and thermodynamics, we will see how these same gravitational forces govern the pressures deep inside liquid oceans and planetary atmospheres.

## Key ideas
- Newton's law of universal gravitation states that every particle attracts every other particle with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.
- An orbit is a state of continuous free fall where an object's sideways velocity keeps it curving around a central body instead of crashing into it.
- Kepler's third law establishes that the square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit, a result naturally derived from Newton's law of gravitation.
- Doubling the distance between two masses reduces the gravitational pull between them by a factor of four due to the inverse square nature of the interaction.
