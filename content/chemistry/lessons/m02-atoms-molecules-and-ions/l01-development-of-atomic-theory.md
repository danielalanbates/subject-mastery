# The Development of Atomic Theory

Imagine you are holding a single grain of salt. If you could slice that grain in half, and then half again, and continue this process indefinitely, would you eventually reach a point where the piece is too small to be divided further? This question has fascinated thinkers for thousands of years. While our previous study of unit conversions helped us measure the physical world, we now turn our attention to the invisible building blocks that make up that world. The journey to understand the atom is a story of clever experiments and bold logical leaps.

In the early nineteenth century, an English schoolteacher named John Dalton provided the first scientific framework for atomic theory. Building on the work of others who observed that matter is neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions, Dalton explained the law of conservation of mass. He proposed that elements consist of tiny, indestructible particles called atoms. He argued that all atoms of a given element are identical in mass and properties, while atoms of different elements differ. Crucially, he proposed that compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in fixed, whole-number ratios. This explained the law of definite proportions, stated a few years earlier by the French chemist Joseph Proust: a given compound always contains its elements in the same proportions by mass. If you take carbon and oxygen to form carbon monoxide, they always combine in the same mass ratio. Furthermore, Dalton noted that the same elements can sometimes combine in different ratios to form different compounds, a concept called the law of multiple proportions. For instance, carbon and oxygen can form carbon monoxide, where one carbon atom binds to one oxygen atom, or carbon dioxide, where one carbon atom binds to two oxygen atoms. So for the same mass of carbon, carbon dioxide contains exactly twice the mass of oxygen that carbon monoxide does, a simple whole-number ratio of two to one.

For nearly a century, Dalton’s model of the atom as a solid, indivisible sphere stood firm. However, as technology advanced, scientists began to look inside the sphere. In the late eighteen hundreds, J.J. Thomson used a cathode ray tube, a vacuum tube that produces a beam of particles, to study electricity. He noticed that the beam was deflected by magnetic and electric fields. Because the beam bent toward the positive plate, he concluded it must be made of negatively charged particles, which we now call electrons. By measuring the degree of deflection, Thomson calculated the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron. He proposed a model where these tiny negative electrons were embedded in a positively charged sphere, much like raisins in a pudding.

Shortly after, Robert Millikan refined our understanding of these particles. In his famous oil drop experiment, he sprayed tiny droplets of oil into a chamber and observed how they moved between charged metal plates. By adjusting the strength of the electric field, he could suspend the droplets in midair. By measuring the charge required to balance the gravity acting on those droplets, Millikan calculated the exact charge of a single electron. Combining this with Thomson’s charge-to-mass ratio, scientists were finally able to determine the mass of the electron, which is incredibly small compared to the mass of an atom.

The final major shift in this early era came from Ernest Rutherford. In nineteen eleven, he and his team fired alpha particles, which are heavy, positively charged bits of matter, at a thin sheet of gold foil. Most of the particles passed straight through, just as expected. To their shock, a small fraction bounced back at sharp angles. Rutherford famously remarked that it was as if you fired a fifteen-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back to hit you. He realized the atom could not be a uniform pudding. Instead, almost all of the mass and all of the positive charge must be concentrated in a tiny, dense core at the center, which he named the nucleus. The electrons, he concluded, must occupy the vast, empty space surrounding this core. This discovery transformed our view of the atom from a solid ball into a miniature system with a dense center and orbiting electrons.

## Key ideas

- John Dalton’s atomic theory explained the law of conservation of mass and the law of definite proportions, establishing that atoms combine in fixed, whole-number ratios.
- J.J. Thomson discovered the electron and used cathode ray experiments to determine its charge-to-mass ratio.
- Robert Millikan’s oil drop experiment provided the precise charge of the electron, allowing for the calculation of its mass.
- Ernest Rutherford’s gold foil experiment proved that the atom contains a small, dense, positively charged nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space.
