# Fighting World War Two

Imagine standing on the deck of a transport ship in the freezing, gray waters of the North Atlantic, or staring into the thick, humid jungle of a Pacific island. By 1942, the United States had been thrust into a global conflict that spanned two oceans and multiple continents. Following the shock of Pearl Harbor, the American military machine shifted from a peacetime footing to an industrial juggernaut. The strategy, largely determined by President Franklin Roosevelt and his military advisors, focused first on defeating Nazi Germany in Europe, while maintaining a holding action against Japan in the Pacific.

In the European theater, the Allied effort was a massive collaborative undertaking. After securing North Africa in 1943, American and British forces pushed into Italy, slowly grinding their way up the peninsula. The decisive turning point came on June 6, 1944. Known as D-Day, the Allied invasion of the beaches of Normandy, France, opened the long-awaited Western Front. From those sands, Allied troops pushed eastward, eventually meeting the Soviet Union’s forces, which were crushing the German army from the east. By May 1945, the Nazi regime had collapsed, and Germany surrendered.

The war was fought by a diverse American military. African American soldiers served in segregated units, such as the pilots of the Tuskegee Airmen and the tankers of the 761st Battalion, despite facing discrimination at home. Navajo code talkers used their native language to create unbreakable codes that proved vital to success in the Pacific. Meanwhile, women served in auxiliary branches like the Women’s Army Corps and the Women Accepted for Volunteer Emergency Service, performing essential roles that freed men for combat. Immigrant laborers also filled critical gaps in the workforce, ensuring the military remained supplied.

Simultaneously, the war in the Pacific was defined by a brutal, island-hopping strategy. American commanders bypassed heavily defended positions, instead capturing key islands to build airfields. This allowed the United States to strike closer to the Japanese home islands. Battles at Midway, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa were characterized by intense, close-quarters combat and staggering casualties. The Japanese military, bound by a code of honor that prioritized death over surrender, fought with a ferocity that deeply unsettled American commanders and soldiers alike.

During these years, the United States and its allies also confronted the horrifying reality of the Holocaust. As Allied troops liberated concentration and death camps across Europe, they uncovered the systematic, state-sponsored murder of six million Jews. The Nazis also murdered millions of others, including Romani people, people with disabilities, Soviet prisoners of war, and political dissidents. Before the war, the American government had been aware of the rising persecution in Germany, but immigration quotas and a pervasive culture of isolationism and antisemitism had limited the number of refugees allowed to enter the country. While the United States eventually created the War Refugee Board in 1944 to assist those fleeing the Nazis, historians often point to this period as a profound moral failure of the international community to act earlier against the genocide.

As the war in Europe ended, the focus shifted to the Pacific. Planning for a full-scale invasion of the Japanese mainland suggested that the cost in human lives would be catastrophic for both American forces and Japanese civilians. President Harry Truman, who had assumed office following Roosevelt’s death in April 1945, faced a momentous decision. Scientists working on the top-secret Manhattan Project had successfully tested the first atomic bomb in the New Mexico desert. Truman authorized the use of these weapons to force a Japanese surrender without a mainland invasion. In August 1945, the United States dropped atomic bombs on the cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, killing over one hundred thousand people instantly and leaving thousands more to die from radiation sickness.

The debate over the use of the atomic bomb remains one of the most contentious topics in American history. Supporters argue that the bombs ended the war quickly and saved hundreds of thousands of lives that would have been lost in a conventional invasion. Critics contend that Japan was already near defeat and that the bombs were used partly to intimidate the Soviet Union, signaling the start of a new, nuclear-armed era of global politics. Some also argue that the targeting of civilian-populated cities was morally indefensible. This decision brought a violent end to the Second World War and fundamentally changed the nature of global warfare and diplomacy.

## Key ideas

- The United States pursued a two-front strategy that prioritized the defeat of Nazi Germany in Europe before focusing full military might on the Pacific theater.
- The American military relied on a diverse force, including African American combat units, Navajo code talkers, and women in auxiliary services, to sustain the global war effort.
- The Allied invasion of Normandy on D-Day proved to be the decisive turning point in the European war, leading to the eventual collapse of the Nazi regime.
- Allied forces uncovered the full extent of the Holocaust as they liberated concentration camps, revealing a systemic genocide that remains a central point of historical and moral study.
- President Harry Truman authorized the use of atomic weapons on Hiroshima and Nagasaki to hasten the end of the war, a decision that continues to spark intense debate among historians regarding its military necessity and ethical implications.
