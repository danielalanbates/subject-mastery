# Revolution, Depression and Dictators

Imagine standing in a crowded public square in Berlin in 1932. The air is cold, and you have been unemployed for two years. Your life savings were wiped out, first by the hyperinflation of 1923, and now by a global economic paralysis. In the distance, you hear two competing rallies. On one corner, communists promise a worker-led utopia, pointing to the newly formed Soviet Union. On another corner, national socialists promise to restore German pride, blaming minority groups and foreign powers for your suffering. This was the volatile reality of the interwar years. The devastation of World War One, which we explored in our last lesson, did not bring lasting peace. Instead, it fractured the global order, unleashing a wave of revolution, economic ruin, and aggressive dictatorship.

The first major fracture occurred in Russia during World War One. By 1917, the Russian Empire was buckling under the weight of military defeat and severe food shortages. In this chaos, Tsar Nicholas the Second abdicated, and by late autumn, a radical socialist group called the Bolsheviks, led by Vladimir Lenin, seized power. The Bolsheviks established the world's first communist state, later organized as the Soviet Union. Communism, in theory, sought a classless society where the workers owned the means of production. In practice, under Lenin and his ruthless successor, Joseph Stalin, it meant absolute state control. Stalin launched rapid industrialization and the forced collectivization of agriculture. This state-controlled economic shift came at a devastating human cost, including the man-made famine of 1932 and 1933 that killed millions, above all in Ukraine and Kazakhstan, and political purges that eliminated any perceived opposition.

While the Soviet Union built its closed, state-run economy, the capitalist world experienced a brief, roaring boom in the 1920s, followed by a spectacular crash. In October 1929, the United States stock market collapsed. Because the global economy was deeply interconnected through wartime debts and American loans, the crash triggered the Great Depression. This was not a typical economic downturn; it was a global paralysis. Industrial production plummeted, banks failed, and the value of world trade fell by about two-thirds between 1929 and 1934.

This global economic paralysis did not just affect factories; it devastated agriculture and trade worldwide. In colonial regions of Africa and Asia, the collapse of commodity prices meant that local farmers could no longer afford basic goods, sparking protests against colonial rule. In Europe, the economic pain was directly tied to political instability. In Germany, unemployment reached thirty percent by 1932. It was in this atmosphere of extreme anxiety that many middle-class citizens, fearing a communist revolution like the one in Russia, threw their support behind radical right-wing parties.

This widespread misery created fertile ground for radical political movements. Many citizens lost faith in democracy and capitalism, turning instead to authoritarian leaders who promised quick, decisive action. In Italy, Benito Mussolini had already seized power in 1922, pioneering a political ideology known as fascism. Fascism is characterized by extreme nationalism, the rejection of democratic institutions, a cult of leadership, and the subordination of individual interests to the power of the state. Mussolini promised to restore the glory of the ancient Roman Empire through militarism and strict social order.

In Germany, Adolf Hitler used the desperation of the Great Depression to propel his Nazi Party to power; he was appointed chancellor in January 1933. Hitler combined fascism with virulent antisemitism and racial theories, blaming Jewish people and communists for Germany’s defeat in World War One and its subsequent economic ruin. Once in power, Hitler dismantled democratic institutions, rebuilt the German military in defiance of international treaties, and sought what he called "living space" for the German people. Meanwhile, in Asia, Japan faced its own economic crises. Imperial Japan shifted toward a military-dominated government that sought to solve its resource shortages by invading neighboring Manchuria in 1931 and launching a full-scale invasion of China in 1937.

By the late 1930s, the international system established after World War One had completely broken down. The League of Nations, created to prevent future conflicts, proved powerless to stop the aggression of these expansionist dictatorships. The rise of militaristic regimes in Europe and Asia, coupled with the ideological clash between communism, fascism, and liberal democracy, created a highly combustible global environment. The fragile peace of the interwar years was about to shatter, paving the way for the most destructive conflict in human history: World War Two and the horrors of the Holocaust.

## Key ideas

- The Russian Revolution of 1917 established the world's first communist state, which under Joseph Stalin transformed into a highly centralized, authoritarian regime that used state terror and forced collectivization to rapidly industrialize.
- The Great Depression of 1929 was a global economic collapse that severely damaged democratic governments, crippled international trade, and caused widespread unemployment and political instability across the globe.
- Fascism emerged as a powerful political force in Italy and Germany, characterized by extreme nationalism, totalitarian control, militarism, and the suppression of individual liberties in favor of the state.
- Imperial Japan responded to economic hardship by embracing militarism and launching aggressive territorial expansion in Asia, beginning with the invasion of Manchuria in 1931.
- The failure of democratic nations and the League of Nations to contain the aggressive expansion of fascist and militarist regimes directly set the stage for World War Two.
