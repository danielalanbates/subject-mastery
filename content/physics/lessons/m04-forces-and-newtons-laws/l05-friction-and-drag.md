# Friction and Drag

Imagine sliding a heavy wooden chest across a living room floor. You push steadily, yet the moment you stop pushing, the chest grinds to a halt. In our last lesson, we explored how gravity pulls downward and how the normal force pushes straight back up, yet neither of those vertical forces explains why that chest refuses to coast across the room. The culprit is friction, a silent resistive force born from the microscopic roughness of touching surfaces. Even a surface that looks polished to the naked eye resembles a jagged mountain range under a microscope. When two objects press together, tiny peaks and valleys catch on one another, creating a stubborn mechanical resistance that fights any attempt at sliding motion.

To understand this mathematically, physicists divide friction into two distinct flavors. Static friction acts when an object is completely at rest relative to a surface. As you begin to push our heavy chest, static friction matches your push force exactly, keeping the chest stationary. If you push with ten newtons, static friction exerts ten newtons in the opposite direction. As you push harder, static friction rises to meet you, right up to a strict limit known as the maximum static friction. This maximum limit equals the coefficient of static friction multiplied by the normal force. For most materials, this static coefficient is somewhat higher than the kinetic coefficient you encounter once motion begins. Once your push exceeds that static limit, the microscopic bonds shear apart, and the chest breaks free into motion.

The moment sliding begins, we cross into kinetic friction. Kinetic friction is the resistive force that acts on objects already sliding across one another. Remarkably, kinetic friction is generally weaker than the maximum static friction, which is why it always feels easier to keep a heavy piece of furniture moving than it did to get it started in the first place. Furthermore, kinetic friction is approximately independent of sliding speed for most common materials over typical ranges. Both static and kinetic friction depend directly on two factors: the roughness of the materials, represented by a dimensionless property called the coefficient of friction, and the magnitude of the normal force pressing the surfaces together. The harder the surfaces are squeezed into each other, the greater the frictional resistance.

Consider a small crate resting on a warehouse floor with a mass of fifty kilograms, meaning gravity pulls down with a force of four hundred ninety newtons, matched by an equal normal force upward. If the coefficient of kinetic friction between the wood and the concrete is zero point two, we can calculate the sliding resistance. Multiplying the normal force of four hundred ninety newtons by zero point two gives a kinetic friction force of ninety-eight newtons. If a worker pulls the crate with a constant force of ninety-eight newtons, the forward pull exactly balances the backward friction, resulting in zero net force and a steady, constant speed. If the worker pulls with one hundred fifty newtons instead, a net force of fifty-two newtons remains, causing the crate to accelerate across the floor.

When objects move through fluids rather than across solid surfaces, a different kind of resistive force takes over, known as drag. While solid friction arises from direct surface contact, drag arises because an object must physically push fluid molecules out of its way. Whether that fluid is water or air, the moving object experiences a retarding force that grows noticeably stronger as speed increases. The exact nature of this force depends on the flow regime, which is characterized by a dimensionless value called the Reynolds number. At very low speeds and small scales, corresponding to low Reynolds numbers, drag is directly proportional to velocity. At higher Reynolds numbers, where flow becomes turbulent, drag increases dramatically with the square of the speed. For everyday objects moving through air, like a car cruising down a highway or a skydiver falling through the sky, this quadratic drag dominates. The full drag equation states that the resistive force equals one half multiplied by the fluid density, the drag coefficient, the cross sectional area, and the square of the speed. Double your speed in this quadratic regime, and the wind resistance quadruples.

This squared relationship leads directly to the concept of terminal velocity. When a skydiver leaps from an airplane, gravity pulls downward with a constant force, causing the jumper to accelerate. As speed increases, the upward drag force from the rushing air grows stronger and stronger. Eventually, the upward drag matches the downward pull of gravity precisely. At that exact moment, the net force drops to zero, acceleration stops, and the skydiver continues falling at a steady, unchanging maximum speed. Understanding how these resistive forces balance out sets the stage for our next lesson, where we will assemble all these interactions together to master free body reasoning.

## Key ideas
- Friction is a resistive force caused by microscopic roughness between touching surfaces, with the coefficient of static friction typically exceeding the coefficient of kinetic friction.
- Static friction prevents stationary objects from moving and matches applied forces up to a maximum limit, while kinetic friction opposes objects that are already sliding and remains approximately independent of sliding speed.
- The magnitude of kinetic friction, and of the maximum possible static friction, equals the matching coefficient of friction multiplied by the normal force pressing the surfaces together.
- Fluid drag transitions from being linear with velocity at very low Reynolds numbers to proportional to the square of velocity at higher Reynolds numbers, incorporating fluid density, drag coefficient, cross sectional area, and a factor of one half.
- Terminal velocity occurs when the upward drag force equals the downward force of gravity, resulting in zero net force and a constant falling speed.
