# The American and French Revolutions

Imagine a world where kings rule by divine right, where a person’s entire life—their taxes, their labor, and their social standing—is dictated by the accident of their birth. In our last lesson, we explored how Enlightenment thinkers like John Locke and Jean-Jacques Rousseau began to challenge this ancient order with radical ideas about liberty, natural rights, and the social contract. But those ideas remained ink on paper until the late 1700s, when ordinary people and revolutionaries decided to put them into practice, transforming abstract philosophy into explosive political action.

The first major tremor of this revolutionary age shook the British colonies in North America. By the 1770s, colonists who had grown accustomed to governing themselves chafed against new taxes and tighter imperial controls imposed by London, famously protesting taxation without representation. British troops and colonial militiamen exchanged gunfire at Lexington and Concord in 1775. A local dispute over colonial administration rapidly escalated into a war for independence. When the Continental Congress formally adopted the Declaration of Independence in 1776, written primarily by Thomas Jefferson, it did something unprecedented in statecraft. It justified breaking away from a monarch not by citing ancient charters or local privileges, but by appealing to universal human rights.

The American victory over the British Empire proved that a powerful monarchical state could be defeated by a mobilized populace fighting for self-governance. Following the war, American leaders drafted and adopted the United States Constitution in 1787, establishing a federal republic with a system of checks and balances. Yet the American Revolution was fundamentally a political separation rather than a complete overturning of domestic society. While it established a constitutional republic built on Enlightenment principles, it preserved existing social hierarchies. It left the institution of chattel slavery intact and extended political rights almost exclusively to propertied white men. Even so, women like Abigail Adams actively advocated for the inclusion of women's rights in the new political framework, warning her husband to remember the ladies in the new code of laws.

Across the Atlantic Ocean, the financial strains of supporting the American war effort pushed the French absolute monarchy to the brink of collapse. In 1789, King Louis the Sixteenth summoned the Estates-General, a representative assembly that had not met for over a century, to solve a spiraling national debt crisis. Instead of rubber-stamping royal decrees, the commoners of the third estate, joined by reform-minded members of the clergy and nobility, broke away to form the National Assembly. They demanded a constitution and declared that sovereignty rested with the nation rather than the crown. On July 14, 1789, an angry crowd in Paris stormed the Bastille, a medieval fortress and prison symbolizing royal tyranny, violently signaling that the people would no longer tolerate absolute rule.

The French Revolution was deeply influenced by the American precedent, while also inspiring French revolutionary thought. Figures such as the Marquis de Lafayette, who had fought in the American war for independence, helped draft the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. This new charter asserted that all men are born free and equal in rights, directly echoing Enlightenment philosophy. Women like Olympe de Gouges challenged the gender exclusions of this philosophy by publishing the Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen, arguing that women deserved equal political rights.

As foreign armies threatened France's borders and internal dissent boiled over, the revolution moved through distinct internal political phases. It shifted from a constitutional monarchy to a radical republic under the National Convention, which abolished the monarchy and executed King Louis the Sixteenth by guillotine. This radical phase culminated in the Reign of Terror under the leadership of Maximilien Robespierre, where revolutionary tribunals executed thousands of suspected counter-revolutionaries. Following the fall of Robespierre, the revolution experienced the Thermidorian Reaction and the establishment of the Directory, a five-member executive branch that governed France until the end of the century. Throughout these internal shifts, the Revolutionary Wars carried French armies across Europe, spreading revolutionary ideas, abolishing feudalism in conquered territories, and provoking fierce nationalist backlashes.

The political exhaustion of the Directory paved the way for a young military commander named Napoleon Bonaparte to seize power in a coup d'état in 1799. Although Napoleon preserved many legal and administrative reforms of the revolution, such as the centralized civil code known as the Napoleonic Code, he centralized political authority in his own hands and crowned himself emperor.

Together, the American and French revolutions shattered the divine right of kings and permanently altered the global political landscape. They established the modern concepts of citizenship, written constitutions, and the nation-state, proving that governments derive their legitimacy from the consent of the governed. Yet these dramatic upheavals also left deep contradictions regarding who was included in those universal declarations of liberty. As the shockwaves of French radicalism rippled across the Atlantic, enslaved people in the French Caribbean colony of Saint-Domingue took the rhetoric of freedom to its logical conclusion, launching a revolution that would birth the world's first free Black republic.

## Key ideas
- The American Revolution translated Enlightenment theories of natural rights and the social contract into a successful war for independence against the British Empire, culminating in the drafting and adoption of the United States Constitution in 1787.
- The French Revolution of 1789 dismantled absolute monarchy and feudalism in France, influenced by transatlantic exchanges involving figures like the Marquis de Lafayette and challenged by gender-rights advocates such as Olympe de Gouges.
- The French Revolution progressed through distinct internal phases, moving from a constitutional monarchy to a radical republic, the Reign of Terror, the Thermidorian Reaction, and the Directory, while Revolutionary Wars spread these conflicts across Europe.
- Both revolutions redefined political legitimacy, shifting the source of sovereignty from the divine right of monarchs to the consent of the governed and the rights of citizens.
