# The Constitutional Convention

Imagine fifty-five men gathered in a single room in Philadelphia during the sweltering summer of 1787. They have shuttered the windows to keep their proceedings secret, despite the stifling heat. They are there because the government they created just a few years earlier, the Articles of Confederation, is failing. The national government lacks the power to tax, regulate trade, or effectively manage the debts left over from the war. These men have arrived to fix these problems, but they quickly realize that a simple patch-up will not suffice. They are about to embark on a radical experiment in self-government, but the path forward is paved with intense conflict and uncomfortable compromises.

The most immediate tension arises from a question of power: who should have the most say in the new government? Delegates from large, populous states like Virginia propose a plan where representation is based on population. The more people you have, the more power you get. Smaller states, fearing they will be completely overshadowed by their larger neighbors, counter with a plan that gives every state an equal number of votes, regardless of size. This debate threatens to derail the convention entirely. Eventually, they reach what historians call the Great Compromise. They decide on a two-house legislature. In the House of Representatives, states are represented by population, while in the Senate, every state receives two votes.

If representation was the first major hurdle, the status of enslaved people was the second, and arguably far more consequential. Delegates from the South demand that their enslaved populations be counted toward their total numbers in the House of Representatives, as this would give them significantly more political clout. Northern delegates, many of whom are beginning to see the moral contradiction of slavery in a republic, argue that enslaved people are treated as property by the law and should not be counted as citizens for political representation.

The resulting agreement, known as the Three-Fifths Compromise, dictates that three out of every five enslaved people will be counted for both representation and taxation. This decision hardens the political power of slaveholders, embedding the institution of slavery into the very structure of the Constitution. It is a moment of profound moral failure that casts a long shadow over American history. This count also directly influences the creation of the Electoral College. By basing the number of electors each state receives on its total representation in Congress, the framers ensured that the three-fifths count would also inflate the influence of Southern states in presidential elections.

The delegates also reached a compromise regarding the international slave trade. While many delegates favored an immediate ban, Southern representatives insisted on maintaining the trade. They eventually agreed that the federal government would be prohibited from banning the importation of enslaved people for twenty years, until 1808. This provision, found in Article One, Section Nine, guaranteed the expansion of the slave trade for two decades after the Constitution took effect.

Historians have long debated the true motives of these men, whom we call the framers. In the early twentieth century, scholar Charles Beard famously argued that the framers were primarily motivated by their own economic interests, seeking to create a strong central government that would protect their investments and property. Later historians shifted the focus, emphasizing the ideological commitments of the framers to republicanism, liberty, and the desire to prevent the chaos they saw in the weak Articles of Confederation. Today, most scholars hold a nuanced view: the framers were likely driven by a complex mix of both idealistic vision and practical, self-interested concern for the stability of their new nation.

The document they produced was not a perfect blueprint, but a series of calculated trade-offs. They created a system of checks and balances, ensuring that no single branch of government—the executive, the legislative, or the judicial—could dominate the others. They also debated how much power the federal government should have compared to the states, a tension that would fuel political conflict for centuries to come. As they left the state house, the delegates knew that their work was only half-finished. They had designed a framework, but it was still just a piece of parchment.

Whether this new, more powerful government would actually be accepted by the people remained an open question. The delegates had created a structure that required the consent of the governed, yet many Americans remained deeply suspicious of any central authority that felt too much like the monarchy they had just fought to escape. The document still needed to survive the gauntlet of state conventions, where opponents would demand further protections for individual liberties before they would ever agree to sign on.

## Key ideas

- The Constitutional Convention of 1787 was called to address the failures of the Articles of Confederation, specifically the inability of the national government to manage finances and trade.
- The Great Compromise resolved the conflict over representation by creating a bicameral legislature with a House based on population and a Senate providing equal representation for all states.
- The Three-Fifths Compromise counted three-fifths of the enslaved population for representation and taxation, which also inflated the power of Southern states within the Electoral College.
- A compromise on the slave trade prohibited the federal government from banning the importation of enslaved people until 1808, securing the institution's growth for two decades.
- Historians interpret the framers' motives in various ways, ranging from a focus on personal economic interests to a deeper commitment to republican ideals and political stability.
