# Working with Power Series

Imagine you are trying to calculate the natural logarithm of one point five by hand, without a calculator. Arithmetic can handle addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, but it stumbles when faced with transcendental functions. Calculus offers a brilliant way out of this trap. In our last lesson, we discovered that power series act like infinite polynomials, converging to specific functions inside an interval of convergence. Today, we take a giant step further. We will learn that these infinite series can be differentiated and integrated just like ordinary polynomials, term by term. This remarkable property allows us to take a function whose series we already know, and build entirely new series and new function representations with ease.

To understand why this works, think about how polynomials behave. If you want the derivative of a sum of terms, you simply take the derivative of each term separately and add the results together. Power series share this friendly behavior everywhere inside their radius of convergence. Suppose we have a power series representing a function. We can find the derivative of that function simply by differentiating each term of the series with respect to our variable, such as x, treating the coefficients as constants. Similarly, we can find an antiderivative by integrating each term one by one, and then adding our constant of integration to keep everything correct. A crucial fact to remember is that the radius of convergence remains exactly the same after differentiation or integration, even though the behavior at the endpoints of the interval may sometimes change.

Let us watch this happen with a classic example. We know from our work with geometric series that the function one divided by the quantity one minus x can be represented as the sum of x to the power of n, where n runs from zero to infinity, as long as the absolute value of x is less than one. This simple fraction is a cornerstone for building other series. What happens if we integrate this function with respect to x? On the left side, the integral of one divided by the quantity one minus x gives us the negative of the natural logarithm of the quantity one minus x. On the right side, we integrate each term of our power series term by term. The term x to the power of n integrates to x to the power of n plus one, divided by the quantity n plus one.

Let us trace the terms of this new series explicitly. When n is zero, the term is x. When n is one, we get x squared divided by two. When n is two, we get x cubed divided by three. The pattern continues indefinitely, generating a series of powers divided by their respective integers. To find our constant of integration, we can substitute zero for x into both sides of our equation. The left side becomes the natural logarithm of one, which is zero. The right side becomes zero as well, meaning our constant of integration is simply zero.

By adjusting our signs and variables, we have just discovered the power series representation for the natural logarithm of the quantity one plus x. It equals the sum from n equals one to infinity of negative one raised to the quantity n minus one, multiplied by x to the power of n, divided by n. This is valid for x between negative one and one. Just think about what we achieved. We started with a basic algebraic fraction, applied term by term integration, and successfully created a series for a transcendental logarithm. We can now approximate the logarithm of one point five by plugging zero point five into our new series, adding up a few fractions, and watching the partial sums close in on the true value.

Differentiating power series is equally powerful. If we take our original geometric series and differentiate it term by term with respect to x, we obtain a series for the function one divided by the square of the quantity one minus x. Each term is differentiated with respect to x, shifting the powers and multiplying by the previous exponents. This technique opens up a vast catalog of functions, allowing engineers and physicists to replace complicated expressions with manageable polynomial approximations. As we look ahead to our next lesson, we will build directly on these tools to construct general Taylor and Maclaurin series for any smooth function whatsoever.

## Key ideas
- Power series can be differentiated and integrated term by term within their radius of convergence, just like ordinary finite polynomials.
- The radius of convergence remains unchanged after term by term differentiation or integration, although convergence behavior at the endpoints of the interval may change.
- Integrating a known power series, such as the geometric series representation, provides a direct method for finding series representations for new functions like logarithms.
- Differentiating a power series allows us to find series for rational functions like one divided by the square of the quantity one minus x.
- Substituting specific numbers into these newly constructed series enables accurate numerical approximations for complex transcendental functions.
