# Arithmetic Sequences and Series

Imagine stacking logs for a winter fire so that the bottom row has twenty logs, the next row up has eighteen, the next has sixteen, and each subsequent row drops by the exact same number of logs until you reach a top row with just two. That predictable, step-by-step change is the heartbeat of an arithmetic sequence. Building on our previous look at general number lists and summation notation, today we zoom in on sequences where the difference between any term and its immediate predecessor is always constant.

We call this constant amount the common difference. If you start at five and add three each time, you get eight, then eleven, then fourteen. The common difference here is positive three. If you start at fifty and subtract four each time, you get forty-six, then forty-two, and the common difference is negative four. Notice that a sequence does not care whether it climbs or descends; as long as the gap between consecutive entries never wavers, it is arithmetic.

To find any term in an arithmetic sequence without having to list out every single intermediate step, we can build a direct formula. Let us call the first term u and the constant common difference d. The first term is just u. The second term is u plus d. The third term is u plus two times d. Do you see the rhythm? For any position n, the term is equal to the starting term u plus the quantity n minus one times the common difference d. Let us test this on our earlier list of five, eight, eleven, fourteen. The starting term u is five, and d is three. If we want the fourth term, we take five plus the quantity four minus one times three, which is five plus three times three, giving fourteen. It matches our list precisely.

Often, mathematicians want to know the sum of a string of these numbers, which is called an arithmetic series. Imagine you are asked to add up all the whole numbers from one to one hundred. A young Carl Friedrich Gauss allegedly solved this in seconds by pairing the numbers from opposite ends. One plus one hundred is one hundred one. Two plus ninety-nine is one hundred one. Three plus ninety-eight is one hundred one. Every single pair sums to one hundred one, and out of one hundred numbers, you have fifty such pairs. Multiplying fifty by one hundred one gives five thousand fifty.

We can turn this brilliant pairing insight into a general rule for any arithmetic series. The sum of a finite arithmetic sequence is found by taking the number of terms, multiplying it by the sum of the very first term and the very last term, and dividing that entire product by two. In other words, you average the endpoints and multiply by how many terms you have. Let us practice this with our earlier sequence of five, eight, eleven, fourteen. Suppose we want to add these four terms together. The number of terms is four. The first term is five, and the last term is fourteen. Five plus fourteen is nineteen. Multiply four by nineteen to get seventy-six, and divide by two to get thirty-eight. Let us check by adding them manually: five plus eight is thirteen, plus eleven is twenty-four, plus fourteen is thirty-eight. The formula works seamlessly, saving enormous effort when a series stretches to hundreds of terms.

Understanding how these linear patterns grow and accumulate gives us a powerful lens for modeling steady, linear changes over time. Yet not all patterns progress by adding a constant amount; some grow by multiplying. In our next lesson, we will explore geometric sequences and series, where each step multiplies by a constant ratio rather than adding a fixed difference.

## Key ideas
- An arithmetic sequence is a list of numbers where the difference between any term and its predecessor is a constant value called the common difference.
- Any term in an arithmetic sequence can be found directly by taking the first term and adding the quantity n minus one times the common difference.
- The sum of a finite arithmetic series is calculated by multiplying the number of terms by the sum of the first and last terms, then dividing by two.
- The pairing method demonstrates that adding the outer extremes of a symmetric sequence gives the same total as adding any inner pairs equidistant from the center.
