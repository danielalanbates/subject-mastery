# Scarcity and the Economic Way of Thinking

Imagine you are standing in front of your favorite food truck on a sunny afternoon. You have twenty dollars in your pocket and a very hungry stomach. You look at the menu and realize you want the gourmet burger, the truffle fries, and the large salted caramel milkshake. However, the total cost of those three items comes to twenty-five dollars. You are forced to make a choice. You might drop the milkshake, or perhaps you choose a smaller burger to afford the fries. This tiny moment of frustration is a perfect introduction to the entire field of economics.

Many people think economics is strictly the study of the stock market, banking, or how to get rich. While those topics are part of the field, they are not its heart. At its core, economics is the study of how individuals, businesses, and societies make choices when faced with scarcity. Scarcity is the fundamental reality that human wants are essentially infinite, but the resources available to satisfy those wants are strictly limited. These resources include things like time, money, land, and raw materials. Because we cannot have everything we want, we must choose.

When we make a choice, we inevitably face a trade-off. This leads to the concept of opportunity cost. Simply put, the opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the next best alternative that you give up. If you spend your twenty dollars on the burger and fries, the opportunity cost is the milkshake you could have enjoyed instead. Economics helps us analyze these trade-offs at every level of society.

To understand these choices, economists start with the assumption of rationality. This means we assume that individuals are purposeful. They use the information available to them to make decisions that will best achieve their own goals. Because people are rational, they respond to incentives. An incentive is anything that motivates a person to act, such as a reward or a penalty. For example, if the price of gasoline rises, the incentive to carpool or use public transit increases. Most of economic theory is built on the idea that if you change the incentives, you will change the way people behave.

Economists use specific tools to study this behavior. The first is the scientific model. In the real world, the economy is incredibly complex. It involves billions of people making trillions of decisions every day. To make sense of this, economists build models. A model is a simplified representation of reality.

Think of an economic model like a subway map. A subway map does not show every tree, building, or sidewalk in the city. If it did, it would be too cluttered to use. Instead, it simplifies the city down to lines and dots. This helps you understand how to get from one station to another. Economists use graphs and equations in the same way. They strip away unnecessary details to reveal how variables like price and quantity interact.

When using these models, economists rely on the assumption of ceteris paribus. This is a Latin phrase meaning "all other things being equal." It allows us to look at the relationship between two specific variables while assuming that everything else in the world stays the same. For instance, if we want to study how a change in the price of coffee affects sales, we assume that the price of tea and the income of consumers do not change at the same time.

Another pillar of the economic way of thinking is marginal thinking. Most decisions in life are not all or nothing. You rarely choose between eating zero calories a day or ten thousand calories. Instead, you decide whether to eat one more slice of pizza. In economics, the word "marginal" means "additional" or "one more."

Suppose you are studying for an exam. You have already studied for three hours. You are tired, but you are considering staying up for a fourth hour. To make a rational decision, you compare the marginal benefit to the marginal cost. The marginal benefit might be an extra five points on your test. The marginal cost is the loss of one hour of sleep. If the benefit of those five points is greater than the cost of being tired, a rational person will stay up. By focusing on the margin, we can understand why people change their behavior in response to small shifts in their environment.

Finally, it is important to distinguish between the two types of claims economists make. These are positive and normative statements. A positive claim is a statement about how the world actually works. It is an assertion of fact that can be tested or proven false with data. For example, if an economist says that a ten percent increase in the minimum wage will lead to a two percent decrease in teen employment, they are making a positive statement. We can look at employment data to see if it is true.

A normative statement is a statement about how the world should be. It is based on values, ethics, and opinions. If an economist says the government should raise the minimum wage to help the poor, they have moved into normative territory. They are making a value judgment. While economists try to remain objective by focusing on positive analysis, their work often informs these normative debates in politics and social policy.

## Key ideas

- Scarcity is the fundamental economic problem where unlimited human wants collide with limited resources, forcing individuals and societies to make choices.
- The opportunity cost of any decision is the value of the next best alternative that must be sacrificed.
- Economists assume that individuals are rational and respond to incentives, meaning they purposefully choose options that provide the greatest personal benefit.
- Models are simplified versions of reality that use the ceteris paribus assumption to isolate the relationship between specific variables by holding all other factors constant.
- Marginal analysis involves comparing the additional benefits of an action against the additional costs to determine the most efficient choice.
- Positive economics describes the world as it is through testable facts, while normative economics describes how the world should be based on value judgments.
