# Rational Functions and Asymptotes

Imagine an engineered boat hull designed to slice through water more and more efficiently the faster it travels, yet no matter how much engine power you apply, the top speed creeps closer to a certain limit without ever quite reaching it. Mathematics captures this kind of boundary behavior through rational functions, which are fractions where both the numerator and the denominator are polynomial functions of an input variable. Building on our work with polynomial zeros, we now examine what happens when one polynomial divides another, creating a landscape where function values can shatter into separate pieces and soar toward invisible walls.

To understand a rational function, we must first look at its domain, which is the set of all allowable input numbers. Because division by zero is undefined, any input number that makes the denominator polynomial equal to zero must be excluded from the domain of the function. For example, consider the function f of x equals one divided by the quantity x minus three. Here, the denominator is x minus three, which equals zero when x equals three. Therefore, three is excluded from the domain, leaving every other real number. When we try to evaluate the function very close to three, something dramatic happens to the output values.

If you substitute numbers increasingly close to three from the right side, such as three point one or three point zero one, the denominator becomes a tiny positive number, driving the fraction to enormous positive values. If you approach from the left side with numbers like two point nine or two point nine nine, the denominator becomes a tiny negative number, driving the fraction down toward negative infinity. This invisible vertical boundary where the function outputs shoot upward or downward is called a vertical asymptote. The equation of this vertical line is x equals three, mirroring the root of the denominator polynomial.

Sometimes, an input value makes both the numerator and the denominator equal to zero at the exact same time. When this happens, it does not automatically create a vertical asymptote. Instead, if that common factor can be canceled algebraically from both parts of the fraction, the graph exhibits a tiny removable discontinuity, which we call a hole. Think of a continuous curve with a single, infinitely small missing point punched right out of the middle. For instance, in the function g of x equals the quantity x squared minus four divided by the quantity x minus two, the numerator factors into the quantity x minus two times the quantity x plus two. Because x minus two appears in both numerator and denominator, we can cancel it for all inputs except x equals two, leaving the line g of x equals x plus two with a literal hole at the coordinate where x equals two.

Beyond vertical barriers, rational functions also possess long-term horizontal or slant boundaries that describe how the function behaves as the input variable grows infinitely large in the positive or negative direction. To find these horizontal or slant asymptotes, we compare the degree of the numerator polynomial with the degree of the denominator polynomial. The degree is simply the highest exponent of the variable in that polynomial expression.

If the degree of the denominator is larger than the degree of the numerator, the bottom grows much faster than the top as the input grows, pulling the fraction toward zero. This means the horizontal line y equals zero acts as a horizontal asymptote. If the numerator and the denominator share the exact same degree, the horizontal asymptote is found by dividing the leading coefficient of the numerator by the leading coefficient of the denominator. For example, if the function is the quantity two x squared over the quantity three x squared plus five, both polynomials have degree two, and the function values flatten out horizontally at the value two-thirds as x moves toward positive or negative infinity.

When the numerator degree is exactly one higher than the denominator degree, the function settles into a slanted linear path rather than a flat horizontal line. We find this slant asymptote by using polynomial long division to divide the numerator by the denominator, ignoring the trailing remainder as the input grows massive. Let us trace this with actual numbers. Suppose we divide the numerator polynomial x squared plus five x plus six by the denominator polynomial x plus one. Using polynomial long division, we divide x squared by x, giving a quotient term of x. Multiplying x by the quantity x plus one gives x squared plus x. Subtracting this from our original numerator leaves four x plus six. Next, we divide four x by x, giving four. Multiplying four by the quantity x plus one gives four x plus four. Subtracting this leaves a remainder of two. So the result of our division is the quotient polynomial x plus four, plus the remainder two over the quantity x plus one. As x grows infinitely large, the remainder fraction two over the quantity x plus one approaches zero, leaving the line y equals x plus four as our slant asymptote.

When the degree of the numerator exceeds the degree of the denominator by more than one, neither a horizontal nor a slant asymptote exists. Instead, the end behavior follows the quotient polynomial obtained from long division, meaning the function values grow faster than any linear path as the input increases. Describing these behaviors in words allows us to understand the entire life cycle of the curve, from its sudden spikes near vertical walls to its steady drift along horizontal or slanted paths.

## Key ideas
- A rational function is a ratio of two polynomials, and its domain excludes any input values that make the denominator equal to zero.
- Vertical asymptotes occur at domain restrictions where the simplified denominator equals zero, causing the function outputs to shoot toward positive or negative infinity.
- Common factors in both the numerator and denominator create removable holes in the function rather than vertical asymptotes.
- Comparing the degrees of the numerator and denominator polynomials determines whether the function has a horizontal asymptote, a slant asymptote, or neither.
