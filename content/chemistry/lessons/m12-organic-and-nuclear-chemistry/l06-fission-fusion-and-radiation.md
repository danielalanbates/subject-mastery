# Fission, Fusion and Radiation

Imagine holding a small piece of uranium fuel the size of a marble. That tiny amount of matter contains enough potential energy to power an entire home for a year. This is not science fiction; it is the reality of nuclear energy. In our previous lesson, we explored how unstable nuclei decay over time, a process governed by the steady rhythm of half-lives. Today, we move from that slow, natural decay to the high-energy processes that humans have harnessed to generate electricity and that power the very stars themselves. We are looking at the massive energy changes that occur when we force atomic nuclei to split apart or fuse together.

Nuclear fission is the process where a heavy, unstable nucleus, such as uranium two thirty-five, is struck by a neutron and splits into two smaller, more stable nuclei. This split releases a tremendous amount of energy and several additional neutrons. These neutrons then strike other uranium nuclei, creating a self-sustaining chain reaction. To maintain a steady output in a power plant, we must achieve a critical mass, which is the minimum amount of fuel needed to keep the reaction going. We use moderators, such as water or graphite, to slow down the fast-moving neutrons, because slower neutrons are more likely to cause further fission. We also insert control rods made of materials like boron or cadmium to absorb excess neutrons and regulate the rate of the reaction.

The energy released in these reactions occurs because the total mass of the products is slightly less than the mass of the original nucleus and the incident neutron. This missing mass is converted into energy. This happens because nuclei of intermediate mass, like iron, are the most stable. They have the highest binding energy per nucleon, which is the energy holding the protons and neutrons together. When we split a very heavy nucleus, the resulting fragments are closer to this stable middle ground, releasing the excess binding energy.

While fission breaks atoms apart, nuclear fusion does the opposite. Fusion occurs when two light nuclei, typically isotopes of hydrogen, are forced together under extreme heat and pressure to form a single, heavier nucleus, like helium. This is the same process that fuels the sun. Fusion releases even more energy per gram of fuel than fission does, as it moves from light elements toward that stable middle ground of the binding energy curve. The challenge for scientists is that fusion requires conditions similar to the core of a star, making it incredibly difficult to contain here on Earth.

Beyond power generation, the radiation released by these nuclear processes has profound effects on the world. Ionizing radiation, which includes alpha particles, beta particles, and gamma rays, carries enough energy to strip electrons from atoms and break chemical bonds in molecules, including deoxyribonucleic acid, or DNA. To measure the impact of this radiation on human health, we use specific units. The absorbed dose measures the energy deposited in tissue, while the equivalent dose, measured in sieverts or rems, accounts for the biological effectiveness of the radiation type. High doses can lead to cell death or mutations, which is why we use shielding and strict safety protocols.

However, this ability to damage cells is a powerful tool in medicine. In cancer treatment, doctors use focused beams of radiation to damage the DNA of tumor cells, preventing them from dividing. We also use radioactive tracers in diagnostic imaging. By introducing a safe amount of a radioactive isotope into a patient, medical professionals can track metabolic processes in real time. It is a striking example of how a force that can be destructive in high doses becomes life-saving when handled with precision.

As we conclude our study of chemistry, remember that these nuclear processes remind us that matter and energy are linked. From the bonding of atoms to the reactions within the nucleus, chemistry provides the language we use to decode the physical world. You have now completed your journey through the foundations of chemistry, moving from the smallest subatomic particles to the energetic transformations that drive our universe.

## Key ideas

- Nuclear fission releases energy by splitting heavy nuclei into smaller, more stable fragments, while nuclear fusion releases energy by combining light nuclei into heavier ones.
- The energy produced in nuclear reactions comes from the conversion of a small amount of mass into energy, driven by the fact that intermediate-mass nuclei have the highest binding energy per nucleon.
- Nuclear reactors use moderators to slow neutrons and control rods to absorb them, ensuring a steady, self-sustaining chain reaction.
- Ionizing radiation is quantified by units like the sievert to assess biological risk, as this energy can break chemical bonds and damage DNA.
- While high doses of radiation are hazardous to living tissue, controlled applications of radiation are essential for medical diagnostics and cancer therapy.
