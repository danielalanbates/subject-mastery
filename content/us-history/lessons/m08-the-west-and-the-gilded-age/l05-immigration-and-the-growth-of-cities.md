# Immigration and the Growth of Cities

Imagine standing on the deck of a steamship in the late 1880s, watching the gray silhouette of the Statue of Liberty emerge through the New York harbor fog. For millions of people, that sight represented the culmination of a grueling journey across the Atlantic Ocean. These travelers were the vanguard of what historians call the new immigration. While earlier waves of arrivals came primarily from northern and western Europe, such as Great Britain, Ireland, and Germany, this new surge brought millions from the southern and eastern reaches of the continent. They arrived from Italy, Greece, Poland, and the Russian Empire, driven by a volatile mix of economic hardship, religious persecution, and the promise of industrial work.

These newcomers entered a nation in the midst of a profound transformation. As we saw in our previous look at industrial labor, the American economy was shifting away from the farm and toward the factory. This economic engine required a massive, concentrated workforce, and the city became the natural destination. Between 1870 and 1900, the number of Americans living in cities tripled. New York, Chicago, and Philadelphia exploded in size, stretching upward with the invention of the steel-frame skyscraper and outward through the development of electric streetcars and elevated trains.

Life in these fast-growing cities was a study in stark contrasts. For many immigrants, the reality of urban life was defined by the tenement. These were narrow walk-up apartment buildings, often five or six stories tall and packed with families in small, windowless rooms. Sanitation was primitive, disease spread quickly through crowded hallways, and the air was thick with the soot of nearby factories. Yet, despite these hardships, the city offered a vibrant, collective life. Immigrants clustered in ethnic neighborhoods, creating enclaves where they could speak their native languages, shop in familiar markets, and maintain the cultural traditions they brought from home. These neighborhoods became the bedrock of immigrant survival and community identity.

As cities ballooned, the traditional structures of local government often struggled to keep pace. Into this vacuum stepped the political machine. A machine was an informal, highly organized political group that controlled a city’s government by trading favors for votes. The leader of the machine, known as the ward boss, acted as a broker. He might provide a family with coal in the winter, find a man a job on a city construction project, or help an immigrant navigate the legal system to secure citizenship. In return, the boss expected the grateful immigrant to vote for the machine’s preferred candidates. While critics rightly condemned these machines for endemic corruption and the embezzlement of public funds, they provided a vital, albeit deeply flawed, social safety net that the official government had yet to establish.

Not everyone in America welcomed this changing landscape. A powerful nativist reaction bubbled up among those who feared that the new arrivals threatened the nation’s social and cultural fabric. Nativism is the policy or practice of protecting the interests of native-born citizens against those of immigrants. Many nativists argued that these new arrivals were unassimilable, pointing to their different languages, religions, and customs as proof that they could never become true Americans. They feared that the influx of cheap labor would drive down wages for native-born workers and that foreign ideologies, like socialism or anarchism, would undermine American democracy.

This prejudice occasionally manifested in organized movements. The Immigration Restriction League, for example, campaigned for literacy tests to keep out those they deemed undesirable. In 1882, the federal government took a decisive step in this direction by passing the Chinese Exclusion Act, which effectively banned the immigration of Chinese laborers. This was a turning point, marking the first time the United States had restricted immigration based specifically on national origin or race. It signaled a growing anxiety about the nation’s identity, an anxiety that would continue to shape American law and social policy for decades to come.

The city was a crucible where the promise of industrial capitalism met the human cost of rapid expansion. It was a place of immense opportunity and crushing poverty, of democratic participation through machines and exclusionary prejudice through nativism. As the population shifted and the urban environment grew more complex, the political system would soon face new pressures from disgruntled farmers and restless citizens who felt left behind by this rapid modernization. These mounting tensions would eventually push the nation toward a new era of populist agitation and institutional reform.

## Key ideas

- The new immigration brought millions of people from southern and eastern Europe to American cities, seeking work in an expanding industrial economy.
- Urban life was characterized by rapid growth, the rise of cramped tenement housing, and the development of distinct ethnic neighborhoods that provided community support.
- Political machines gained power by offering essential social services and jobs to immigrants in exchange for their political loyalty and votes.
- Nativism grew alongside the immigrant population, leading to social friction and eventually to federal restrictions on immigration, such as the Chinese Exclusion Act.
