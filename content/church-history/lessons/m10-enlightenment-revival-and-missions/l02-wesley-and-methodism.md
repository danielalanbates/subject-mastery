# Wesley and Methodism

In the spring of 1738, an Anglican priest named John Wesley sat in a small meeting room on Aldersgate Street in London. He was a man of intense discipline and profound frustration. Despite years of rigorous study at Oxford and a sincere, if unsuccessful, missionary trip to the American colonies, Wesley felt his faith lacked power. He was a man of the head, not the heart. On that May evening, as he listened to a reading of Martin Luther’s preface to the Epistle to the Romans, something shifted. Wesley later wrote in his journal that he felt his heart strangely warmed. He felt he did trust in Christ, and Christ alone, for his salvation. This moment of awakening would soon ignite a movement that reshaped the landscape of global Christianity.

While the previous lesson explored how the Enlightenment brought a new, often skeptical, focus on human reason, Wesley’s experience pointed toward a different kind of authority: the direct, transformative power of the Holy Spirit. He did not reject reason, but he insisted that true religion was a matter of the affections. It was not merely an intellectual assent to doctrine, but a personal encounter with the grace of God. This conviction became the heartbeat of the Methodist movement.

Methodism began as a renewal movement within the Church of England, not as a separate denomination. The name itself was originally a mocking term used by students at Oxford to describe Wesley and his brother Charles, who followed a strict, methodical approach to prayer, fasting, and Bible study. John Wesley took this criticism and turned it into a badge of honor. He believed that the church had grown cold and formal, and that it needed a return to the primitive piety of the early Christians. To achieve this, he organized believers into small groups known as classes and bands. These were settings where members met weekly to confess their sins, pray for one another, and hold each other accountable for their spiritual growth.

One of the most radical aspects of Wesley’s ministry was his decision to preach outside the walls of the church. When many Anglican pulpits were closed to him due to his unconventional methods, he took to the fields and the town squares. He preached to miners, factory workers, and the poor—people who were largely ignored by the established religious institutions of the eighteenth century. His message was simple: God’s grace is available to everyone, and it is capable of transforming the sinner into a saint.

This brings us to one of Wesley’s most distinctive and often misunderstood teachings: the concept of Christian perfection. Wesley did not mean that a person could become sinless or beyond the reach of temptation. Rather, he taught that a believer could reach a state of perfect love, where the heart is entirely filled with the love of God and neighbor. For Wesley, this was not a static destination but a dynamic process of growing in holiness. He believed that the goal of the Christian life was to be so fully surrendered to God that one’s motives and actions were consistently guided by divine love.

This focus on internal transformation naturally flowed into what Wesley called social holiness. In his preface to a collection of hymns, he wrote that the gospel of Christ knows of no religion but social, no holiness but social holiness. He believed that the gospel could not be contained within the individual’s private life. It had to manifest in the public sphere. Under his influence, Methodists became pioneers in social reform. They helped establish schools for the poor, visited those in prison, provided medical care, and became some of the most vocal opponents of the slave trade. For Wesley, to love God was to love one’s neighbor, and that love had to be practical, tangible, and courageous.

By the time of his death in 1791, John Wesley had traveled hundreds of thousands of miles on horseback, preaching thousands of sermons and organizing a vast network of societies. He had effectively created an international movement that bridged the gap between the intellectual rigor of the Enlightenment and the emotional depth of a personal, vital faith. His emphasis on small-group accountability and the practical application of faith provided a blueprint for future revival movements. As the eighteenth century drew to a close, this fervor for personal holiness and social reform would cross the Atlantic, fueling a series of massive, transformative revivals that would change the American religious landscape forever.

## Key ideas
- John Wesley’s spiritual awakening at Aldersgate redirected his ministry from a formal, duty-based religion to a heart-centered faith defined by a personal experience of God's grace.
- Methodism began as a renewal movement within the Church of England, emphasizing small group accountability and the methodical practice of prayer, fasting, and scripture study.
- Wesley’s theology of Christian perfection taught that believers could and should strive to be so filled with the love of God that their lives were transformed in both intent and action.
- The concept of social holiness insisted that personal piety must inevitably lead to public engagement, prompting early Methodists to become leaders in social reform, education, and the fight against slavery.
