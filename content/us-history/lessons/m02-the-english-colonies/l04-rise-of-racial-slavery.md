# The Rise of Racial Slavery

Imagine standing on the creaking deck of a wooden ship in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean during the late seventeenth century. Below the waterline, in dark and cramped holds, human beings were chained together, separated from their homes and families. This was the Middle Passage, the brutal middle leg of a triangular trade route that moved manufactured goods, raw materials, and human beings between Europe, Africa, and the Americas. The voyage could last weeks or even months. It was a journey of unimaginable suffering and sensory deprivation. Roughly fifteen percent of the captives died during the crossing due to rampant disease, severe malnutrition, and the sheer psychological terror of their confinement.

In our previous lesson, we examined the development of the southern colonies, where the intense agricultural demands for tobacco, and later rice and indigo, created a massive, persistent need for labor. Initially, many plantation owners relied on indentured servants from England. These were people who signed legal contracts agreeing to work for a fixed number of years in exchange for their passage to the New World. Once their time was up, they were theoretically free to own land and participate in colonial society. However, this system became increasingly unstable over time. As life expectancy in the colonies improved, servants began surviving their terms in greater numbers. This created a growing, restless class of landless, frustrated men who expected economic opportunity that the colonial elite were unwilling to provide.

A major turning point occurred in 1676 with Bacon’s Rebellion in Virginia. This uprising, led by a wealthy planter named Nathaniel Bacon, brought together landless white servants and enslaved people against the colonial government. While the rebellion was ultimately crushed, it left a lasting mark on the colonial psyche. Fearing such alliances between the poor and the enslaved, the colonial elite moved to replace an unstable, temporary workforce with a permanent, enslaved one. This shift was supported by a series of new, restrictive laws. In sixteen sixty-seven, the Virginia assembly declared that baptism did not change the legal status of an enslaved person. This ensured that conversion to Christianity could not be used as a legal argument to claim freedom. Even earlier, in 1662, they established that the status of a child followed the condition of the mother. This meant that if a mother was enslaved, her children were born into slavery, ensuring that the institution became a perpetual, inherited condition.

The economic landscape also shifted in favor of this new system. In sixteen ninety-eight, the English Parliament ended the Royal African Company’s monopoly on the slave trade. This opened the market to private merchants, which significantly increased the number of enslaved Africans brought to the British colonies. By seventeen hundred, enslaved Africans constituted a majority of the labor force in the Chesapeake colonies. To manage this growing population and enforce total control, Virginia passed a comprehensive slave code in 1705. This landmark legislation consolidated earlier, scattered laws and explicitly defined enslaved people as real property. It stripped them of their basic legal rights and placed them under the absolute, unchecked authority of their masters.

This transition was deeply rooted in the deliberate construction of race. To justify lifetime, hereditary enslavement, colonial leaders created a legal and social wall between white and Black people. By defining slavery through the lens of skin color, the colonial elite created a system where white indentured servants were offered the promise of racial solidarity and social advancement. Meanwhile, Black people were relegated to the status of permanent property. This was a calculated move to prevent the lower classes from uniting against the wealthy.

Enslaved people were never passive victims in this process. They resisted in every way possible, from working slowly and breaking tools to running away or organizing armed revolts. The fear of this resistance led to even stricter slave codes, which gave masters nearly total power over the lives of those they held in bondage. By the dawn of the eighteenth century, the foundation of a society built on the brutal logic of hereditary racial slavery was firmly set. This transition fundamentally shaped the economic development of the southern colonies and created a rigid social divide that would define American life for centuries to come. The legacy of these laws and the social hierarchies they enforced continued to echo long after the colonial period ended.

## Key ideas

- The Middle Passage was the horrific, forced oceanic transit of millions of Africans, with about fifteen percent of captives dying during the voyage.
- Bacon’s Rebellion in 1676 frightened the colonial elite, accelerating their shift away from indentured servitude toward a permanent, enslaved labor force.
- Colonial assemblies reinforced lifelong slavery through laws like the sixteen sixty-seven act regarding baptism and the seventeen hundred and five Virginia slave code, which defined enslaved people as real property.
- The end of the Royal African Company’s monopoly in 1698 expanded the slave trade and helped ensure that by 1700, enslaved Africans formed the majority of the labor force in the Chesapeake.
- By codifying racial slavery, colonial leaders created a rigid social hierarchy that offered white laborers a sense of racial privilege while systematically stripping Black people of their humanity.
