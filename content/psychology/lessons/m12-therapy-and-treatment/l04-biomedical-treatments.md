# Biomedical Treatments

Imagine you are looking at a complex piece of machinery, like a high-end watch. If the gears are misaligned, you do not just talk to the watch to fix it; you adjust the internal components. In our last lesson, we explored how talking through our thoughts and behaviors can reshape our mental state. But psychology also recognizes that the mind is fundamentally rooted in the biology of the brain. When the gears of our neurochemistry or neural circuitry are out of balance, biomedical treatments offer a way to intervene directly at the physical source.

Psychiatric medications are the most common form of biomedical intervention. These drugs work by altering the levels of neurotransmitters, the chemical messengers that allow neurons to communicate. For example, antidepressants are frequently prescribed for major depressive disorder. Randomized controlled trials consistently show that these medications provide a modest, statistically significant effect size over a placebo. However, there is a notable delay in their action, as patients often do not feel the benefits for several weeks. These drugs can also cause side effects like nausea or insomnia.

For bipolar disorder, clinicians often prescribe mood stabilizers, such as lithium or certain anticonvulsants. These medications are essential for managing the extreme shifts between manic and depressive episodes. Meanwhile, antipsychotic medications are used primarily to treat schizophrenia. They typically function by blocking dopamine receptors in the brain. While effective at reducing hallucinations, long-term use requires careful monitoring because these drugs carry metabolic risks, including significant weight gain and an increased risk of diabetes.

When medication does not provide enough relief, particularly in cases of severe, treatment-resistant depression, clinicians may turn to brain stimulation. The most well-known, and historically misunderstood, version of this is electroconvulsive therapy, or ECT. In the early days of psychiatry, ECT was sometimes used in ways that were inhumane and poorly regulated. Today, it is a highly controlled medical procedure. A patient is placed under general anesthesia and given muscle relaxants. Then, a brief, controlled electrical current is passed through the brain to induce a therapeutic seizure. Meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials support its efficacy for severe depression. While it is highly effective, a common side effect is temporary short-term memory loss.

Newer technologies are also emerging that focus on more localized areas of the brain. Transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS, is one such method. As of the mid-twenty-twenties, TMS is approved by the Food and Drug Administration for treatment-resistant depression based on multiple sham-controlled studies. Unlike ECT, it does not require anesthesia or induce a seizure. Instead, a magnetic coil is placed against the scalp, delivering focused magnetic pulses to specific regions, such as the prefrontal cortex, which sits at the very front of the brain and is associated with mood regulation. A typical course of TMS involves daily sessions over several weeks. Because it is non-invasive and targets specific areas, it generally has fewer systemic side effects than medication.

It is important to remember that biomedical treatments are rarely used in a vacuum. The most effective approach is often an integrative one, combining the physical stabilization provided by medication or stimulation with the psychological tools learned in therapy. If a medication can lift the fog of a severe depressive episode, the patient is in a much better position to engage with the cognitive work of identifying and challenging negative thought patterns. We treat the biology to support the mind, and we treat the mind to sustain the biological health of the brain.

## Key ideas

- Biomedical treatments address mental health by targeting the physical and chemical processes of the brain.
- Psychiatric medications, including antidepressants, mood stabilizers, and antipsychotics, are evidence-based interventions that require careful management of delayed onset and potential metabolic or physical side effects.
- Electroconvulsive therapy is a highly regulated, evidence-based procedure for severe conditions, while transcranial magnetic stimulation offers a localized, non-invasive alternative for treatment-resistant depression.
- The most effective mental health care often integrates biomedical interventions with psychotherapy to address both the biological and psychological aspects of a disorder.
