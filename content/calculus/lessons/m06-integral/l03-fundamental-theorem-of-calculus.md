# The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Imagine you are tracking a car speeding down a long, straight highway. Your speedometer tells you your exact velocity at every single second, giving you a continuous curve of speed over time. If you want to know the total distance you have traveled, you could picture adding up countless tiny slices of time multiplied by your speed during each instant, which is the exact problem of the definite integral we explored last time. But here is the remarkable puzzle that kept mathematicians awake at night for centuries. Finding that total accumulated distance is precisely the reverse operation of reading your speedometer in the first place. Differentiation, which breaks motion down into instantaneous rates of change, and integration, which stitches those rates back together into total accumulation, are exact inverse twins.

This profound realization is called the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. To understand its dual nature, we can look at it in two distinct pieces. The first part tells us that if you take a continuous function and define a new function by taking its definite integral from a fixed starting point up to a variable endpoint, the rate of change of that new accumulated area function is simply the original function itself. To write this precisely, we use a dummy variable of integration distinct from the upper limit, taking the integral from a fixed starting point to the variable upper limit of f of t, d t. The derivative of this accumulated area with respect to the upper limit brings you right back to the original function evaluated at that upper limit. In other words, if you accumulate area under a curve, differentiating that accumulation brings you right back to the height of the curve.

The second part of the theorem gives us the practical superpower we use to evaluate definite integrals every day without resorting to infinite Riemann sums. It states that to find the integral of a function over a closed interval, you simply need to find any antiderivative of that function, evaluate it at the upper endpoint, and subtract its value at the lower endpoint. Think about what this means in practice. Evaluating a limit of a sum involving millions of tiny rectangles is arduous. But finding an antiderivative lets you evaluate the entire accumulation in a single stroke.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Suppose we want to find the definite integral from the lower limit of one to the upper limit of three of the function two times x, d x. First, we find an antiderivative for two times x, which is x squared, because the derivative of x squared with respect to x is two times x. According to the second part of the fundamental theorem, we evaluate this antiderivative at our upper limit of three, giving three squared, which is nine. Then we evaluate it at our lower limit of one, giving one squared, which is one. Subtracting the lower value from the upper value gives nine minus one, which equals eight. We can check our work by differentiating our antiderivative, x squared, which gives back our original integrand, two times x.

The historical discovery of this grand synthesis is one of the most dramatic tales in the history of science. In the seventeenth century, Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently unlocked the connection between slopes and areas. Newton arrived at the concept first, driven by his physical studies of motion and fluxions, viewing derivatives and integrals through the lens of moving particles, and he introduced a dot notation placed above variables to denote fluxions. Leibniz approached the problem through geometry and philosophy, seeking a universal calculus of numbers, and he introduced the notation we still use today, including the elongated letter s for the integral sign and the d x to represent an infinitesimal width.

Unfortunately, instead of celebrating a shared triumph, the two geniuses and their respective followers engaged in a bitter, lifelong priority dispute over who invented calculus first. The controversy poisoned academic relations between British and continental European mathematicians for generations. Yet history has judged that both men deserved immense credit, because while earlier scholars caught glimpses of the truth, Newton and Leibniz forged the scattered geometric tricks of the past into a systematic, universal language. They handed us a tool that bridges the infinitely small and the infinitely large.

## Key ideas
- The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus reveals that differentiation and integration are inverse operations that undo one another.
- The first part of the theorem proves that the derivative of an accumulated area function, using a dummy variable of integration distinct from the upper limit, returns the original integrand.
- The second part of the theorem provides the evaluation method, stating that a definite integral equals the difference between an antiderivative evaluated at the upper and lower limits.
- Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently discovered the theorem in the seventeenth century, introducing fluxion dot notation and integral notation respectively, though their achievement was marred by a bitter priority dispute.
