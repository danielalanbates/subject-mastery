# Measuring Intelligence

Imagine you are sitting in a quiet office, facing a series of abstract puzzles, pattern recognition tasks, and vocabulary questions. You are taking a standardized test designed to produce a single number representing your cognitive ability. But how did we get to the point where we reduce human potential to a score? In our last lesson, we discussed theories like Charles Spearman’s general intelligence factor and Howard Gardner’s multiple intelligences. Today, we turn to the practical side of those theories: the tools we use to measure them and the complex history behind those measurements.

To measure intelligence, psychologists build tests that must meet three rigorous criteria. First is standardization, which means giving the test to a large, representative sample of people to establish a baseline. When you take such a test, your score is compared to this group. Second is reliability, which ensures that the test produces consistent results. Psychologists measure this through test-retest reliability, often seeing correlations of about point nine, a strong link indicating that scores remain stable over short periods. Third is validity, which asks if the test actually measures what it claims to measure. Evidence for validity is often found through criterion validity, which shows a moderate correlation, typically around point five, between test scores and real-world outcomes like academic grades or job performance.

When we talk about intelligence scores, we refer to the deviation Intelligence Quotient. Modern tests are designed so that the average score is one hundred, with a standard deviation of fifteen. This means the scores follow a bell-shaped curve, or normal distribution. About sixty-eight percent of the population scores between eighty-five and one hundred fifteen. This distribution allows us to use scores for practical purposes. For example, scores below seventy to seventy-five are often used as one criterion for diagnosing intellectual disabilities, while scores above one hundred thirty are often used as a threshold for giftedness in educational placement.

These tests are updated periodically to ensure they remain accurate. This leads us to a phenomenon known as the Flynn effect. As of the mid-twenty-twenties, researchers have observed that IQ scores rose steadily throughout the twentieth century. Because it is unlikely that human intelligence itself increased so rapidly, researchers infer from longitudinal and cross-sectional studies that better nutrition, improved education, and more stimulating environments are associated with these gains. It is important to note that this is a correlation observed across populations, not a proven direct cause for every individual.

The history of intelligence testing is also a cautionary tale. In the early twentieth century, the eugenics movement—a pseudoscientific effort to improve the human race by encouraging reproduction among those deemed fit and discouraging it among others—hijacked intelligence testing. These early tests were often culturally biased, favoring people from Western, educated backgrounds. They were used to label immigrants and marginalized groups as inherently inferior, directly influencing discriminatory immigration policies.

To address these biases, researchers have developed culture-fair or non-verbal measures, such as Raven’s Progressive Matrices, which rely on pattern recognition rather than language-based knowledge. Despite these efforts, we recognize that while intelligence tests provide useful information about cognitive strengths, they are not perfect measures of human potential. They are limited by the cultural context in which they were created. Furthermore, intelligence is not a fixed, unchanging entity; it is a dynamic trait influenced by a lifetime of experiences.

Reliability and validity are technical requirements, but the ethical application of these tools requires a constant awareness of our own biases. We have learned that intelligence tests can be helpful in identifying students who need extra support or in diagnosing specific cognitive challenges. Yet, we must always resist the urge to view a single number as a complete definition of a human being. Understanding how these tests are built is the first step toward using them wisely and preventing the abuses of the past.

## Key ideas

- Intelligence tests must be standardized against a representative population and demonstrate reliability through consistent scores and validity through their ability to predict outcomes.
- Deviation IQ scores use a mean of one hundred and a standard deviation of fifteen, placing about sixty-eight percent of the population within the average range.
- The Flynn effect describes the historical rise in IQ scores over the twentieth century, which researchers associate with environmental improvements rather than genetic changes.
- To mitigate cultural bias, psychologists utilize non-verbal, culture-fair measures, though the history of testing reminds us that these tools must be used with ethical caution to avoid discriminatory misuse.
