# The Physics of Sound

Imagine standing by a train track as a locomotive rushes past, its horn blaring a steady note. To your ears, that note shifts instantly from a high, sharp pitch to a lower, dull roar the moment the engine passes you. That everyday auditory surprise is not a trick of your mind. It is a fundamental consequence of how mechanical waves travel through air, and it reveals how our ears translate invisible changes in pressure into the rich tapestry of human speech, music, and noise.

In our last lesson, we saw how waves interact to create standing patterns when confined to strings and tubes. Today, we turn our focus to the specific type of longitudinal wave that travels through fluids and solids to reach our ears: sound. A sound wave is a mechanical pressure wave. As an object vibrates, it pushes and pulls on the surrounding air molecules. Where those molecules are crowded together, we get a region of high pressure called a compression. Where they spread apart, we get a region of low pressure called a rarefaction. These alternating squeezes and stretches propagate outward from the source at the local speed of sound, which in room temperature air is about three hundred forty-three meters per second.

Our brains translate the physical properties of these pressure waves into two primary subjective experiences: pitch and loudness. Pitch is our perception of how high or low a sound is, and it corresponds directly to frequency, which is the number of pressure cycles passing a point each second, measured in hertz. A high-frequency wave with short wavelengths packs many cycles into every second and registers as a high-pitched whistle. A low-frequency wave with long wavelengths arrives slowly and registers as a deep bass rumble. Loudness, meanwhile, corresponds to the amplitude of the pressure wave. Amplitude measures the magnitude of the pressure fluctuations between compressions and rarefactions. Larger pressure changes mean more energy is carried by the wave, which our ears perceive as a louder sound.

To see how this works in practice, consider a guitar string vibrating at a frequency of two hundred sixty-two hertz, which is very close to middle C. If the speed of sound in the surrounding air is three hundred forty-three meters per second, we can find the spatial length of one complete wave cycle by dividing the speed of sound by the frequency. Dividing three hundred forty-three meters per second by two hundred sixty-two hertz gives a wavelength of about one point three meters. This means that every one point three meters along the path of the sound wave, the air pressure goes through one complete cycle of compression and rarefaction. A quick sense check confirms this scale makes sense for human speech and acoustic instruments, which routinely produce sound waves spanning from fractions of a meter to several meters in length.

Things grow even more interesting when the source of the sound is in motion. We call this phenomenon the Doppler effect, named after the Austrian physicist Christian Doppler. When a sound source stands still, it emits concentric spherical waves of pressure that expand outward evenly in all directions. But if the source starts moving toward you while it vibrates, it chases after its own previously emitted waves. This compresses the distance between wave fronts ahead of the moving source, making the wavelength shorter and the frequency higher than normal. Conversely, as the source moves away from you, it stretches the distance between wave fronts behind it, making the wavelength longer and the frequency lower.

Let us walk through a quick numerical example of this shift. Suppose an ambulance siren emits a steady frequency of seven hundred hertz while stationary. If that ambulance drives toward a stationary listener at a speed of thirty meters per second, while the speed of sound in air is three hundred forty meters per second, the perceived frequency increases. We calculate this by taking the speed of sound, dividing it by the speed of sound minus the source speed, and multiplying that ratio by the source frequency. Subtracting thirty meters per second from three hundred forty meters per second gives three hundred ten meters per second. Dividing three hundred forty meters per second by three hundred ten meters per second gives a ratio of about one point one. Multiplying seven hundred hertz by that ratio yields a perceived frequency of about seven hundred sixty-eight hertz. The listener hears a noticeably sharper pitch until the vehicle passes, at which point the denominator becomes the speed of sound plus the source speed, dropping the pitch down to about six hundred forty-three hertz. This shift lets you track moving objects by ear alone.

Understanding how pressure waves create pitch and loudness, and how motion alters that perception, sets the stage for exploring how musical instruments select and amplify specific frequencies.

## Key ideas
- Sound is a longitudinal mechanical pressure wave consisting of alternating compressions and rarefactions traveling through a medium.
- Pitch corresponds to the frequency of the wave measured in hertz, while loudness corresponds to the amplitude of the pressure fluctuations.
- The Doppler effect describes the shift in perceived frequency that occurs when a sound source and an observer are in motion relative to one another.
- Wave speed remains constant for a given medium and temperature, meaning that changes in source frequency result in inverse changes in wavelength.
