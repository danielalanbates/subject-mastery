# Concepts, Schemas and Problem Solving

Imagine you are walking into a kitchen you have never visited before. You see a metal object with a handle and a spout sitting on the stove, and you immediately know it is a kettle. You do not need to pause and analyze its components to figure out its purpose. This rapid recognition is possible because your brain organizes the world into concepts, which are mental groupings of similar objects, events, or people. By categorizing the kettle as a member of the kitchen-tool category, you save yourself the mental effort of relearning its function. Evidence from cognitive psychology, particularly studies on categorization, strongly supports the idea that humans naturally organize information into hierarchies. We categorize items at a superordinate level, such as furniture, a basic level, such as chair, and a subordinate level, such as a desk chair. The basic level is typically the most efficient for our daily interactions.

To make this classification even more efficient, your brain often relies on a prototype. A prototype is the best example or the mental average of a category. When you hear the word bird, you likely think of a robin rather than a penguin. If an object closely matches your prototype, you categorize it quickly. Research using reaction-time tasks consistently shows that people identify prototypical members of a category faster than atypical ones, providing robust evidence for this model of mental organization.

These concepts are organized into larger frameworks called schemas. A schema is a structured body of knowledge that helps you interpret and navigate your environment. Schemas include scripts, which are sequences of expected behaviors for a given situation, such as how to act in a restaurant, and frames, which are mental structures that help us understand the context of an event. When you enter a room and see balloons, your party schema activates, helping you predict what will happen next. While memory involves storing information, schemas provide the context that gives that information meaning.

However, life frequently presents us with challenges that our existing schemas cannot solve. This is where problem-solving comes in. Sometimes we use trial and error, which is trying various solutions until one works. This is useful for simple tasks, like finding the right key for a locked door. For more complex problems, we might use an algorithm. An algorithm is a step-by-step procedure that guarantees a correct result because it follows a logical, exhaustive set of rules. While algorithms are reliable, they are often computationally expensive and slow.

Often, we experience a sudden insight, which is a flash of realization that provides the answer without conscious, step-by-step deliberation. Neuroscientific research, using functional magnetic resonance imaging, has identified specific neural correlates for these moments. Specifically, a burst of gamma-band activity in the anterior superior temporal gyrus often precedes an insight. This suggests that the brain engages in unconscious restructuring, quietly connecting disparate pieces of information before the solution enters your conscious awareness.

Despite our mental agility, we are prone to getting stuck. One major obstacle is fixation, the inability to see a problem from a fresh perspective. A specific manifestation of this is functional fixedness, where we fail to see that an object can be used for something other than its intended purpose. For example, if you need a paperweight but only have a heavy book, functional fixedness might prevent you from realizing the book can serve your need. We also fall into a mental set, a tendency to approach a problem with the same mindset that has worked in the past. If you have always opened a door by turning the handle, you might struggle if you encounter a door that requires a push, even if the door is clearly labeled.

Overcoming these blocks requires cognitive flexibility. Recognizing that your current mental set might be preventing you from seeing the solution is the first step toward clearer thinking. While schemas and concepts help us navigate the world with speed and ease, they can also limit our creativity when we rely on them too rigidly. As we move forward, we will look at how these mental shortcuts, known as heuristics, influence our decisions and how they can sometimes lead us into predictable biases.

## Key ideas
- Concepts are organized into hierarchies, ranging from broad superordinate categories to specific subordinate ones, with the basic level being the most efficient for daily use.
- Schemas function as mental blueprints, incorporating scripts for expected behaviors and frames for understanding context.
- Insights are supported by measurable neural activity, specifically gamma bursts in the anterior superior temporal gyrus, following periods of unconscious restructuring.
- Functional fixedness is a specific type of fixation where our reliance on an object’s traditional use prevents us from seeing its potential for solving new problems.
