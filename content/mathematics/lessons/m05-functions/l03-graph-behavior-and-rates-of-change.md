# Graph Behavior and Rates of Change

Imagine tracking the temperature of a greenhouse from dawn until midnight. The resulting curve climbs as the sun warms the glass, plateaus in the afternoon, and then drops into the evening. To truly understand this thermal journey, you need more than a list of points. You need a narrative of the graph. Last time, we mapped out domains and built piecewise functions to handle sudden shifts in behavior. Today, we turn our attention to the shape of those curves, translating ups and downs into precise mathematical descriptions, and measuring how fast things change along the way.

Let us start by looking at where a graph anchors itself on the coordinate plane. The intercepts are the points where our curve crosses the horizontal and vertical axes. To find where a function crosses the vertical axis, we evaluate the function at zero. If the formula is f of x equals x squared minus four x plus three, evaluating f of zero gives three, meaning the graph crosses the vertical axis at the point zero comma three. To find where the curve crosses the horizontal axis, we set the entire function equal to zero and solve for x. Setting x squared minus four x plus three equal to zero, we can factor the left side into the quantity x minus one times the quantity x minus three. This reveals two horizontal intercepts: one at one comma zero, and the other at three comma zero. These intercepts give us fixed reference points in an otherwise unbounded plane.

Beyond crossing the axes, a function's story is told by its direction. As you move from left to right across a graph, the curve might climb, fall, or stay completely flat. We say a function is increasing on an interval if its height grows as the input grows, and decreasing if its height shrinks as the input grows. There is also a third category of graph behavior: constant intervals, where the function neither increases nor decreases, remaining at a steady height as the input grows. For our parabola given by f of x equals x squared minus four x plus three, the curve falls as x moves from negative infinity up to two, and then it rises as x moves past two toward positive infinity.

The exact turning point where the graph switches from falling to rising is a local minimum, which is part of a broader study of local extrema. Local extrema refer to points where a function reaches a local high or a local low compared to all immediately neighboring points. These local extrema are distinct from absolute extrema, which represent the single highest or lowest value the function achieves across its entire domain. For our parabola, the lowest point sits at two comma negative one, which happens to be both a local minimum and the absolute minimum of the function. If a graph curves upward to a peak before heading back down, that peak is a local maximum.

Knowing where a graph rises and falls tells us its qualitative direction, but how do we measure the steepness of that climb or descent between two specific locations? Enter the average rate of change. Suppose we want to know how fast our function grows between the input values one and four. We first find the corresponding outputs. For an input of one, the output is zero. For an input of four, evaluating four squared minus sixteen plus three gives an output of three. The average rate of change is the change in the outputs divided by the change in the inputs. Here, the change in the outputs is three minus zero, which is three. The change in the inputs is four minus one, which is three. Dividing three by three gives an average rate of change of one. On average, between x equals one and x equals four, the function rises one vertical unit for every one horizontal unit.

We can also see a negative average rate of change by looking at a falling interval. If we calculate the average rate of change for our parabola between the input values zero and two, the output at zero is three, and the output at two is negative one. The change in outputs is negative one minus three, which is negative four. The change in inputs is two minus zero, which is two. Dividing negative four by two gives an average rate of change of negative two, reflecting the downward trend of the graph on that interval.

In algebra, this concept is formalized by the difference quotient. The difference quotient is f of the quantity x plus h, minus f of x, all divided by h. This algebraic expression represents the average rate of change of the function over a small interval of length h, serving as a foundational tool you will use extensively in calculus to study instantaneous rates of change.

This average rate of change is simply the slope of the straight line connecting two points on the graph, often called a secant line. These behaviors also apply directly to piecewise-defined functions, where different rules govern different parts of the domain. For a piecewise function, you must check the intercepts, test for increasing and decreasing intervals, and calculate average rates of change across each distinct formula piece separately, paying careful attention to what happens at the boundary points where the function rules switch. By combining our knowledge of intercepts, local and absolute extrema, intervals of behavior, and average rates of change, we can describe any continuous curve with remarkable accuracy. As we move forward, we will see how shifting these entire graphs around the coordinate plane alters every single one of these behaviors.

## Key ideas
- The vertical intercept of a function is found by evaluating the function at zero, while horizontal intercepts occur where the function equals zero.
- A function can be increasing, decreasing, or constant on various intervals depending on whether its outputs grow, shrink, or stay flat as inputs increase.
- Local extrema are peaks and valleys relative to neighboring points, whereas absolute extrema represent the single highest or lowest values across the entire domain.
- The average rate of change, expressed algebraically as the difference quotient, measures the steepness of a graph between two points by dividing the change in outputs by the change in inputs.
