# Income Inequality and Redistribution

Imagine lining up one hundred people in a room, ordered from the person with the least wealth to the person with the most, and stacking their fortunes in front of them as literal piles of gold. In a perfectly equal society, every pile would be identical. But in the real world, the first ninety piles might form a low, flat terrace across the floor, while the final few piles stretch upward like skyscrapers toward the ceiling. Economists study these disparities not just to tally numbers, but to understand the health of the economic engine. Building directly on our previous look at basic poverty safety nets, we now turn our lens to the broader shape of income and wealth distribution across an entire population.

To visualize this uneven landscape, economists use a clever graphical tool called the Lorenz curve. Imagine drawing a square box where the horizontal axis represents the cumulative percentage of households, moving from zero to one hundred percent, and the vertical axis represents the cumulative percentage of total income they earn. If income were distributed with absolute equality, every ten percent of households would earn exactly ten percent of the income, creating a straight diagonal line running from the bottom left corner to the top right. We call this the line of absolute equality. The actual Lorenz curve, however, sags downward below that line, because the bottom twenty or forty percent of households take in a much smaller share of total income. The greater the bow or sag in that curve, the greater the inequality in that economy.

We can turn that visual curve into a single, precise number using the Gini coefficient. Picture the area trapped between the straight line of absolute equality and the bowed Lorenz curve, and compare it to the entire triangular area underneath the line of equality. The Gini coefficient is simply the ratio of that trapped area to the total triangle. If a society has a Gini coefficient of zero, there is total equality, meaning the Lorenz curve matches the diagonal line. If the coefficient reaches one, absolute inequality reigns, meaning a single person holds every last penny in the economy. Real-world Gini numbers usually fall somewhere between zero point two five and zero point six zero, allowing us to compare inequality across different nations and historical eras with surprising clarity.

What drives these wide variations in income and wealth? Several powerful economic forces are at play. Technological change has heavily favored skilled, highly educated workers over routine labor, driving a wedge between their earnings. Globalization allows top innovators, executives, and superstar performers to access massive global markets, multiplying their compensation far beyond historical norms. Furthermore, wealth tends to compound over time; capital begets more capital through investments, inheritances, and asset appreciation. These market dynamics mean that even in a growing economy where total output rises, the distribution of that new wealth can become increasingly concentrated at the top.

When governments look at high Gini coefficients, many decide to step in through public policy and redistribution. Redistribution typically relies on a combination of progressive taxation, where high earners pay a larger share of their income in taxes, and targeted transfer payments, such as social security, food assistance, and housing vouchers. These interventions physically reshape the Lorenz curve, pulling the bottom up and smoothing out the extremes. For example, if a government taxes a wealthy earner at a high rate and channels those funds into healthcare and education for low-income families, market income inequality is transformed into a more compressed disposable income inequality.

Yet every redistributive policy comes with classic economic trade-offs, particularly involving incentives and efficiency. When taxes on high incomes or capital gains rise significantly, they can alter human behavior. Highly skilled professionals, entrepreneurs, and investors might reduce their work hours, move their capital abroad, or invest less in risky ventures, which can slow overall economic growth. On the other end, overly generous transfer payments can sometimes weaken the incentive to seek employment or pursue further education. Economists often debate the optimal balance here, weighing the moral and social benefits of a compressed income distribution against the potential loss of productive output and dynamic market energy.

Ultimately, deciding how much a society should redistribute wealth is not just a technical puzzle for spreadsheets, but a fundamental question about collective values and the rules of governance. How citizens and leaders weigh these competing priorities shapes the political battles that determine tax codes and spending bills. As we move forward, we will examine how these very choices are forged through political competition and institutional design, exploring how public choice theory explains the inner workings of government decision-making.

## Key ideas
- The Lorenz curve visually maps income inequality by plotting the cumulative percentage of households against their cumulative share of total income.
- The Gini coefficient condenses this distributional data into a single numerical index ranging from zero for total equality to one for absolute inequality.
- Underlying drivers of inequality include skill-biased technological change, globalization, and the natural compounding of accumulated wealth.
- Government redistribution efforts, such as progressive taxation and transfer payments, can reduce inequality but often introduce trade-offs involving economic incentives and efficiency.
