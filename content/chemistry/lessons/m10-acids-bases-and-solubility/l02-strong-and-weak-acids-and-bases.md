# Strong and Weak Acids and Bases

Imagine you are in a kitchen, holding a bottle of vinegar and a bottle of hydrochloric acid, which is often sold as a heavy-duty cleaner. Both are acidic, and both can sting if they touch a cut on your finger. Yet, if you were to accidentally spill a drop of vinegar, you would simply wipe it up. If you spilled that same amount of concentrated hydrochloric acid, it could eat through your clothing and cause a serious burn. Why the difference? The answer lies in how these substances behave when they dissolve in water. While our last lesson focused on how we measure acidity using the scale of potential of hydrogen, today we look at what is actually happening at the molecular level to make an acid or base strong or weak.

When we say an acid is strong, we mean it is a bit like a generous giver that never wants its gift back. A strong acid completely dissociates in water. This means that every single molecule of the acid breaks apart into its constituent ions. If you put one hundred molecules of hydrochloric acid into water, you will end up with one hundred hydrogen ions and one hundred chloride ions. There is no hydrochloric acid left intact. Because every molecule contributes to the concentration of hydrogen ions, the resulting solution is highly acidic.

Weak acids, like the acetic acid found in vinegar, are far more cautious. When you put acetic acid into water, it reaches a state of dynamic equilibrium. Some molecules break apart into hydrogen ions and acetate ions, but many others stay together as whole acetic acid molecules. Because only a small fraction of the molecules ionize, the concentration of hydrogen ions remains much lower than it would be for a strong acid of the same concentration. To quantify this behavior, chemists use the acid ionization constant. This value is a ratio that compares the concentration of the products of the dissociation to the concentration of the remaining intact acid. A very small acid ionization constant tells us that the equilibrium favors the intact acid, confirming that it is weak.

We apply the same logic to bases. A strong base, such as sodium hydroxide, dissociates completely to release hydroxide ions into the solution. A weak base, such as ammonia, only partially reacts with water to produce hydroxide ions. To quantify the strength of a weak base, we use the base ionization constant. This constant is a ratio that compares the concentration of the products of the base reaction with water to the concentration of the remaining intact base. Just as with acids, a very small base ionization constant indicates that the equilibrium favors the intact base, confirming that it is weak.

We can measure this behavior using percent ionization. This is simply the amount of acid that has ionized divided by the initial concentration of the acid, multiplied by one hundred to get a percentage. For a strong acid, the percent ionization is effectively one hundred percent. For a weak acid, it might be less than one percent.

This concept of strength also explains why some salt solutions are not neutral. You might expect a salt to be neutral because it is formed from an acid and a base, but that is not always true. Salts are ionic compounds that break apart in water. Sometimes, the ions that form from the salt react with water in a process called hydrolysis. If you dissolve sodium acetate in water, the acetate ion acts as a weak base. It grabs a hydrogen ion from a water molecule, leaving behind a hydroxide ion. Because the solution now has more hydroxide ions than hydrogen ions, the salt solution becomes basic. Conversely, a salt formed from a weak base and a strong acid will produce an acidic solution because the positive ion from the weak base will release hydrogen ions into the water.

Let us walk through a quick example. Imagine you have a solution of a weak acid with an initial concentration of zero point one moles per liter. Through experimental measurements, you find that the concentration of hydrogen ions at equilibrium is only zero point zero zero one moles per liter. To find the percent ionization, you divide the hydrogen ion concentration by the initial acid concentration. Zero point zero zero one divided by zero point one equals zero point zero one. Multiply that by one hundred, and you get one percent. This confirms that only one percent of the acid molecules have ionized, clearly identifying it as a weak acid.

Understanding these differences in strength is essential for predicting how substances will interact in complex environments. Whether you are balancing the chemistry of a swimming pool or analyzing the biological fluids in your own body, the distinction between strong and weak electrolytes dictates the outcome. Now that we understand why acids and bases behave differently based on their ionization, we can explore how nature and science use these principles to resist sudden changes in acidity.

## Key ideas
- Strong acids and bases dissociate completely in water, while weak acids and bases only partially ionize and exist in a state of dynamic equilibrium.
- The acid ionization constant and the base ionization constant provide a numerical way to describe the strength of a weak acid or base by comparing the concentration of products to the concentration of intact molecules.
- Percent ionization is calculated by dividing the concentration of ionized acid by the initial concentration and multiplying by one hundred.
- Salt solutions can be acidic or basic because the ions produced by the salt may undergo hydrolysis, reacting with water to change the balance of hydrogen and hydroxide ions.
