# Persecution Under Rome

Imagine standing in a crowded, sun-drenched stadium in the heart of the Roman Empire. You are a citizen of the second century, surrounded by thousands of people roaring for blood. To your neighbors, this is entertainment. But to the small group of people being led into the arena, this is a final, public testimony of their faith. Why would the most powerful empire on earth, a society known for its law, order, and relative tolerance of diverse local gods, feel so threatened by a small, growing movement of Christians that it would resort to state-sponsored execution?

In our last lesson, we explored the parting of the ways between the early church and the synagogue. As Christianity moved out of its Jewish cradle and into the wider Greco-Roman world, it began to look different to the authorities. Roman society was built on the idea that social harmony depended on keeping the gods happy. This was known as the peace of the gods. Because Christians refused to burn incense to the Roman emperor or participate in the traditional public sacrifices, they were often viewed not as a separate religion, but as dangerous atheists who invited divine wrath upon the state.

Crucially, there was no general, empire-wide law against Christianity for the first two centuries. Persecution was typically an ad hoc affair, driven by local officials responding to charges of illegal assembly or the vague accusation of superstition. The first state-sponsored action against the church occurred under Emperor Nero in 64 CE, following the great fire of Rome. Nero used Christians as convenient scapegoats to deflect blame from himself, leading to brutal executions in the capital.

Following that, localized persecution remained the norm. A governor in a province like Bithynia, in what is now Turkey, might receive a report that Christians were meeting in secret and refusing to honor the state. If the governor felt the local population was becoming agitated, he might arrest a few leaders to restore order. This was the case for Ignatius of Antioch, in what is now southern Turkey, who was taken to Rome to face the lions about 107 CE. He famously wrote to the Roman Christians, begging them not to prevent his martyrdom, viewing his death as a way to imitate the sufferings of Christ.

The intensity of these trials varied wildly. Sometimes, Christians were simply ignored. Other times, they were interrogated and given a chance to recant by offering a pinch of incense to the imperial image. Those who refused were often executed, while those who complied were released. This reality created a deep crisis within the church. What should the community do with those who had denied their faith to save their lives? This question of the lapsed became a major point of contention, shaping how the church defined repentance and membership.

By the middle of the third century, however, persecution became more systematic. Emperors like Decius and later Diocletian began to view the church as a state within a state. They saw the church’s growing organization and its refusal to bow to imperial authority as a direct threat to the unity of the empire. They issued edicts requiring all citizens to perform public sacrifices and obtain certificates to prove their loyalty. This was no longer just about local unrest; it was an attempt to force the church into submission or destroy it entirely.

Yet, rather than breaking the church, these years of trial acted as a crucible. The term martyr comes from the Greek word for witness. The early church saw these individuals not merely as victims, but as powerful witnesses whose courage solidified the faith of those who remained. The stories of the martyrs, such as the noblewoman Perpetua and her servant Felicity, who were killed in Carthage, in what is now Tunisia, about 203 CE, were circulated throughout the empire. Their steadfastness became a source of inspiration, helping to develop a distinct cult of the martyrs that shaped Christian identity and liturgy for centuries to come.

This period of the martyrs did more than just test the church’s resolve; it forced the community to articulate exactly what it believed and why. When the state demanded a confession of loyalty, the church had to clarify its own rule of faith. As Christians faced the intellectual and legal pressures of the Roman courts, they began to write formal defenses of their life and doctrine. These writers, known as the apologists, stepped forward to argue that Christianity was not a threat to the empire, but a higher form of wisdom that could sustain it.

## Key ideas
- Roman persecution was largely driven by the belief that refusing to honor the emperor and the traditional gods threatened the stability and divine favor of the empire.
- Early persecutions were ad hoc and local, following the precedent of Nero’s state-sponsored action in 64 CE, rather than being governed by empire-wide laws.
- The term martyr means witness, and the circulation of martyr acts created a powerful identity that strengthened the faith of the broader Christian community.
- Faced with state pressure, the church was forced to refine its identity, leading to the development of the apologists who defended the faith against Roman accusations.
