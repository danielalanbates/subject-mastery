# Theories of Motivation

Imagine you have been working at your desk for three hours straight. Suddenly, you realize you are famished, your back aches, and you feel a restless urge to stand up and walk around. Why did you stop working? Was it because your body needed fuel, or was it because you were bored and craved a change of scenery? In psychology, the study of motivation explores the forces that initiate, guide, and maintain our goal-oriented behaviors. It is time to examine the internal and external engines that keep us moving through our daily lives.

Early psychologists often looked to instinct theory to explain behavior. This view suggested that humans, like animals, are driven by innate, fixed patterns of behavior that are unlearned. While humans certainly have reflexes, labeling complex human behaviors as instincts—such as calling a person’s desire to work an instinct—fails to explain the behavior. It merely labels it. By the early twentieth century, this theory largely fell out of favor because it lacked empirical support for explaining complex human actions and ignored the massive influence of learning and culture.

A more influential perspective arose with drive-reduction theory. This model suggests that we have physiological needs, such as the need for food or water, which create an aroused state of tension known as a drive. We are then motivated to reduce that drive. If your blood sugar drops, you feel hunger. You eat, the drive is reduced, and your body returns to a state of balance called homeostasis. Research has linked these drives to specific homeostatic mechanisms, such as the role of the hypothalamus in regulating internal states. While this theory explains basic biological needs well, it is limited because it cannot explain why we sometimes seek out arousal, such as when we watch a scary movie or go for a run even when we are not hungry.

This limitation led to arousal theory. This perspective argues that we often seek to maintain an optimal level of excitement rather than just eliminating tension. A key piece of evidence here is the Yerkes-Dodson law, which suggests that performance on a task is best at an intermediate level of arousal. If we are under-stimulated, we feel bored and seek activity. If we are over-stimulated, we feel stressed and seek quiet. The original Yerkes-Dodson study, published in 1908, tested mice rather than people, and it also suggested that harder tasks are best done at lower arousal. The general idea is widely taught, but evidence in humans is mixed, and the best level of arousal differs between tasks and between people.

Moving beyond biology, we must consider the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Intrinsic motivation is the internal desire to perform an activity for its own sake. Extrinsic motivation is the desire to perform a behavior to receive a reward or avoid punishment. Self-determination theory expands on this by proposing that humans have three core psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Research consistently shows that when these three needs are supported, intrinsic motivation flourishes. Furthermore, experimental studies have demonstrated the overjustification effect, where providing extrinsic rewards for tasks that are already intrinsically motivating can undermine that inner drive. This finding is well supported for expected, tangible rewards, though researchers still debate how large and general the effect is.

Perhaps the most famous framework for understanding these needs is Maslow’s hierarchy of needs. Abraham Maslow proposed that we are motivated to satisfy our needs in a specific order, starting with basic physiological requirements and moving up to self-actualization. While this pyramid is intuitively appealing, the evidence supporting it as a strict, universal hierarchy is weak. People often sacrifice lower-level needs, such as physical safety, to pursue higher-level goals, such as artistic expression or political causes. Modern psychology views the hierarchy as a flexible guide rather than a rigid rulebook.

It is important to remember that motivation is rarely the result of a single factor. It is usually a blend of biological drives, psychological desires, and social influences. When we study these theories, we are building a toolkit to understand why we do what we do. By recognizing whether our actions are driven by internal goals or external pressures, we gain greater insight into our own decision-making processes. As we move forward, we will apply these concepts to the specific, powerful drives that govern our survival. In our next lesson, we will zoom in on the biological and psychological triggers of hunger and the complex ways we regulate our eating habits.

## Key ideas

- Motivation is the study of the forces that initiate, guide, and maintain goal-oriented behavior.
- Drive-reduction theory explains how we act to restore homeostasis, while the Yerkes-Dodson law provides evidence that we perform best at optimal levels of arousal.
- Self-determination theory posits that autonomy, competence, and relatedness are essential for intrinsic motivation, which can be undermined by external rewards through the overjustification effect.
- Maslow’s hierarchy of needs is a popular conceptual framework, but empirical evidence suggests that human priorities are flexible rather than strictly ordered.
