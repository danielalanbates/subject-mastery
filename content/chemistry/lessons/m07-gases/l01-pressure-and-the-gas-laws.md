# Pressure and the Gas Laws

Have you ever noticed how a car tire looks slightly flattened on a cold winter morning, only to return to its normal shape after you have driven for a few miles? This simple observation is not just about rubber and road friction; it is a direct result of the invisible, energetic dance of gas molecules. In our previous study of molecular orbital theory, we explored how electrons inhabit specific energy levels within molecules. Now, we shift our focus from the internal architecture of atoms to the external behavior of matter in its most chaotic and energetic state: the gas phase. Unlike solids or liquids, gases consist of particles that are far apart and moving rapidly in random directions. To understand how they behave, we must first define the concept of pressure.

Pressure is simply a measure of the force exerted over a specific area. Think of the atmosphere above you right now. It consists of a vast column of air extending miles into the sky. Because air has mass, gravity pulls it down, creating pressure at the surface of the earth. We measure this force using units like atmospheres, millimeters of mercury, or the pascal. One atmosphere of pressure is defined as the average pressure at sea level, which is equivalent to seven hundred and sixty millimeters of mercury. When you study gases, you will frequently switch between these units, so becoming comfortable with these conversions is essential for solving problems accurately.

The relationships between pressure, volume, temperature, and the amount of gas are described by the gas laws. These laws were discovered over centuries of experimentation, and they provide the framework for predicting how a gas will react to changes in its environment. The most important rule to remember for all these calculations is that temperature must always be expressed in kelvins. Since the kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, it avoids the mathematical complications of negative temperatures, which would make gas law calculations impossible. To convert from degrees Celsius to kelvins, simply add two hundred and seventy-three point one five to your Celsius value.

Robert Boyle was among the first to notice that if you keep the temperature and the amount of gas constant, pressure and volume have an inverse relationship. This is Boyle's Law. If you squeeze a gas into a smaller container, the pressure rises because the particles collide with the walls more frequently. Conversely, Jacques Charles discovered that volume and temperature are directly proportional, provided the pressure remains constant. As you heat a gas, the particles gain kinetic energy and spread out, increasing the volume. This is known as Charles's Law.

Next, we have the law associated with Guillaume Amontons and Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac. They observed that if you hold the volume constant, the pressure of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature. If you heat a sealed container of gas, the pressure inside will increase, which is exactly why pressurized spray cans carry warnings against exposure to extreme heat. Finally, Amedeo Avogadro contributed the idea that equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of molecules. This means that the amount of gas, measured in moles, is directly proportional to the volume it occupies.

Let us look at a quick example using Boyle's Law. Imagine you have a gas trapped in a cylinder with a movable piston. The gas currently occupies two liters at a pressure of one atmosphere. If you push the piston down to compress the gas into a volume of only one liter, what happens to the pressure? Because the volume was cut in half, the pressure must double to maintain the inverse relationship. Therefore, the new pressure will be two atmospheres.

We can apply these principles to almost any scenario involving gas behavior. Whether it is the expansion of a balloon in the sun or the way your lungs draw in air, these laws provide the mathematical language to describe the natural world. By understanding how these four variables interact, we can predict the behavior of gases with remarkable precision. While these individual laws are powerful on their own, they are often just pieces of a much larger puzzle. In our next lesson, we will combine these individual relationships into a single, comprehensive equation known as the ideal gas law, which allows us to calculate the state of a gas sample even when multiple variables change at once.

## Key ideas
- Pressure is defined as the force exerted over a given area, and standard atmospheric pressure is equal to seven hundred and sixty millimeters of mercury.
- All gas law calculations require temperatures to be converted into kelvins by adding two hundred and seventy-three point one five to the value in degrees Celsius.
- Boyle's Law establishes that pressure and volume are inversely proportional, while Charles's Law and the law of Amontons and Gay-Lussac show that temperature is directly proportional to volume and pressure, respectively.
- Avogadro's Law states that the volume of a gas is directly proportional to the number of moles of gas present when temperature and pressure are held constant.
