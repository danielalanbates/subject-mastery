# Indeterminate Forms and L'Hôpital's Rule

Imagine you are pushing a heavy sled across snow, and you want to know your exact speed at a single frozen second. When you try to calculate it using the standard formula for average speed, you divide the change in distance by the change in time. But as your time interval shrinks down toward zero, both your distance traveled and your time elapsed shrink down toward zero right along with it. You end up trying to evaluate zero divided by zero. That expression is a mathematical ghost. It has no fixed value on its own. Yet, as we saw when exploring how derivatives describe the shape of graphs, limits allow us to pierce through this fog and find a definite answer.

In earlier modules, we used algebra and factoring to strip away zero from denominators. But what happens when algebraic tricks fail us entirely? Suppose you are investigating the limit as x approaches zero of the quantity sine of x, all divided by x. If you substitute zero directly into that expression, the sine of zero is zero, and the denominator is zero. You face the zero over zero trap. Another common ghost is infinity over infinity, which occurs when examining limits as the variable grows without bound. In mathematics, these baffling expressions are called indeterminate forms, because knowing that the top and the bottom both head toward zero or both head toward infinity tells you nothing yet about what their ratio is doing.

Enter a brilliant piece of seventeenth-century insight known as L'Hôpital's Rule. While seventeenth-century mathematics was rocked by the bitter priority dispute between Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz over the invention of calculus, this specific rule for evaluating stubborn limits first appeared in print in a pioneering textbook written by the French mathematician the Marquis de L'Hôpital, though it was largely discovered by his brilliant contemporary Johann Bernoulli.

To use L'Hôpital's Rule correctly, we must ensure our functions meet specific mathematical conditions. Both the numerator and the denominator must be differentiable on an open interval surrounding our target point, except possibly at the point itself. Furthermore, the derivative of the denominator must not equal zero anywhere near that point. Finally, the limit of the ratio of the derivatives must actually exist or be infinite. When these hypotheses are satisfied and we face a limit of a fraction that gives the zero over zero or infinity over infinity indeterminate form, we can differentiate the numerator and the denominator separately. Then, we take the limit of that new fraction instead.

Let us walk through a concrete example step by step. Consider the limit as x approaches zero of the quantity cosine of x minus one, all divided by x squared. If you substitute zero for x, the cosine of zero is one, so the numerator becomes one minus one, which is zero. The denominator is zero squared, which is zero. We have the zero over zero indeterminate form. Since basic algebra does not offer an obvious factorization here, we apply L'Hôpital's Rule. We take the derivative of the numerator, which gives negative sine of x, and the derivative of the denominator, which gives two x.

Now we evaluate the new limit as x approaches zero of negative sine of x, all divided by two x. If we plug in zero again, the sine of zero is zero, and the denominator two times zero is zero. We still have zero over zero. Do not panic. L'Hôpital's Rule can be applied multiple times in succession as long as the indeterminate form persists. We differentiate the numerator a second time, yielding negative cosine of x, and we differentiate the denominator a second time, yielding the constant two.

With this second round complete, we evaluate the limit as x approaches zero of negative cosine of x, all divided by two. Substituting zero gives negative one divided by two, because the cosine of zero is one. The ghost vanishes, and our final answer is negative one half. L'Hôpital's Rule also applies when variables grow without bound. Consider the limit as x approaches infinity of the natural logarithm of x, all divided by x. As x grows larger, both the numerator and the denominator grow larger without bound, yielding the infinity over infinity indeterminate form. We differentiate the numerator to get one over x, and the denominator to get one, which evaluates directly to zero.

Not all indeterminate expressions come ready-made as fractions. Other indeterminate forms, such as zero multiplied by infinity, infinity minus infinity, and various powers, can be rewritten using algebra, logarithms, or exponentiation into a zero over zero or infinity over infinity form before applying the rule. For example, a product of zero and infinity can be dragged down into a fraction by moving one factor to the denominator as its reciprocal.

We must also exercise caution and verify our hypotheses every single time. If we apply L'Hôpital's Rule blindly without checking whether the limit produces a valid indeterminate form, we obtain nonsense. Whenever you face an indeterminate limit, always verify that the form is genuinely zero over zero or infinity over infinity before you differentiate. Next, we will put our growing toolkit of derivatives to work on real-world problems.

## Key ideas
- Indeterminate forms like zero over zero and infinity over infinity arise when limit substitutions yield expressions with no predefined arithmetic value.
- L'Hôpital's Rule allows us to evaluate stubborn indeterminate limits by taking the derivative of the numerator and the denominator separately.
- Other indeterminate forms involving products, differences, and powers can be algebraically rewritten into fractions before applying the rule.
- Bernoulli and the Marquis de L'Hôpital popularized this technique, which relies on the local linear behavior of functions near a point.
- Blindly applying the rule without verifying the correct indeterminate form or checking whether the hypotheses hold leads to incorrect results.
