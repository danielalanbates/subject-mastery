# Washington, Hamilton and the First Parties

Imagine you are living in 1789. The Constitution has just been ratified, but it is little more than a set of instructions on parchment. The federal government is still being built. Congress has only recently established the first executive departments, including State, Treasury, and War, along with the federal judiciary. A massive debt remains from the war for independence. The new nation is like a house with a blueprint but no walls. George Washington, the man chosen to be the first president, understood that his every action would set a precedent—a model for future leaders to follow. He had to prove that the new federal government could actually govern.

To stabilize the country, Washington turned to his Secretary of the Treasury, Alexander Hamilton. Hamilton was a brilliant, ambitious man who believed that for the United States to survive, it needed to be a centralized, industrial power. He proposed a bold financial plan: the federal government would assume the unpaid war debts of the individual states and create a National Bank. By doing this, Hamilton hoped to tie the interests of wealthy merchants and investors to the success of the federal government. He wanted to establish national credit, making the United States a reliable borrower on the world stage.

Not everyone agreed with this vision. Thomas Jefferson, Washington’s Secretary of State, led the opposition. Jefferson and his supporters, who called themselves Republicans and are often known today as Democratic-Republicans, feared that Hamilton’s plan favored the elites in northern cities at the expense of ordinary farmers. They argued that the Constitution did not explicitly grant the federal government the power to create a bank. This disagreement sparked the first real political party system in America. The Federalists, led by Hamilton, pushed for a strong central government and close ties to Great Britain. The Democratic-Republicans, led by Jefferson, championed states' rights and preferred a more agrarian society.

Foreign policy further divided these groups. In 1794, the Jay Treaty sought to resolve lingering conflicts with Great Britain. While Federalists supported the treaty as a way to avoid war and secure trade, Democratic-Republicans were outraged, viewing it as a betrayal of revolutionary ideals and a submission to British power.

This tension also hit the streets during the Whiskey Rebellion of 1794. To pay off the national debt, Hamilton had convinced Congress to pass a tax on distilled spirits. Farmers in western Pennsylvania, who relied on turning their grain into whiskey to transport it over the mountains, saw this as an unfair burden. They rose up in protest, even attacking tax collectors. Washington took the threat to federal authority seriously. He personally led a militia force into Pennsylvania to suppress the uprising. His show of force sent a clear message: the new government would not tolerate violent resistance to federal law.

The divide between the parties deepened as conflict erupted in Europe. The French Revolution led to a period of maritime tension known as the Quasi-War, an undeclared naval conflict between the United States and France from 1798 to 1800. President John Adams responded by expanding the navy, a move that Federalists cheered but Republicans feared as a step toward militarism. Amid this paranoia, the Federalist-controlled Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts in 1798. These laws made it harder for immigrants to become citizens and criminalized criticism of the government.

The Sedition Act was a direct attack on free speech. It led to the arrest of several newspaper editors who supported Jefferson. In response, Jefferson and James Madison authored the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions. These documents argued that the states had the right to judge the constitutionality of federal laws and asserted the principle of nullification, suggesting that states could ignore federal actions they deemed unconstitutional.

This Federalist overreach backfired, fueling public anger and helping Jefferson win the presidency in the election of 1800. This transition of power from the Federalists to the Democratic-Republicans was a crucial test of the American system. It proved that political parties could compete for control of the government without resorting to civil war. However, the questions of how much power the federal government should hold, and how it should treat its own citizens, remained deeply unsettled.

## Key ideas

- George Washington established the presidency by setting vital precedents, such as the use of executive power to enforce federal law during the Whiskey Rebellion.
- Alexander Hamilton’s financial plan created a national bank and assumed state debts, which built national credit but triggered the formation of the first political parties.
- The Federalist and Democratic-Republican parties emerged from deep disagreements over the power of the central government, the interpretation of the Constitution, and foreign policy toward Britain and France.
- The Alien and Sedition Acts spurred the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions, which introduced the concept of nullification and challenged the scope of federal authority.
- The election of 1800 marked the first peaceful transfer of power between opposing political parties, proving the resilience of the new constitutional system.
