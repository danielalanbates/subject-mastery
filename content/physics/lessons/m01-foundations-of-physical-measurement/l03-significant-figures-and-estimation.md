# Significant Figures and Estimation

Imagine measuring a wooden lab desk with a standard measuring tape. You record its length as 1.25 meters. But what if a colleague claims the desk is exactly 1.258934 meters long? Unless they used an optical laser calibrated in a temperature-controlled laboratory, those extra digits are meaningless. Every physical measurement carries uncertainty. In our previous lesson, we used the International System of Units to give measurements standard labels like meters and kilograms. Today, we focus on reporting those numbers honestly, ensuring we never claim more precision than our instruments can actually deliver.

Precision and accuracy sound identical in casual conversation, but physics distinguishes clearly between them. Accuracy describes how close a measured value is to the true, accepted value of a quantity. Precision describes how detailed and reproducible a measurement is. Significant figures are the digits in a measured quantity that carry actual physical meaning. They consist of every digit you know with certainty, plus one final estimated digit. If your ruler has millimeter markings, you can read 324 millimeters confidently and estimate the fraction between lines for a final digit.

To count significant figures correctly, follow a few key guidelines. All non-zero digits are significant. Any zero trapped between non-zero digits is also significant, as in 105 meters, which has three significant figures. Leading zeros, however, are merely placeholders that locate the decimal point. In the quantity zero point zero zero four two kilograms, there are only two significant figures, the four and the two. Trailing zeros located after a decimal point do count, because their presence shows explicit measurement down to that decimal place. Reporting twelve point zero zero meters communicates precision to the nearest hundredth of a meter.

A problem arises with trailing zeros in whole numbers without a decimal point, such as 1,500 meters. It is often unclear if those zeros are precise measurements or just placeholders. To solve this, physicists use scientific notation. If you write one point five times ten to the third meters, you are indicating two significant figures. If you write one point five zero zero times ten to the third meters, you are explicitly claiming four significant figures. Scientific notation allows us to show exactly which zeros were measured and which are just setting the scale.

When combining measurements in calculations, you must avoid creating false precision. For multiplication and division, your final answer must have the same number of significant figures as the measurement with the fewest significant figures. Suppose you calculate the area of a room by multiplying a length of four point two meters by a width of one point three five meters. Your calculator displays five point six seven square meters. Because four point two has only two significant figures, you must round the final area to two significant figures.

This brings us to the standard rounding rule. When rounding a number, look at the first digit you intend to drop. If that digit is five or greater, round the previous digit up. If it is less than five, leave the previous digit as it is. In our area calculation, five point six seven rounds up to five point seven square meters because the seven is greater than five.

For addition and subtraction, the rule shifts from total significant figures to decimal places. Your final result must match the decimal place precision of the least precise input. If you add fifteen point six meters to zero point two four eight meters, your result must be rounded to the tenths place, yielding fifteen point eight meters. This prevents an uncertain tenths place from hiding behind a falsely detailed thousandths place.

While precision is essential, physics also requires the opposite skill: the ability to make rapid, approximate estimates without a calculator. These are called order of magnitude estimates, or Fermi problems, named after the physicist Enrico Fermi. An order of magnitude estimate rounds numbers to the nearest power of ten to find the rough scale of an answer, typically within a factor of ten. Fermi famously estimated the energy released by the first atomic test explosion by dropping scraps of paper as the blast wave arrived and measuring how far they blew.

Let us walk through an order of magnitude estimate: how many times does a human heart beat in a lifetime? Suppose a heart beats seventy times per minute. Round seventy to one hundred beats per minute for simple mental math. There are sixty minutes in an hour and twenty-four hours in a day, giving about 1,400 minutes per day. Multiplying by 365 days yields roughly 500,000 minutes per year. One hundred beats per minute times 500,000 minutes yields 50,000,000 beats per year. Over an eighty-year lifetime, multiplying 50,000,000 by eighty gives 4,000,000,000 beats, or four times ten to the ninth beats. We quickly learn that a heart beats on the order of billions of times.

Order of magnitude estimates provide an essential sanity check throughout physics. If a complex theoretical calculation claims that a standard car battery stores four times ten to the twentieth joules of energy, a fast back-of-the-envelope check immediately alerts you to an error. Combining the rigor of significant figures with the speed of order of magnitude estimation ensures your physics remains both precise and physically reasonable.

Whether you are carefully recording significant figures or estimating values to the nearest power of ten, you are defining the size or magnitude of a measurement. But single numbers of magnitude only tell part of the story in physics. ## Key ideas

- Significant figures represent all the reliably known digits in a physical measurement plus one final estimated digit.
- Scientific notation is used to clarify the number of significant figures in large whole numbers by showing only the measured zeros after the decimal point.
- When rounding a calculated value, the standard rule is to round the last kept digit up if the next digit is five or greater.
- In multiplication and division, the result retains the fewest significant figures of any input, while in addition and subtraction, it retains the fewest decimal places.
- Order of magnitude estimates round values to the nearest power of ten to establish the overall scale of a quantity and serve as a sanity check for complex calculations.
