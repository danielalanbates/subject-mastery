# Interpreting Motion Graphs

Imagine riding in a car equipped with a digital screen that instantly tracks your entire journey as a sweeping curve. You do not just see where you are right now. You see the entire history of your position over the last minute. This storytelling is the power of motion graphs. Building on our recent look at gravity and free fall, today we explore how reading lines on a page lets us decode an object's position, velocity, and acceleration all at once without doing heavy arithmetic.

Our first tool is the position versus time graph. Picture the horizontal axis representing time ticking forward in seconds. Picture the vertical axis representing position, measured in meters from a starting line. If a delivery van sits completely parked, its graph is a flat, horizontal line because its position does not change as the clock ticks. But if the van cruises down the road at a steady pace, its graph becomes a slanted, straight upward slope. The steepness of that line tells us how fast the van is moving. In physics terms, the slope of a position versus time graph is the velocity. A steeper line means a larger velocity, while a gentle slope means a slow crawl. If the line slants downward toward the right, the velocity is negative, telling us the van is heading backward toward our starting point.

What happens when the van speeds up or slows down? The graph stops being a straight line. It bends into a curve. When position curves upward, the slope gets steeper with every passing second. This means the velocity is increasing. To find the exact velocity at any single instant on that curved path, we look at the slope of a tangent line skimming just one point on the curve. This instantaneous slope gives us the velocity right at that exact tick of the clock.

This brings us to our second major tool, the velocity versus time graph. Here, time stays on the horizontal axis. The vertical axis now shows velocity in meters per second. If our delivery van maintains a constant speed of twenty meters per second, its velocity graph is a flat horizontal line hovering at the height of twenty. The slope of this velocity graph is zero because the speed is not changing. But when the van steps on the gas pedal or hits the brakes, the line tilts up or down. The slope of a velocity versus time graph gives us the acceleration, measured in meters per second squared. A sharp upward tilt means a high positive acceleration, while a downward tilt means deceleration.

Beyond slopes, velocity versus time graphs offer a second secret treasure. This is the area enclosed underneath the graph line. Imagine shading the space between the velocity line and the horizontal time axis from zero to five seconds. For our van traveling at twenty meters per second for five seconds, that shaded region forms a rectangle with a base of five seconds and a height of twenty meters per second. Multiplying those two values gives one hundred meters. That area represents the total displacement of the van. When the graph dips below the time axis into negative velocity, the area becomes negative, subtracting from our net displacement as the vehicle reverses direction. If we want the total distance traveled rather than the net displacement, we take the sum of the absolute values of all areas above and below the axis, treating every chunk of area as positive. Similarly, the area under an acceleration versus time graph represents the total change in velocity over that time interval.

Let us walk through a quick example with round numbers. Suppose a runner starts from rest and her velocity graph rises as a straight line, reaching a speed of six meters per second after three seconds. To find her acceleration, we calculate the slope of that line by taking the rise of six meters per second and dividing by the run of three seconds, giving an acceleration of two meters per second squared. To find how far she traveled during those three seconds, we look at the area under that triangular graph, which is one half times the base of three seconds times the height of six meters per second, yielding a total displacement of nine meters. Checking our intuition, a runner accelerating smoothly from zero to six meters per second covers an average speed of three meters per second for three seconds, which indeed gives nine meters.

## Key ideas
- A position versus time graph shows where an object is located at any moment, and the slope of its line represents the velocity.
- When a position versus time graph curves, the slope is changing, which means the object is accelerating.
- The slope of a velocity versus time graph represents the acceleration of the object.
- The area under a velocity versus time graph equals the net displacement of the object, while the sum of the absolute values of those areas gives the total distance traveled.
- The area under an acceleration versus time graph represents the total change in velocity over that time interval.
