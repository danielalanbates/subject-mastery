# Taylor and Maclaurin Series

Imagine you are trying to calculate the cosine of a strange angle, say one point five radians, using only a pencil and paper. You know how to add, subtract, multiply, and divide, but you do not have a trigonometric table or a calculator handy. How can arithmetic possibly capture the behavior of a wave like cosine? In our last lesson, we learned how to build power series for certain functions by manipulating geometric series, but that method only works for a lucky few. Today, we bridge that gap by discovering how to construct a polynomial approximation of any smooth function, no matter how complicated, using derivatives.

The core idea behind a Taylor series is a brilliant matchmaking strategy. We want to build an infinite polynomial, centered at some number we will call c, that matches a given function f of x not just in value at that single point, but in every single rate of change as well. If our polynomial shares the same value, the same first derivative, the same second derivative, and so on indefinitely at the number c, then near that point, the polynomial and the function must become virtually indistinguishable.

Let us build this polynomial step by step. Suppose we want our approximation to match f of c at our center point. A simple constant term, f of c, accomplishes this. But it is completely flat and misses all slope. To fix this, we add a linear term involving the quantity x minus c, multiplied by the first derivative, f prime of c. Now our approximation matches both the value and the slope at the center. To match the curvature, we add a second-degree term, involving the quantity x minus c, all squared, divided by two, and multiplied by the second derivative, f double prime of c.

If we keep going, a clear pattern emerges for the coefficients of our power series. For any positive integer n, we look at the nth power of the quantity x minus c. We multiply this power by the nth derivative of our function evaluated at the center point c. Then, we divide that product by n factorial. When our center point c is chosen to be zero, this construction is called a Maclaurin series, named after the Scottish mathematician Colin Maclaurin. A Taylor series is simply the more general version centered at any number c.

Let us construct the Maclaurin series for the exponential function e to the x from scratch. We begin by evaluating the function and all its derivatives at zero. The function e to the x evaluated at zero is one. What is its first derivative? It is still e to the x, which evaluated at zero is also one. In fact, every higher derivative of e to the x is just e to the x, meaning every single derivative evaluated at zero equals one. Putting these values into our general formula, our constant term is one, our first-degree term is one times x, and our second-degree term is one times the quantity x squared over two factorial. Thus, the Maclaurin series for e to the x is the sum from n equals zero to infinity of the quantity x to the n divided by n factorial. Because factorials grow much faster than any power, this series converges for all real numbers, giving us a precise way to compute exponential values using only basic arithmetic.

We can apply this exact same derivative machine to construct Maclaurin series for trigonometric functions like sine and cosine. For the sine function, we evaluate sine of zero, which is zero. The first derivative is cosine, and cosine of zero is one. The second derivative is negative sine, which gives zero at zero. The third derivative is negative cosine, which evaluates to negative one at zero. If we assemble these non-zero terms, the Maclaurin series for the sine of x begins with x minus the quantity x cubed over three factorial, plus the quantity x to the fifth power over five factorial, continuing with alternating signs and odd powers. A similar process for the cosine function yields a series of even powers starting with one, minus the quantity x squared over two factorial, plus the quantity x to the fourth power over four factorial.

As we look toward our next lesson, we will see how to manipulate these newly constructed Taylor series to evaluate difficult limits, approximate definite integrals, and solve complex problems that defy standard calculus techniques.

## Key ideas
- A Taylor series constructs an infinite polynomial that matches a function's value and all its derivatives at a specific center point.
- When a Taylor series is centered at zero, it is specifically called a Maclaurin series.
- The coefficients of a Taylor series are formed by taking the nth derivative of the function at the center point and dividing by n factorial.
- Common transcendental functions like the exponential, sine, and cosine can be fully represented across their domains by these polynomial series.
