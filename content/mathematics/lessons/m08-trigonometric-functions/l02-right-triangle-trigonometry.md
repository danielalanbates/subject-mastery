# Right Triangle Trigonometry

Imagine you are standing on level ground, staring up at the top of a tall lighthouse. You know how far you are standing from the base of the tower, and you can easily measure the angle of your gaze upward. How could you calculate the exact height of the lighthouse without climbing it with a tape measure? This is the fundamental challenge that gave birth to trigonometry, a branch of mathematics that translates the geometry of shapes into the language of ratios. In our previous lesson, we explored how to measure angles. Now, we turn our attention to the internal relationships within right triangles, where those angles act as keys to unlocking the lengths of unknown sides.

A right triangle contains one ninety-degree angle, which defines its structure. The side directly opposite the right angle is always the longest side, known as the hypotenuse. The other two sides are defined relative to a specific internal angle that is not the right angle. We call the side touching that angle the adjacent side, and the side across from that angle the opposite side. Because all right triangles with the same internal angles are similar, the ratios of their sides remain constant regardless of the triangle's overall size. These ratios depend only on the measure of the angle itself.

The three primary ratios are sine, cosine, and tangent. The sine of an angle is the ratio of the opposite side over the hypotenuse. The cosine of an angle is the ratio of the adjacent side over the hypotenuse. The tangent of an angle is the ratio of the opposite side over the adjacent side. For any given angle, these ratios are fixed. If you know the angle and one side length, you can use these ratios to solve for the missing sides.

Beyond these three, we define three reciprocal functions. The cosecant is the reciprocal of sine, meaning it is the ratio of the hypotenuse over the opposite side. The secant is the reciprocal of cosine, defined as the ratio of the hypotenuse over the adjacent side. Lastly, the cotangent is the reciprocal of tangent, representing the ratio of the adjacent side over the opposite side. Note that these functions have domain restrictions because we cannot divide by zero. Cosecant and cotangent are undefined when the angle is zero degrees, because the opposite side length would be zero. Secant is undefined at ninety degrees, because the adjacent side length would be zero.

Certain angles appear so frequently that we should know their exact values. For a thirty-degree angle, the sine is one half, the cosine is the square root of three over two, and the tangent is one over the square root of three. For a forty-five-degree angle, the sine is the square root of two over two, the cosine is the square root of two over two, and the tangent is one. For a sixty-degree angle, the sine is the square root of three over two, the cosine is one half, and the tangent is the square root of three.

Let us apply this to a practical problem. Suppose you are standing ten feet from the base of a wall, and you look up at a point on the wall at an angle of forty-five degrees. Since the tangent of forty-five degrees is the ratio of the opposite side over the adjacent side, and we know the tangent of forty-five degrees is exactly one, we set up an equation. One equals the opposite side over ten. Multiplying both sides by ten, we find that the opposite side is exactly ten feet long.

When we are faced with an unknown angle instead of an unknown side, we use inverse trigonometric functions. These are written as arcsine, arccosine, and arctangent. If we know that the sine of an angle is one half, we use the arcsine of one half to identify the angle as thirty degrees. Mastering these ratios allows us to move fluidly between the physical world of distances and the abstract world of angular measurement. While right triangles provide a perfect entry point, they only tell part of the story. As we broaden our perspective, we will see that these same ratios govern motion and rotation in a much wider, circular context.

## Key ideas

- The sine, cosine, and tangent functions are defined by the ratios of specific sides in a right triangle, which remain constant for a given angle due to the properties of similar triangles.
- Reciprocal trigonometric functions, specifically cosecant, secant, and cotangent, provide the inverse ratios of the sine, cosine, and tangent functions, with specific domain restrictions where division by zero would occur.
- Special right triangles with angles of thirty, forty-five, and sixty degrees allow us to determine exact trigonometric values without needing a calculator.
- Inverse trigonometric functions, such as arcsine and arctangent, allow us to determine an unknown angle measure when the ratio of two sides is known.
