# Exponential Models in the Real World

Imagine you place a small sum of money in an account, or perhaps you introduce a single bacterium into a nutrient-rich dish. At first, change feels slow and manageable. But soon, the numbers accelerate so rapidly that they outpace our everyday intuition. In our last lesson, we learned how to solve exponential and logarithmic equations using inverses. Now, we turn to the vital question of what those equations actually mean for the world around us, using exponential models to describe physical processes like population growth, radioactive decay, temperature changes, and natural resource limits.

Our first major pattern is doubling time, which applies to anything that grows exponentially, such as bacteria colonies or financial investments. Suppose a culture of microorganisms starts with one thousand cells, and we notice it doubles every three hours. We can model the population at any time t by taking our initial amount and multiplying it by two raised to the power of the quantity t divided by three. If we want to know how long it takes for the population to reach eight thousand cells, we set our model equal to eight thousand, divide both sides by one thousand to isolate the exponential expression, and obtain two raised to the power of the quantity t divided by three equals eight. Because eight is two cubed, we equate the exponents, setting the quantity t divided by three equal to three, which tells us it takes nine hours. When the resulting numbers do not match neatly as powers of two, we use logarithms to solve for time. Taking the natural logarithm of both sides brings the variable down from the exponent, allowing us to isolate t precisely.

The mirror image of doubling time is half-life, which governs radioactive decay and the breakdown of substances in medicine. When a radioactive isotope decays, its mass decreases by half over a fixed span of time. Suppose we begin with one hundred grams of a substance with a half-life of five years. The remaining mass equals one hundred times one-half raised to the power of the quantity t divided by five. If a laboratory technician needs to know when only ten grams of the substance remain, we set the expression equal to ten, divide by one hundred to get one-tenth on one side, and set one-half raised to the power of the quantity t divided by five equal to one-tenth. Since one-half and one-tenth do not share a common base, we apply natural logarithms to both sides, use the power rule to bring down the exponent t divided by five, and solve for time: t equals five times the natural logarithm of one-tenth, divided by the natural logarithm of one-half, which is about sixteen point six years.

Exponential functions also describe how hot objects cool in a cooler room, a process known as Newton's Law of Cooling. The temperature of the object at any time t is equal to the surrounding room temperature plus the initial temperature difference multiplied by e raised to the power of negative k times t, where k is a positive constant representing the cooling rate. Suppose a cup of coffee starts at two hundred degrees Fahrenheit in a room kept at seventy degrees, so the initial difference is one hundred thirty degrees. If the cooling constant is zero point one, and we want to know when the coffee reaches one hundred degrees, we set our model equal to one hundred. We subtract seventy from both sides to get thirty equals one hundred thirty times e raised to the power of negative zero point one t. Dividing by one hundred thirty gives three over thirteen equals e raised to the power of negative zero point one t. Taking the natural logarithm of both sides gives the natural logarithm of three over thirteen equals negative zero point one t. Dividing both sides by negative zero point one yields the exact time, about fourteen point seven minutes if the constant is measured per minute.

Similarly, unrestricted growth cannot continue forever in a finite world. When resources like food, space, or energy run short, population growth slows down and levels off. This phenomenon is modeled by logistic growth, where the population at any time t is given by the formula P of t equals L over the quantity one plus C times e raised to the power of negative r times t. In this formula, L represents the carrying capacity, which is the maximum population the environment can sustainably support, C is a constant determined by the initial population, and r is the growth rate. Suppose a wildlife reserve has a carrying capacity of one thousand animals, and the population begins at one hundred animals, meaning C equals nine, with a growth rate of zero point two. If we want to find when the population reaches five hundred animals, we set our logistic model equal to five hundred. We multiply both sides by the denominator, divide by five hundred to isolate the exponential expression, and obtain one plus nine times e raised to the power of negative zero point two t equals two. Subtracting one and dividing by nine gives e raised to the power of negative zero point two t equals one over nine. Taking the natural logarithm of both sides yields negative zero point two t equals the natural logarithm of one over nine. Dividing by negative zero point two gives the exact time required to reach five hundred animals, about eleven years if the rate is measured per year. By using logarithms to solve these complex equations, scientists and economists can predict everything from epidemic peaks to economic saturation, bridging pure mathematics and the physical universe. As we move forward, we will leave the realm of exponents and logarithms behind to explore angles, radians, and the periodic functions of trigonometry.

## Key ideas
- Exponential models use doubling time to track rapid growth and half-life to measure radioactive decay or substance reduction over fixed periods.
- Logarithms provide the algebraic tool needed to solve for time in exponential equations when the initial and final values do not share a convenient common base.
- Newton's Law of Cooling uses exponential expressions involving the natural base e and logarithms to determine exactly when an object reaches a target temperature.
- Logistic growth modifies standard exponential models by introducing a carrying capacity and using logarithms to find when a population reaches specific levels before leveling off.
