# Electromagnets and Motors

Imagine holding a small iron paperclip near a piece of ordinary copper wire. Nothing happens. Now, connect that wire to a battery so that electric charge starts flowing through it. Suddenly, the wire acts like a magnet, snapping the paperclip right to its side. Disconnect the battery, and the magnetic grip vanishes instantly. This invisible bridge between moving electrical charges and magnetic forces powers an astonishing share of modern technology, turning simple currents into controlled movement.

In our last lesson, we explored how permanent magnetic fields exert forces on moving charges. Here, we reverse that perspective to see how electric currents create their own magnetic fields. When electric charge moves through a conductor, it generates a circular magnetic field wrapping around the wire. If you point the thumb of your right hand in the direction the positive current flows, your fingers curl naturally in the direction of the magnetic field lines. This direct link means electricity and magnetism are two sides of the same coin, an insight first clarified by Hans Christian Oersted and later formalized by André Marie Ampère.

To make this magnetic effect strong enough to lift heavy steel beams or spin a shaft, engineers pack many turns of wire together into a coil. This configuration is called a solenoid. When current runs through a tightly wound helix of wire, the individual magnetic fields from each loop add up, creating a strong, uniform magnetic field inside the coil and a pattern outside that looks identical to the field of a bar magnet. Slip a core of soft iron inside the solenoid, and the iron domains align with the field, multiplying the magnetic strength by thousands of times over. This device is an electromagnet. You control its magnetism simply by turning the current on and off, or by changing the amount of electrical current flowing through the wire.

Let us walk through a practical calculation of how this works. The magnitude of the magnetic field inside a long solenoid equals the product of the permeability of free space, the number of turns, the current, and the inverse of the length of the coil. Suppose an engineer builds an air core electromagnet using a coil with five hundred turns of wire, operating on a direct current of two amperes over a length of zero point five meters.

To compute the magnetic field, we first multiply the number of turns, which is five hundred, by the current, which is two amperes, giving one thousand ampere turns. Next, we divide this product by the length of the coil, which is zero point five meters, resulting in two thousand ampere turns per meter. Then, we multiply this result by the permeability of free space, which is approximately twelve point five six times ten to the negative seven tesla meters per ampere. Carrying out this multiplication yields an internal magnetic field strength of about two point five millitesla. That is roughly fifty times the strength of Earth's magnetic field, though still weaker than a typical refrigerator magnet, providing a clear baseline before adding an iron core that would amplify the field to hold heavy industrial loads. When the operator cuts the current down to zero, the field collapses and the magnetic grip releases immediately.

Translating this magnetic force into continuous mechanical motion gives us the electric motor. A simple direct current motor places a rotating loop of wire, known as the armature, inside a stationary magnetic field provided by permanent magnets or external electromagnets. When current flows into the loop, the magnetic force pushes one side of the wire upward and the other side downward, creating a torque that makes the loop spin. If the current kept flowing in the exact same direction, the loop would simply stall halfway through its rotation. To keep it spinning smoothly, a clever device called a commutator reverses the direction of the current in the loop every half turn. This precise timing ensures the magnetic forces always push in the direction of rotation, turning electrical energy into continuous mechanical work.

Electric motors drive everything from factory conveyor belts to the cooling fans inside household computers. By managing the interplay between moving charges, magnetic fields, and rotating forces, we harness the fundamental rules of electromagnetism to move the physical world.

## Key ideas
- Electric currents flowing through a conductor generate circular magnetic fields that wrap around the wire.
- Wrapping a current carrying wire into a coiled solenoid creates an electromagnet whose strength is easily controlled by the current and the number of turns.
- The magnetic field inside a solenoid depends on the permeability of free space, the turn density, and the current, and can be multiplied massively by inserting a soft iron core.
- Placing a current carrying loop inside an external magnetic field produces a torque, while a mechanical commutator reverses the current every half turn to ensure continuous rotation in a direct current motor.
