# Light and Electromagnetic Energy

When you look at a neon sign glowing in the night, you are seeing far more than just a colorful display. You are witnessing a fundamental interaction between matter and energy. In our previous look at thermochemistry, we explored how energy moves through chemical reactions as heat. Now, we shift our focus to understand how atoms themselves store and release that energy. To unlock the secrets of the atom, we must first master the nature of light, which serves as our primary messenger from the subatomic world.

Physicists and chemists describe light as a wave, similar to ripples moving across the surface of a pond. Imagine a wave traveling through space. The distance from one peak of the wave to the next is its wavelength. The number of peaks that pass a specific point every second is the frequency. These two properties are inversely related. If the wavelength is long, the frequency is low. If the wavelength is short, the frequency is high. Because the speed of light is a constant, the product of the wavelength and the frequency always equals the speed of light, which is approximately three hundred million meters per second.

Light is not limited to the colors we see with our eyes. It exists as a broad range of energies known as the electromagnetic spectrum. This spectrum includes everything from low-energy radio waves and microwaves to high-energy ultraviolet rays, X-rays, and gamma rays. Visible light is just a tiny sliver in the middle of this vast range. This range relates directly to the periodic table, as the energy required to remove an electron from an atom generally increases as we move across a period from left to right, reflecting the increasing effective nuclear charge that holds electrons more tightly.

For a long time, scientists thought light was strictly a wave. However, experiments in the early twentieth century proved that light also behaves like a stream of tiny, individual packets of energy called photons. Think of a photon as a single unit of light. The energy of a photon is directly proportional to its frequency. A high-frequency photon, like those found in ultraviolet light, carries much more energy than a low-frequency photon, such as those in infrared light. This concept of quantized energy, or energy coming in specific, discrete packets, is the bridge between classical physics and the modern quantum world of the atom.

The photoelectric effect provides the most striking evidence for this particle-like behavior. When you shine light on a metal surface, it can knock electrons off the metal, creating an electric current. If light were only a wave, a dim light left on for a long time should eventually build up enough energy to eject an electron. In reality, it does not matter how long you wait or how bright the light is if the frequency is below a specific threshold frequency. Only photons with sufficient energy can overcome the work function of the metal, which is the minimum energy required to dislodge an electron. Once this threshold is met, the kinetic energy of the ejected electron equals the energy of the incoming photon minus the work function. Increasing the intensity of the light increases the number of photons striking the surface, which increases the total electric current, but it does not increase the kinetic energy of individual ejected electrons.

Let us consider a calculation. Suppose we have a photon of blue light with a frequency of six point five times ten to the fourteenth hertz. To find its energy, we multiply its frequency by Planck’s constant, which is six point six three times ten to the negative thirty-fourth joule seconds. When we multiply these values, we find that the energy of this single photon is approximately four point three times ten to the negative nineteenth joules. This tiny amount of energy is exactly what is needed to trigger changes at the atomic scale.

By understanding that light carries energy in discrete packets, we can finally begin to see why atoms behave the way they do. Atoms do not absorb or release energy in a continuous flow. Instead, they exchange energy in specific, calculated amounts. This realization fundamentally changed how we view the electron's home within the atom. As we prepare to move beyond the simple planetary model of the atom, we will look at how these quantized energy levels allow us to map out the specific light patterns emitted by different elements.

## Key ideas

- Light acts as both a wave and a stream of particles called photons, where photon energy is directly proportional to frequency.
- The photoelectric effect confirms that light must meet a threshold frequency to overcome a metal's work function and eject an electron.
- Increasing light intensity increases the number of ejected electrons but does not change the kinetic energy of individual electrons.
- The energy required to interact with electrons in atoms follows periodic trends, mirroring the way atoms hold onto their electrons across the periodic table.
