# Energy in Living Systems

Imagine sitting completely still while reading a book, yet burning enough energy to power a bright household light bulb. While it feels like you are doing no mechanical work against the outside world, your body is an engine that never truly idles. In our previous lesson, we looked at how machines use power and lose energy to friction. Today, we turn that lens inward to explore how the human body converts chemical energy into work, and how the laws of thermodynamics govern living tissue just as reliably as they govern steam engines and electrical circuits.

To understand human energy, we must trace where it begins. Food contains chemical potential energy stored in molecular bonds, primarily within carbohydrates, fats, and proteins. When your digestive system breaks these molecules down, cells capture the released energy through major metabolic pathways known as glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. These pathways package the energy into a molecule called adenosine triphosphate. You can think of adenosine triphosphate as the cellular currency of energy. When a muscle fiber contracts, the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate releases about thirty kilojoules per mole of energy, directly powering the cross bridge cycle that slides microscopic protein filaments past one another to generate tension and movement.

The body must manage its energy reserves carefully between meals. Excess chemical energy is stored in two main forms. The liver and muscles store carbohydrates as glycogen for quick mobilization, while adipose tissue stores excess energy as fat for long term reserves. Your basal metabolic rate, which is the resting metabolic energy expenditure required to keep your vital organs functioning while you do nothing at all, makes up the largest component of your daily energy use.

This process involves a fundamental transformation from chemical potential energy into mechanical work and thermal energy. Consider walking up a flight of stairs. If your mass is seventy kilograms and the stairs rise three meters, you increase your gravitational potential energy. To find this increase, multiply your mass by the acceleration due to gravity of nine point eight meters per second squared, and then multiply that result by the height of three meters. This calculation yields an increase of about two thousand and sixty joules. Your muscles accomplish this by exerting forces internally, changing chemical bonds into macroscopic movement. Yet, if you measure the total chemical energy your body consumed to climb those stairs, you will find it was significantly higher than two thousand and sixty joules.

Where did the rest of the energy go? It escaped as heat. The human body is a thermodynamic system, and biological energy conversion is far from one hundred percent efficient. In fact, human muscle efficiency typically ranges from twenty percent to twenty-five percent. If you burn ten thousand joules of chemical energy during our stair climbing example, only about two thousand to two thousand five hundred joules actually turn into mechanical work or stored potential energy. The remaining energy is released as thermal energy, which your body must continually dissipate. Physiological mechanisms such as radiation, convection, conduction, and sweating work together to release this heat and keep your internal body temperature stable.

Let us walk through a quick estimation to see this in practice. Suppose an adult consumes a diet providing ten million joules of chemical energy in a single day. If their mechanical work output over that day, including walking, lifting, and moving, totals five hundred thousand joules, what is their overall daily efficiency? We divide the useful work output of five hundred thousand joules by the total energy input of ten million joules. This yields a fraction of zero point zero five, or five percent efficiency. That is far below the muscle efficiency of twenty percent, because most of the day's energy simply keeps the body running at rest. The remaining ninety-five percent of the energy input leaves the body as heat, radiating into the environment to maintain thermal equilibrium.

Living systems prove that energy conservation applies universally. Every joule of chemical energy consumed must be accounted for, either as mechanical work, stored chemical potential energy in new tissue, or thermal energy lost to the surroundings. Energy is neither created nor destroyed inside the human body; it simply changes form, driving the complex machinery of life while warming the world around us. As we move from these living engines of flesh and bone to the study of motion and collisions, we will next investigate how objects conserve momentum when they interact and collide in the physical world.

## Key ideas
- Chemical energy from food is processed through glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation to produce adenosine triphosphate, which releases about thirty kilojoules per mole during hydrolysis to power muscle contraction.
- The body stores excess energy as glycogen in the liver and muscles and as fat in adipose tissue, with basal metabolic rate forming the largest component of daily energy use.
- Human muscle efficiency typically ranges from twenty to twenty-five percent, meaning most metabolic energy becomes thermal energy that the body must dissipate through radiation, convection, conduction, and sweating.
- The human body obeys the laws of thermodynamics, requiring a continuous input of chemical energy to sustain life while accounting for every joule through work, storage, or heat dissipation.
