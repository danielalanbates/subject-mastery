# Aggression, Helping and Attraction

Have you ever wondered why you feel an instant, inexplicable warmth toward a stranger at a coffee shop, or why you might choose to walk past someone in distress instead of stopping to help? These behaviors are not just random quirks of personality. They are the result of complex social forces that shape our interactions every single day. Today, we turn our focus to the forces that pull us apart through aggression, the factors that compel us to act on behalf of others, and the mysterious chemistry that draws us together.

Aggression is defined as any physical or verbal behavior intended to harm someone who does not want to be harmed. This behavior emerges from a combination of biological, situational, and learned factors. Biologically, research shows a modest correlation between aggression and the amygdala, a structure deep in the middle of the brain that processes threat. Scientists also observe a modest link between aggression and lower levels of serotonin, which helps regulate impulse control. Additionally, higher levels of the hormone testosterone show a modest correlational link to aggressive behavior in some contexts. However, biology is not destiny. Situational factors, such as extreme heat or frustration, correlate with increased aggressive responses in experimental settings. We also learn aggression through observation and direct reinforcement. When children witness adults acting aggressively, they are more likely to imitate those behaviors. Furthermore, when aggressive acts are rewarded or when non-aggressive behavior is punished, individuals are more likely to learn and repeat aggressive patterns.

While aggression can push people away, prosocial behavior—or acting to benefit others—is the glue of society. A famous case often cited in textbooks is the murder of Kitty Genovese in 1964. For decades, the story was told as a cautionary tale of thirty-eight witnesses who watched from their windows and did nothing. Modern research into police records and witness accounts shows this version is a myth. Few neighbors saw more than a moment of the attack, at least one or two people said they called the police, and one neighbor went out and held her until help arrived. This case sparked the study of the bystander effect. This is the tendency for individuals to be less likely to offer help when others are present. One cause is the diffusion of responsibility, where each person feels less personal pressure to intervene. Another factor is pluralistic ignorance. This occurs when people look to others to see how they are reacting. If everyone else is calm, individuals may mistakenly conclude that the situation is not an emergency. Psychologists also use the arousal-cost-reward model to explain helping. This model suggests that we weigh the emotional arousal caused by seeing someone in distress against the potential costs and rewards of intervening before we decide to act.

To understand why we help, psychologists look at both the situation and the internal state of the individual. We are far more likely to help if we notice the situation, interpret it as an emergency, and feel a sense of personal responsibility. Factors like empathy and the desire to uphold social norms also play a role. If you see someone drop their groceries, your decision to stop depends on your assessment of the situation and your belief that your action will make a difference.

Beyond aggression and helping, psychologists also study what draws us toward specific people. Attraction is not merely a matter of chance. The most reliable predictor of friendship and romance is proximity, or nearness. We are simply more likely to form bonds with people we encounter frequently. This is partly due to the mere exposure effect, which is our tendency to develop a preference for people simply because we are familiar with them.

Similarity also plays a major role. While the old adage says opposites attract, the data suggests the opposite. We tend to gravitate toward those who share our values, interests, and backgrounds. This similarity makes interactions smoother and reinforces our own worldviews. Finally, there is the powerful factor of reciprocity. We like people who like us. Knowing that someone values our company creates a positive feedback loop that strengthens the connection. Proximity, similarity, and reciprocal liking create the foundation for most of our social relationships, providing a sense of belonging and support that is essential to the human experience.

By understanding these dynamics, we gain a clearer picture of how social environments influence our daily choices. We see that our behavior is not just a reflection of our inner character, but a response to the complex, shifting landscape of the people around us.

## Key ideas
- Aggression is a multifaceted behavior influenced by biological predispositions, situational stressors, and learned responses through both observational learning and direct reinforcement.
- The bystander effect occurs when the presence of others leads to a diffusion of responsibility or pluralistic ignorance, making individuals less likely to intervene in emergencies.
- The arousal-cost-reward model suggests that people help others by weighing the emotional distress of the situation against the perceived costs and rewards of taking action.
- Attraction is driven by proximity, which increases familiarity, as well as the tendency to prefer people who are similar to us and who demonstrate that they value us in return.
