# Voting and Government Failure

Imagine a town of one hundred people deciding whether to build a new public park that costs one hundred thousand dollars. If the cost is split evenly, every resident pays one thousand dollars. Suppose that ten avid gardeners each value the park at twelve thousand dollars, while the other ninety residents value it at nothing. The total benefit is one hundred twenty thousand dollars, more than the cost, so the park is worth building. Yet in a simple majority vote, ninety people vote no and ten vote yes, and the project fails. Majority voting counts each person once; it cannot register how strongly the gardeners care.

Now flip the situation. Suppose a smaller project costs ten thousand dollars, or one hundred dollars per resident, and its only real winners are a handful of developers whose nearby land will gain eight thousand dollars in value. The project destroys two thousand dollars of value overall. But the developers organize, lobby, and turn out to vote, while the other residents, each facing a cost of just one hundred dollars, do not bother to follow the issue. The project passes. A wasteful project wins because its benefits are concentrated on a few, while its costs are spread thinly across many.

Building on our understanding of public choice theory, which applies economic tools to political decision-making, we see that democratic institutions do not always correct market failures. Instead, they can lead to government failure, a situation where political allocation of resources results in economic inefficiency and a net loss of social surplus. Just as markets can fail due to externalities or imperfect information, governments can fail because the incentives facing voters, politicians, and bureaucrats diverge from the broader public interest.

Voting rules themselves can also mislead. The median voter theorem predicts that when voters line up along a single issue, such as how much to spend, two competing candidates will drift toward the position of the voter in the middle, whatever the costs and benefits of that position. And the eighteenth-century French thinker the Marquis de Condorcet showed that majority voting can go in circles. If one third of voters rank A over B over C, another third B over C over A, and the last third C over A over B, then A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A. In 1951 the economist Kenneth Arrow proved that no ranked voting system can always avoid such problems while meeting a short list of reasonable fairness conditions.

Another structural challenge in democratic voting is logrolling, the trading of votes among legislators. Imagine two separate spending bills. One is a bridge in district alpha, which costs ten million dollars but provides only two million dollars of local benefit. The other is a harbor dredging project in district beta, costing ten million dollars for three million dollars of benefit. Both projects are economically inefficient because their costs exceed their benefits. Yet, the representative from district alpha and the representative from district beta can shake hands and agree to vote for both bills. Together, they form a winning coalition. Both wasteful projects pass, and the combined tab is handed to taxpayers nationwide.

Bureaucrats and public agencies further complicate the picture. Unlike private business owners who keep residual profits by cutting waste, government bureau managers often increase their power, prestige, and budgets by expanding agency operations. If a bureau's budget depends on demonstrating high need, its managers have little incentive to economize.

None of this means democracy is worthless. Economists who study government failure look for rules that make it less likely, such as independent cost-benefit review, transparency about who gains and who pays, and competition among levels of government. Understanding how democratic mechanics can distort resource allocation gives us a clearer picture of the modern economy. Next, we will shift our focus from public sector decision-making to the overall health of the macroeconomy, beginning with how we measure total national output.

## Key ideas
- Democratic voting systems can produce economically inefficient outcomes known as government failures, where resource allocation reduces total social surplus.
- Majority voting ignores the intensity of preferences, the median voter theorem pulls policy toward the middle voter, and Condorcet and Arrow showed that voting rules can cycle or break basic fairness conditions.
- Concentrated benefits and dispersed costs allow small, well-organized special interest groups to successfully lobby for inefficient subsidies and protections.
- Logrolling occurs when legislators trade votes to pass individual spending projects that would otherwise fail because their costs outweigh their benefits.
