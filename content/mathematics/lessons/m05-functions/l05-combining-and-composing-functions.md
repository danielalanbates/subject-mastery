# Combining and Composing Functions

Imagine you manage a small bakery where the cost of producing flour and sugar depends on the number of batches you bake today, which in turn depends on how many hours your ovens stay lit. You are not just looking at one rule in isolation; you are chaining them together. In our last lesson, we learned how to shift, stretch, and flip the rule of a single function. Today, we take the next step. We will build new functions by adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing existing ones, and more importantly, by nesting them inside each other through a process called composition.

Arithmetic with functions follows a very natural logic. Suppose you have two functions, the function named f and the function named g. If you want to add them together at a specific number like x, you simply evaluate f at x and g at x, and then add the two results. The new function, written as the quantity f plus g, evaluated at x, equals f of x plus g of x. For instance, let f of x be equal to x squared, and let g of x be equal to three times x. To find the sum of f and g at the number two, we evaluate f at two to get four, evaluate g at two to get six, and add them to get ten. Three times two is six, and four plus six is ten. The same pattern holds for subtraction, multiplication, and division, as long as we remember the rules for domains. The domain of the sum, difference, or product of two functions is the intersection of the domains of the individual functions. For division, you can never divide by zero, so wherever the denominator function evaluates to zero, that number must be excluded from the domain of our new divided function.

Now let us turn to a more powerful way of combining functions, known as function composition. This is where the output of one function instantly becomes the input for the next. Picture an assembly line in a factory. The first machine cuts raw wood into shapes, and the second machine paints whatever comes out of the first machine. In mathematics, we write this as f after g, which means we apply g first, and then apply f to that result. Always work from the inside out.

Let us trace a concrete example. Let f of x be equal to the quantity x plus five, and let g of x be equal to two times x squared. Suppose we want to evaluate f after g at the number three. First, we evaluate the inner function, g at three. Three squared is nine, and two times nine is eighteen. Now, that output of eighteen becomes the input for our outer function, f. So we evaluate f at eighteen, which means eighteen plus five, giving us twenty-three. Notice what happens if we reverse the order and look at g after f at three. First, the inner function f at three gives us three plus five, which is eight. Then, we feed that eight into the outer function g, giving us two times the square of eight, which is two times sixty-four, or one hundred twenty-eight. Composition is generally not commutative; the order matters deeply.

Finding the domain of a composite function requires a careful two-step check. First, any number you plug in must be allowed in the domain of the inner function. Second, the resulting output from the inner function must be allowed in the domain of the outer function. If a number causes a division by zero or a negative number inside a square root at either stage, it is locked out of the domain of the overall composition.

Finally, mathematicians often need to reverse this process, taking a complex function apart to see what simpler pieces built it. If you are given a function like the square root of the quantity x squared plus one, you can decompose it by spotting an inner core and an outer shell. The inner function is x squared plus one, and the outer function is the square root of x. Being able to break functions apart prepares us for our next journey, where we will discover how to completely undo a function and find its inverse.

## Key ideas
- Two functions can be added, subtracted, multiplied, or divided by performing those operations on their respective outputs for any given input.
- The domain of the sum, difference, or product of two functions is the intersection of the domains of the individual functions.
- Function composition occurs when the output of an inner function is used directly as the input for an outer function, written as f after g.
- Function composition is generally not commutative, meaning the order in which functions are nested changes the final result.
- The domain of a composite function must exclude any input that violates the domain of the inner function or produces an invalid output for the outer function.
