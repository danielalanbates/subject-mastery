# Indian Removal and the Trail of Tears

Imagine standing in the lush forests of Georgia in the late 1820s. You are a member of the Cherokee Nation, living in a home built in the American style, farming land that has been in your family for generations. You have a written constitution, a newspaper printed in both English and your own syllabary, and a legal system modeled after the United States. Yet, despite these efforts to coexist, your neighbors look at your land with envy. This tension between the expanding American Republic and the sovereignty of Indigenous nations serves as the grim backdrop for one of the most consequential policies of the Jacksonian era.

In our last lesson, we explored how Andrew Jackson championed the common white man and expanded political participation. However, that same spirit of aggressive nationalism often came at the expense of Native Americans. As settlers pushed westward, they demanded access to fertile lands occupied by nations such as the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Muscogee Creek, and Seminole. These nations lived in the Southeast, and their presence stood in the way of a white population eager for expansion. The discovery of gold in northern Georgia in 1829 further intensified this pressure, as white prospectors swarmed onto Cherokee land. Jackson, a veteran of wars against these nations, viewed their removal as a necessary policy for national security and growth.

The turning point arrived in 1830 with the passage of the Indian Removal Act. This federal law authorized the president to negotiate with Southern tribes for their removal to territory west of the Mississippi River, in what is now Oklahoma. While the act was framed as a voluntary negotiation, the reality was coercive. The federal government used threats, bribery, and the withholding of protection from state-level harassment to force tribal leaders to sign treaties.

The Cherokee people chose to fight back through the American legal system. In the case of Cherokee Nation versus Georgia in 1831, the Supreme Court ruled that the Cherokee were a domestic dependent nation, not a foreign one, and therefore could not sue the state in federal court. Yet, in a subsequent 1832 case, Worcester versus Georgia, Chief Justice John Marshall shifted course. He declared that the Cherokee Nation was a distinct community in which the laws of Georgia had no force. Marshall argued that the federal government, not individual states, held authority over tribal lands.

It was a landmark victory for Indigenous rights, but it proved hollow. President Jackson refused to use federal power to protect the Cherokee from Georgia’s encroaching laws. Instead, he continued to pressure tribal factions to sign away their ancestral homes. In 1835, a small, unauthorized group of Cherokee signed the Treaty of New Echota, which surrendered all tribal land east of the Mississippi. Principal Chief John Ross and the vast majority of the Cherokee people vehemently opposed this treaty, submitting a petition signed by thousands to the United States Senate. Despite this protest, the Senate ratified the treaty on May 23, 1836, by a vote of 31 to 15.

The enforcement of this treaty led to the infamous Trail of Tears. Beginning in 1838, the United States Army forcibly rounded up about 16,000 Cherokee people, detaining them in crowded, disease-ridden stockades before marching them thousands of miles toward the West. About 4,000 Cherokee died from cold, starvation, and exposure during the grueling journey. Other nations, including the Choctaw, Creek, and Chickasaw, faced similar forced migrations earlier in the decade, each marked by immense suffering and loss of life. The Seminole in Florida resisted with military force, leading to a prolonged and bloody conflict that resulted in the removal of many, though not all, of their people.

This era of removal was not an isolated event; it was a fundamental reshaping of the American landscape designed to open the interior for a specific economic engine. By clearing these lands of their original inhabitants, the United States government paved the way for the rapid expansion of a plantation economy. As we transition to our next topic, we will examine how this newly cleared land became the foundation for the sprawling, brutal, and highly profitable world of slavery in the cotton South.

## Key ideas

- The Indian Removal Act of 1830 established the federal policy of relocating Southeastern Indigenous nations to lands west of the Mississippi River.
- Although the Supreme Court ruled in Worcester versus Georgia that states lacked authority over tribal lands, President Andrew Jackson refused to enforce the decision.
- The 1829 Georgia Gold Rush intensified white settler pressure on Cherokee lands, accelerating the drive for forced removal.
- The forced removal of the Cherokee and other nations resulted in thousands of deaths and the total displacement of Indigenous populations from the Southeast.
- The removal policy was driven by white settler demand for land, which was subsequently converted into large-scale agricultural operations.
