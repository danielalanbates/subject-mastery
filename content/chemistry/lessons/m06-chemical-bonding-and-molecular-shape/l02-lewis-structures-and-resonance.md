# Lewis Structures, Formal Charge and Resonance

Imagine you are an architect tasked with building a complex structure using only a specific number of building blocks. In chemistry, when atoms join together to form a molecule, they face a similar constraint. They have a fixed number of valence electrons—the electrons in their outermost shell—to use for bonding. Today, we will learn how to map out exactly where those electrons go using Lewis structures. This is our blueprint for understanding how atoms are connected, which in turn dictates how a molecule behaves.

The foundation of building a Lewis structure is counting the total number of valence electrons available from all atoms. For carbon dioxide, we identify carbon in group fourteen, giving it four valence electrons, and two oxygen atoms from group sixteen, giving them six valence electrons each. That gives us a total of sixteen valence electrons to distribute. We place the least electronegative atom in the center and connect the surrounding atoms with single bonds. Each bond represents two shared electrons. We then distribute the remaining electrons as lone pairs to satisfy the octet rule for the outer atoms first. For carbon dioxide, two single bonds use four electrons and the other twelve go to the oxygens as lone pairs, but that leaves carbon with only four electrons. So we turn one lone pair on each oxygen into a second shared pair, giving two double bonds, with two lone pairs left on each oxygen.

Most atoms strive to satisfy the octet rule, meaning they want eight electrons in their valence shell to achieve stability. Hydrogen is a notable exception, as it only needs two electrons to fill its shell. Boron is often stable with six. However, elements in the third period of the periodic table or lower can have an expanded octet. Consider sulfur hexafluoride. Sulfur is in the third period and bonds to six fluorine atoms. In this case, the sulfur atom is surrounded by twelve electrons, which is perfectly stable for elements of that size because they have access to empty d-orbitals.

Sometimes, we must decide which structure is most realistic using formal charge. To find the formal charge of an atom, take the number of valence electrons the atom starts with, then subtract the number of lone pair electrons and half of the number of shared bonding electrons. The sum of all formal charges in a molecule must equal the overall charge of that molecule or ion. Let us calculate the formal charge for the nitrogen atom in a nitrate ion. Nitrate has one nitrogen and three oxygen atoms with a total charge of negative one. In a common resonance structure, nitrogen forms one double bond and two single bonds to oxygen. Nitrogen starts with five valence electrons. It has zero lone pair electrons and four bonds, which hold eight shared electrons, and half of eight is four. We subtract zero and four from five to get a formal charge of positive one. The best structure is generally the one where formal charges are closest to zero, or where negative charges reside on the most electronegative atoms.

Occasionally, no single Lewis structure accurately describes a molecule. Consider ozone, which consists of three oxygen atoms. You might draw a double bond between the first and second oxygen, or between the second and third. In reality, the electrons are not stuck in one place. They are delocalized, or spread out, across the molecule. This phenomenon is called resonance. We represent resonance by drawing all possible valid structures, known as resonance contributors, and imagining the true structure as a hybrid of them all. This resonance delocalization lowers the total energy of the molecule, which provides resonance stabilization. The bonds in a resonance hybrid are an average of the possibilities. In ozone, the bonds are not strictly single or double, but something in between.

The concept of resonance shows us that nature often avoids the rigidity of single, static bonds. When electrons can move freely across a system of atoms, the molecule becomes more stable. This is a fundamental principle in organic chemistry, where resonance explains the unique reactivity of aromatic rings and complex ions. As we study these structures, we must remember that we are working with models. These drawings are tools to help us visualize the invisible behavior of subatomic particles. While they are highly accurate for predicting connectivity, they are still human interpretations of quantum reality.

While Lewis structures provide a map of connections, molecules exist in three dimensions. The way these electron groups repel one another determines the final geometry of the molecule. As we move forward, we will see how these electron arrangements dictate the shapes that matter takes in our physical world. By mastering these basics, you are building the necessary foundation to predict how molecules interact, react, and eventually form the materials that make up our universe.

## Key ideas

- Lewis structures serve as blueprints for molecules by tracking the distribution of valence electrons to satisfy the octet rule, with exceptions for atoms like hydrogen, boron, and period-three elements that can expand their octets.
- Formal charge is a mathematical tool used to identify the most stable arrangement of atoms, with the rule that the sum of all formal charges must equal the total charge of the molecule or ion.
- Resonance occurs when electrons are delocalized across multiple bonds, creating a resonance hybrid that is more stable than any single contributor would be.
- Understanding these electron arrangements is the essential first step toward predicting the three-dimensional shapes that molecules adopt in nature.
