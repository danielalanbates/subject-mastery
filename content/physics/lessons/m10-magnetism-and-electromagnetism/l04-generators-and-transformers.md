# Generators and Transformers

When you switch on a lamp in your home, you rarely think about the massive journey that electricity has taken to reach your room. The energy likely began deep inside a roaring turbine at a power plant miles away, was squeezed to a staggering voltage for travel across the countryside, and was finally stepped down to a safe level right outside your neighborhood. That invisible, continuous dance of mechanical rotation and magnetic manipulation relies entirely on two masterworks of engineering, the electric generator and the transformer. Building on our understanding of electromagnetic induction from our last lesson, we can now see how moving a wire through a magnetic field transforms physical motion into a steady current of electric charge.

A generator is, in essence, an electric motor running in reverse. Instead of pumping current into a coil to make it spin, you force the coil to spin using an external mechanical source, such as falling water, high-pressure steam, or wind pushing against turbine blades. As this loop of wire rotates inside a fixed magnetic field, the magnetic flux passing through the loop constantly changes. According to the law of induction, this changing flux creates an electromotive force, driving charges around the circuit. Because the loop turns in a circle, the side of the loop moving upward through the field reverses direction every half turn, causing the induced voltage and current to switch back and forth in a smooth wave known as alternating current.

Imagine a simple generator with a coil of wire turning at sixty revolutions per second inside a uniform magnetic field. As the loop rotates, the generated voltage rises from zero to a peak, drops back to zero, dips to a negative peak, and returns to zero, completing one full cycle. If that loop has one hundred turns of wire and experiences a magnetic flux change that induces a peak voltage of one hundred seventy volts, you have created a reliable source of alternating electric potential. If you want to increase that output, you can spin the turbine faster to increase the frequency of the rotations, or you can strengthen the magnetic field by using powerful electromagnets instead of permanent magnets, which increases the rate at which magnetic field lines are swept across the conductors.

Yet, generating electricity is only half the battle. Transporting that electricity over long distances presents a severe obstacle because wires have electrical resistance, which turns precious electrical energy into wasted heat. To minimize these losses, power companies use transformers to alter voltages before sending the current down transmission lines. A transformer consists of two separate coils of wire wrapped around a shared iron core, with no physical connection between the two coils. When an alternating current flows through the first coil, it creates a constantly changing magnetic field inside the iron core, which then sweeps through the second coil, inducing a voltage there through mutual induction.

The magic of a transformer lies in the ratio of the number of turns in each coil. If the primary coil connected to the power source has one hundred turns and the secondary coil has one thousand turns, the secondary voltage will be ten times greater than the primary voltage. This is called a step-up transformer, and it is used at power plants to raise voltages to hundreds of thousands of volts, which allows the current to drop significantly and drastically reduces heat loss in the transmission lines. Conversely, when that high-voltage electricity reaches a city, a step-down transformer with more turns on the primary side and fewer turns on the secondary side safely reduces the voltage back down to standard household levels for everyday appliances.

Consider a step-down transformer designed to convert a high-voltage transmission line of seven thousand two hundred volts down to a domestic root mean square voltage of one hundred twenty volts. The ratio of the primary turns to the secondary turns must be sixty to one, because seventy-two hundred divided by one hundred twenty equals sixty. While the voltage drops by a factor of sixty, the available current increases by that same factor of sixty, preserving total power because energy is conserved. Through this clever pairing of generators creating electrical currents from mechanical motion and transformers adjusting voltages for safe transport, modern society lights our cities and powers our industries.

## Key ideas
- Generators convert mechanical energy into electrical energy by rotating conductors through magnetic fields to induce a current via electromagnetic induction.
- Alternating current is produced naturally by rotating generators as the loops of wire continually reverse their direction relative to the magnetic field poles.
- Transformers alter alternating voltages using mutual induction between two separate wire coils wrapped around a shared iron core.
- Step-up transformers increase voltage and decrease current for efficient long-distance power transmission, while step-down transformers reverse the process for safe consumer use.
