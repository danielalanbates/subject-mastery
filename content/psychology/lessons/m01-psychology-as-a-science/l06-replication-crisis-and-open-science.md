# The Replication Crisis and Open Science

Imagine you are reading a headline about a breakthrough study. It claims that simply standing in a specific, confident posture for two minutes changes your hormone levels and makes you more likely to take risks. It sounds empowering, and the data seems to back it up. But years later, when other researchers try to repeat the experiment using the same methods, they find no such effect. This is not just a one-time mistake. In the early twenty-tens, psychologists discovered that a surprising number of famous, widely cited findings were failing to replicate. This realization shook the foundations of the field, leading to what we now call the replication crisis.

In our last lesson, we discussed how reliability and validity are the bedrock of scientific research. If a study is reliable, you should get the same results if you run it again. When researchers across the world began trying to replicate major studies, they often found that the original results were much weaker than reported, or sometimes, entirely absent. This forced the psychological community to look in the mirror. They found that the problem wasn't necessarily bad intentions, but rather a set of common practices that, while standard at the time, made it far too easy to produce false positives—results that look statistically significant but are actually just noise.

The evidence for these systemic issues comes from meta-scientific research, which uses statistical tools to analyze the health of the scientific literature itself. For example, large-scale audits of psychological journals have demonstrated that studies with small sample sizes are indeed more likely to produce extreme, non-replicable results. This is a mathematical certainty: when you study only fifteen or twenty people, your results are highly sensitive to outliers. A single participant acting in an unusual way can skew your entire data set. Statistical simulations have confirmed that this lack of power creates a high probability of finding a significant result by pure chance.

Another issue is publication bias. Meta-analyses of academic journals consistently show that studies with surprising, positive results are published at much higher rates than those with null results. This creates a distorted view of reality, where only the wins are visible, and thousands of non-findings remain hidden. Furthermore, researchers have identified the practice of HARKing, or hypothesizing after results are known. This occurs when a scientist analyzes their data, finds an interesting pattern, and then writes their report as if they had predicted that specific outcome all along. This practice is widely considered a questionable research practice because it masks the fact that the result might have been a lucky guess rather than a tested theory.

Then there is the issue of flexible analysis, sometimes called p-hacking. If a researcher collects data on many variables, they might try different statistical combinations until they find something that crosses the threshold of significance. Because they did not decide on their methods beforehand, they might inadvertently cherry-pick the data to fit a narrative. Research into these practices suggests that this flexibility significantly inflates the rate of false positives.

To fix these problems, the field began a movement toward open science. The goal is to make the entire research process transparent and reproducible. One of the most important tools is preregistration. When a scientist preregisters their study, they submit their research plan, including their hypothesis and how they intend to analyze the data, to a public registry before they collect participants. This prevents the temptation to change the rules of the game halfway through. The field is also adopting registered reports, a publishing format where peer review occurs before the data is collected. This ensures that the study is judged on the quality of its design, not on whether the results turned out to be positive.

Large-scale replication projects have also become a standard for evidence. Instead of relying on a single, small study, researchers now team up to test the same question with hundreds or thousands of participants. These projects are more robust and provide a clearer picture of whether an effect is real. For example, when researchers re-examined the famous marshmallow test, they found that the original link between delaying gratification and future success was overstated. When they accounted for factors like a child’s home environment and economic background, the impact of willpower alone was much smaller than previously thought.

The replication crisis was a painful period, but it has made the field stronger. By embracing open science, researchers are now more transparent and rigorous. We have learned that the most important part of science is the ability of the community to verify results over time. As we move forward, we carry this commitment to evidence into our study of how our physical bodies and evolutionary history shape the way we think, feel, and act.

## Key ideas
- The replication crisis refers to the period in the early twenty-tens when researchers discovered that many published, famous findings could not be repeated in subsequent studies.
- Common practices that contributed to the crisis included using small, underpowered samples, publication bias against negative results, and flexible data analysis techniques.
- HARKing, or hypothesizing after results are known, is a questionable research practice that misrepresents exploratory findings as confirmed predictions.
- Preregistration and registered reports are key open-science reforms that require researchers to commit to their methods before seeing their data to ensure transparency.
- Large-scale replication projects have replaced isolated, small studies to provide more accurate and reliable evidence about human behavior.
