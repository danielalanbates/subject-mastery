# Enthalpy and Thermochemical Equations

Imagine you are holding a cold pack used for a sports injury. When you snap the inner pouch, the pack instantly turns icy cold. Or, consider the warmth of a hand warmer that heats up as soon as you expose it to the air. These reactions are not just changing the identity of the chemicals inside; they are moving energy in the form of heat. In our last lesson, we used calorimetry to measure the heat exchanged during a process by observing temperature changes in the surroundings. Today, we refine that concept by looking at the energy stored within the chemical bonds themselves, a property we call enthalpy.

Enthalpy is a measure of the total heat content of a system. When a chemical reaction occurs at constant pressure, which is true for most experiments conducted in open beakers or flasks, the heat gained or lost by the system is exactly equal to the change in enthalpy. We represent this change with the symbol delta H. Because this change depends only on the starting materials and the final products, it acts as a state function. This means it does not depend on the path taken, only on the difference between the beginning and the end states.

When we write a balanced chemical equation, we can include the enthalpy change right next to it to create a thermochemical equation. This tells us exactly how much heat is involved when the reaction occurs as written. For example, consider the combustion of methane. One mole of methane gas reacts with two moles of oxygen gas to produce one mole of carbon dioxide and two moles of water. If we write that this process has a change in enthalpy of negative eight hundred ninety kilojoules, we are telling you two things. First, the negative sign indicates an exothermic process, meaning heat is released into the surroundings. Second, the value tells us the amount of heat associated with that specific mole ratio.

In contrast, if a process is endothermic, it absorbs heat from the surroundings, and the enthalpy change will have a positive sign. Think of the decomposition of calcium carbonate into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. This reaction requires energy to break the strong ionic bonds, so it is endothermic. We would report a positive enthalpy change, indicating that the system is gaining potential energy in the form of chemical bonds. The sign of the enthalpy change is our primary shorthand for understanding whether a reaction feels hot or cold to the touch.

Because enthalpy is an extensive property, it depends on the amount of matter present. If you double the amount of reactants, you double the amount of heat released or absorbed. Furthermore, if you reverse a chemical reaction, the sign of the enthalpy change must also be reversed. If a forward reaction releases heat, the reverse reaction must absorb the exact same amount of heat.

Let us look at a quick calculation. Suppose we know that the combustion of one mole of methane releases eight hundred ninety kilojoules of energy. If we decide to burn two moles of methane, we simply multiply the enthalpy change by two. Two moles of methane reacting with four moles of oxygen gas will release one thousand seven hundred eighty kilojoules of energy. It is a direct, linear relationship. If you divide the reaction coefficients by two, you also divide the enthalpy change by two.

This scaling behavior is vital when we work in the laboratory. If you are calculating the heat released by burning five grams of methane, you first convert those grams to moles using the molar mass of methane, which is sixteen grams per mole. Five grams divided by sixteen grams per mole gives us zero point three one two five moles of methane. To find the total heat released, you multiply that number of moles by eight hundred ninety kilojoules per mole. The result is approximately two hundred seventy-eight kilojoules of heat released.

By treating enthalpy as a component of the chemical equation, we can predict the energy requirements or output of any process before we even step into the lab. We no longer have to rely solely on measuring temperature changes after the fact; we can use the stoichiometry of the reaction to account for energy as if it were just another product or reactant. This ability to quantify energy flow is the foundation for understanding how complex chemical systems behave. As we continue our study of thermodynamics, we will discover that these individual enthalpy changes can be combined and manipulated to determine the energy of reactions that are far too difficult or dangerous to measure directly in a simple calorimeter.

## Key ideas

- Enthalpy change represents the heat absorbed or released by a system during a process occurring at constant pressure.
- A negative enthalpy change signifies an exothermic process that releases heat, while a positive value signifies an endothermic process that absorbs heat.
- Thermochemical equations include the enthalpy change for the reaction, which is directly proportional to the amount of substance reacting.
- You can scale the enthalpy change of a reaction by multiplying or dividing it by the same factor used to adjust the stoichiometric coefficients of the chemical equation.
- Reversing a chemical reaction changes the sign of its enthalpy change.
