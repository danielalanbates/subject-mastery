# Distance and Midpoint

Imagine standing in a wide-open field, looking at two distant trees. If you wanted to run a straight cable from the base of the first tree to the base of the second, how long would that cable need to be? In our last lesson, we built the coordinate plane, laying a grid of numbers across flat space so every location gained an address of two coordinates. Today, we leave simple plotting behind and use that grid to measure the exact distance between any two locations, and to find the exact point resting right between them.

To measure diagonal distance on a grid, mathematicians rely on an ancient and powerful geometric truth known as the Pythagorean theorem. Picture a right triangle, which is a triangle where two of its sides meet at a square corner. The theorem states that if you square the lengths of the two shorter sides, called the legs, and add those two areas together, the result equals the square of the length of the longest side, which is the slanted side opposite the right corner, known as the hypotenuse. For instance, if your two legs measure three units and four units, three squared is nine and four squared is sixteen. Adding nine and sixteen gives twenty-five. The square root of twenty-five is five, so the hypotenuse measures five units.

We can turn this geometric rule into a direct formula for the coordinate plane. Suppose you have a first point and a second point, and you want the straight-line distance between them. Imagine drawing a right triangle whose hypotenuse connects these two points. The horizontal leg of this triangle is the difference between their horizontal coordinates, and the vertical leg is the difference between their vertical coordinates. By applying the Pythagorean theorem, the square of the distance equals the square of that horizontal difference plus the square of that vertical difference. To find the distance itself, we take the positive square root of that sum.

Let us walk through a worked example. Suppose our first point is located at coordinates one comma two, and our second point is located at coordinates four comma six. First, find the horizontal difference by subtracting the first horizontal coordinate from the second horizontal coordinate, which gives four minus one, or three. Squaring three gives nine. Next, find the vertical difference by subtracting the first vertical coordinate from the second vertical coordinate, which gives six minus two, or four. Squaring four gives sixteen. Now add those two squared differences together, adding nine and sixteen to get twenty-five. Finally, take the square root of twenty-five, which brings us to a total distance of five units.

Beyond measuring how far apart two points are, we often need to locate their exact midpoint. The midpoint is the balancing point, sitting squarely halfway along the segment connecting our two locations. To find the midpoint of any two coordinate points, you simply calculate the average of their horizontal coordinates and the average of their vertical coordinates. To average two numbers, you add them together and divide the sum by two.

Let us use our previous points, one comma two and four comma six, to find their midpoint. For the horizontal coordinate, add the two horizontal values together, one plus four, which equals five. Divide five by two to get two and a half. For the vertical coordinate, add the two vertical values together, two plus six, which equals eight. Divide eight by two to get four. Putting these together, the midpoint is located at the coordinates two and a half comma four. This point sits at an equal distance from both of our original locations, splitting the journey right down the middle. These tools of measurement prepare us to examine how changing one variable affects another, which we will explore when we investigate slope as a rate of change.

## Key ideas
- The Pythagorean theorem states that in a right triangle, the sum of the squares of the two legs equals the square of the hypotenuse.
- The distance formula calculates the straight-line length between two points on the coordinate plane by treating their coordinate differences as the legs of an imaginary right triangle.
- The midpoint of two coordinate points is found by taking the arithmetic mean of their horizontal coordinates and the arithmetic mean of their vertical coordinates.
