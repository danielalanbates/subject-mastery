# Transforming Graphs

Imagine you have a single recipe for bread, but by simply doubling the yeast, adding a handful of seeds, or baking it in a different pan, you create an entirely new loaf without rewriting the instructions from scratch. In our previous look at how graphs behave and how they rise and fall, we saw the fixed shapes of individual functions. Today, we discover that functions share this same flexibility. By making small tweaks to an algebraic rule, we can slide, stretch, compress, and flip its entire graph across the coordinate plane with complete predictability.

Let us start with four foundational shapes, the basic building blocks of our algebraic toolkit. First, consider the squaring function, where any input produces its square, giving us the graph of x squared. Spelled out in words, this graph is a smooth, symmetric bowl opening upward, resting its lowest point, the vertex, right at the origin, and climbing steeply as you move away from the center in either direction. Second is the square root function, taking only non-negative inputs and returning their principal square roots. Its graph starts with a vertical tangent at the origin, meaning it has an infinite slope right at the start, and then rises sharply while curving outward and upward. Third is the absolute value function, which strips away any negative sign. Its graph forms a sharp, perfectly straight corner shaped like the letter V, touching the origin at its point. Finally, we have the reciprocal function, one over x. Its graph consists of two separate curved branches, one hovering in the upper right quadrant and the other in the lower left, hugging the axes without ever touching them, separated by a vertical wall at zero where the function is undefined.

Every one of these four fundamental graphs can be transformed systematically through simple arithmetic changes to the input or the output. Let us see how addition and subtraction create shifts. If you take the function x squared and add three to the entire expression to get the quantity x squared plus three, every single output value becomes three units higher than it was before. In spoken terms, the entire bowl shape slides straight up by three units. Conversely, if you subtract two from the input inside the squaring operation, yielding the quantity x minus two squared, you are asking the function to produce the exact same outputs it used to, but only after x has advanced by two units. That means the entire graph shifts horizontally to the right by two units. Adding a positive number inside shifts the graph left, while subtracting shifts it right, which can feel counterintuitive until you remember that the graph has to chase the new input values.

Multiplication introduces stretches, compressions, and reflections. If you multiply our basic absolute value function by a factor of three outside, giving three times the absolute value of x, every vertical height is tripled. The graph's V shape grows much narrower and steeper, as if someone pulled the top ends upward. If you multiply by a fraction like one half instead, the graph compresses vertically, flattening out like a wide, lazy valley. What happens if we change the input directly by multiplying it inside? If you replace x with two x inside the function, the graph compresses horizontally by a factor of one half, squeezing inward toward the vertical axis. If the input multiplier is a fraction between zero and one, such as one half x inside, the graph stretches horizontally away from the vertical axis.

We can also reflect graphs across either axis. Multiplying the entire output by a negative one turns every positive height negative and every negative height positive, reflecting the graph across the horizontal axis. For our upward-opening bowl of x squared, introducing a negative sign outside creates a negative x squared, which flips the entire parabola vertically to face downward like a frown. Alternatively, replacing x with the negative of x inside the function reflects the graph across the vertical axis.

When applying multiple transformations, the order matters. Inside-the-function changes, such as horizontal shifts, horizontal stretches, and reflections across the vertical axis, are performed before outside-the-function changes. Let us trace a combined transformation step by step. Suppose we want to graph negative one times the square of the quantity x minus one, and then plus four. We begin with our basic graph of x squared, the upward bowl. First, replace x with the quantity x minus one, which shifts the vertex horizontally to the right to the coordinate one. Next, multiply the squared expression by negative one, which reflects the bowl downward, so it now opens toward the floor instead of the ceiling. Finally, add four to the entire expression, which shifts that newly inverted vertex upward by four units. The highest point of our transformed graph now sits at the coordinate point one comma four, pointing downward with the exact same curve as our starting bowl. By understanding how each number in an algebraic rule corresponds to a specific geometric shift, we can sketch complex functions instantly. As we move forward, we will see how these manipulation skills allow us to combine and compose separate functions into richer mathematical structures.

## Key ideas
- The basic graphs of x squared, the square root, the absolute value, and one over x serve as foundational shapes that can be systematically transformed.
- Adding or subtracting a number outside the function shifts its graph vertically, while adding or subtracting inside the function input shifts it horizontally.
- Multiplying a function by a constant stretches or compresses its graph vertically or horizontally, while multiplying by a negative sign reflects the graph across an axis.
- Inside-the-function changes are performed before outside-the-function changes when applying multiple transformations in sequence.
