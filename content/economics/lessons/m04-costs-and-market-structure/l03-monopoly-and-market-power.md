# Monopoly and Market Power

Imagine you are the only person on a scorching summer island with a single working well of fresh water. You do not have to compete with anyone else down the beach, and your thirsty neighbors have nowhere else to turn. Because you face no competition, you can set the price of a bucket of water wherever you like. That power to dictate terms rather than accept the going market price is the essence of monopoly. In our last lesson, we explored perfect competition, where countless small firms act as price takers, pushed by market forces to sell goods at marginal cost. Today, we step into the exact opposite world, examining how a single seller uses market power to restrict output and drive up prices.

A monopoly exists when a single firm is the sole producer of a good or service that has no close substitutes. Monopolies survive only behind barriers to entry: patents, control of a key resource, government licenses, network effects, or economies of scale so large that one firm can serve the whole market more cheaply than several could, a situation called natural monopoly. Unlike a competitive firm that sells every extra unit at a constant market price, a monopolist faces the entire downward-sloping market demand curve. This relationship creates a unique constraint and a powerful opportunity. If the monopolist wants to sell more output, it must lower its price not just on the final unit sold, but on every single unit it offers. Consequently, a monopolist finds that its marginal revenue, which is the addition to total revenue from selling one more unit, lies well below the price it charges.

To maximize profit, the monopolist follows the universal rule of production: it expands output up to the exact point where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. But because marginal revenue sits below the demand curve, this profit-maximizing quantity ends up lower than what a socially efficient market would produce. Once the firm identifies this restricted quantity, it looks up to the demand curve to find the highest price consumers are willing to pay for that amount. The result is a double shift away from competitive outcomes. The monopolist restricts output to create artificial scarcity, and then charges a price significantly higher than marginal cost, generating pure economic profits that persist over time because high barriers to entry keep rival firms out.

Let us trace a simple numerical example to see this mechanics in action. Suppose a pharmaceutical company holds the sole patent on a life-saving medication. Producing each pill has a steady marginal cost of ten dollars. Through market research, the firm discovers that if it produces one hundred pills, it can charge a price of fifty dollars each. If it expands production to one hundred one pills, the market price drops slightly, bringing its marginal revenue for that hundred-first pill down to twenty dollars. The firm will keep producing additional pills as long as that marginal revenue exceeds the ten-dollar marginal cost.

In this case, the marginal revenue of twenty dollars is still greater than the ten-dollar marginal cost, so the firm adds the pill. But once marginal revenue falls to match marginal cost, the firm stops. Suppose that equilibrium happens at one hundred ten pills, where the price consumers are willing to pay is forty dollars. The firm restricts its output to one hundred ten units and charges forty dollars, well above its ten-dollar marginal cost. The gap between that forty-dollar price and the ten-dollar marginal cost represents the firm's substantial markup, sustained by legal patents or exclusive control over a vital resource.

Economists evaluate this market structure through the lens of economic efficiency. In a competitive market, total surplus is maximized because price equals marginal cost, ensuring that society produces every unit where the value to consumers exceeds the cost of production. A monopoly destroys a portion of this potential gain. By restricting output below the competitive level, it creates what economists call deadweight loss, which represents mutually beneficial trades between buyers and sellers that simply never happen. Consumers pay more and receive less, transferring wealth directly from households to the monopolist's bottom line.

While pure monopolies are relatively rare outside of government-regulated utilities or patented innovations, firms frequently try to carve out miniature monopolies through product differentiation and branding. Understanding how a single dominant seller leverages its market power gives us the essential vocabulary for our next destination, monopolistic competition, where many firms each hold a little market power by selling products that are slightly different from their rivals’.

## Key ideas
- A monopoly occurs when a single firm supplies a good or service with no close substitutes, allowing it to act as a price maker rather than a price taker.
- Because a monopolist faces the entire market demand curve, its marginal revenue lies below its price, changing how it calculates profit maximization.
- Monopolists maximize profit by restricting output to the point where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and then charging a higher price determined by consumer demand.
- The combination of restricted output and elevated prices creates a deadweight loss for society, reducing total economic efficiency compared to competitive markets.
