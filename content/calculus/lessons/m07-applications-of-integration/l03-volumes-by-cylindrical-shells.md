# Volumes by Cylindrical Shells

Imagine you are peeling a fresh orange, removing the skin in a series of thin, concentric rings or tubes, one nested inside another. If you took all those nested cylindrical tubes and laid them side by side, their total volume would equal the volume of the entire orange. In our last lesson, we built solids of revolution by slicing them perpendicular to the axis of rotation, creating solid disks or washers. But sometimes, slicing a region that way leads to integrals that are nearly impossible to evaluate. Today, we will explore an alternative approach called the shell method, which lets us integrate parallel to the axis of rotation by summing up the volumes of hollow cylindrical shells.

Before we dive into the mechanics, it is worth pausing to appreciate the historical landscape of calculus. The creation of these powerful integration tools sparked a famously bitter priority dispute between Sir Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in the late seventeenth century. Both men developed the foundations of calculus independently, with Newton introducing fluxions and dot notation, while Leibniz introduced the integral sign and the differential notation d x that we still rely on today. Despite their fierce rivalry, their combined insights gave us the methods we use now to calculate volumes like the ones we are studying.

To understand why we need this new tool, consider the region bounded by the curve where y equals three x squared minus x cubed, and the x axis, rotated around the vertical y axis. If we try to use the washer method, we must integrate with respect to y. That requires solving our equation for x in terms of y, a task that quickly becomes algebraically hopeless because of the cubic term. The shell method bypasses this hurdle by keeping our integration with respect to x, matching the natural orientation of our function.

Picture a single vertical strip of width d x, located at a distance x from the vertical y axis, stretching up to a height equal to our function value, f of x. When we rotate this thin vertical strip around the y axis, it sweeps out a hollow cylinder, or shell. Think of it as a soup can with the top and bottom removed. To find the volume of this single cylindrical shell, we multiply three geometric components together: the radius of the shell, the height of the shell, and the thickness of the shell, multiplied by two pi. The radius is simply our distance x from the axis of rotation. The height is given by our function f of x. The thickness is d x.

Therefore, the infinitesimal volume d v of one shell equals two pi times x times f of x, all multiplied by d x. To find the total volume of the solid generated by rotating the entire region from x equals zero to x equals three around the y axis, we accumulate all these shell volumes using a definite integral. We integrate from our left boundary to our right boundary.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Let our region be bounded above by the curve where y equals two x minus x squared, and below by the x axis, on the interval from x equals zero to x equals two. We will rotate this region around the vertical y axis. First, we identify our radius, which is x, and our height, which is the quantity two x minus x squared.

We set up our definite integral for the volume. It equals two pi times the integral from zero to two of x multiplied by the quantity two x minus x squared, all with respect to x. Before integrating, we expand the integrand. Multiplying x into our height expression gives us two x squared minus x cubed.

Now we find the antiderivative term by term. The antiderivative of two x squared is two thirds x cubed. The antiderivative of x cubed is one fourth x fourth. So our antiderivative expression is two thirds x cubed minus one fourth x fourth. We evaluate this antiderivative from our lower limit of zero to our upper limit of two.

Substituting two into our antiderivative, two cubed is eight, so two thirds multiplied by eight gives sixteen thirds. Two to the fourth power is sixteen, and taking one fourth of that sixteen gives four. Subtracting four from sixteen thirds gives sixteen thirds minus twelve thirds, which evaluates to four thirds. Substituting our lower limit of zero gives zero for all terms. Finally, we multiply our result by two pi. Two pi multiplied by four thirds gives eight pi over three.

To be completely rigorous, we must check our antiderivative via differentiation, as the foundational rules of calculus demand. If we take our antiderivative expression, two thirds x cubed minus one fourth x fourth, and differentiate it with respect to x, we apply the power rule to each term. The derivative of two thirds x cubed is two x squared. The derivative of one fourth x fourth is x cubed. Putting those together gives two x squared minus x cubed, which matches our original expanded integrand inside the integral. This successful differentiation confirms that our antiderivative is correct.

## Key ideas
- The shell method calculates volumes of revolution by slicing the region into thin strips parallel to the axis of rotation, each of which sweeps out a hollow cylindrical shell.
- The volume of a single cylindrical shell is given by two pi multiplied by the radius, the height, and the infinitesimal thickness.
- The historical development of these methods was marked by a bitter priority dispute between Newton and Leibniz, who introduced distinct notations still used today.
- Differentiating an antiderivative allows us to formally check our work and confirm it matches the original integrand.
