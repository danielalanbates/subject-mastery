# Financial Markets: Stocks, Bonds and Saving

Imagine you have saved one thousand dollars from your summer job. You could stuff that cash under your mattress, but it would earn zero interest and slowly lose its purchasing power to inflation. Instead, you decide to put that money to work. How does your hard-earned cash actually travel from your savings account into the hands of a local business wanting to expand, or a government wanting to build a bridge? The journey of savings into investment forms the beating heart of the financial system, linking everyday households with borrowers through a sophisticated network of banks, bonds, and stocks.

In our last lesson, we saw how banks act as intermediaries by taking deposits and creating money through loans. But banks are just one part of a much broader financial market. When households save, they are supplying financial capital. When firms and governments need funds to build factories, invent technologies, or pave highways, they are demanding financial capital. Financial markets are the mechanisms that bring these savers and borrowers together.

The most familiar bridge between savers and borrowers is the commercial bank. When you deposit your one thousand dollars into a savings account, the bank pays you a modest interest rate. The bank then bundles your deposit with money from other savers and lends it out to a homebuyer or a small business at a higher interest rate. The difference between what the bank pays you and what it charges the borrower covers its operating costs and generates profit. Through this process, your dormant savings immediately start fueling economic activity.

Not all borrowing happens through banks, however. Large corporations and governments often bypass banks entirely by issuing bonds. A bond is essentially an IOU, a formal promise to repay a borrowed sum of money at a specific date in the future, while making regular interest payments along the way. Imagine the city government wants to build a new public library costing ten million dollars. It might issue ten thousand bonds priced at one thousand dollars each. You buy one of these bonds, effectively becoming the lender to the city. In return, the city promises to pay you five percent interest every year for ten years, and then return your original one thousand dollars at the end.

Bonds have a fascinating relationship with interest rates that catches many beginners off guard. Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. Suppose you hold that city bond paying fifty dollars a year, which is five percent on your one thousand dollar investment. Suddenly, overall market interest rates rise, and newly issued bonds are now paying seven percent. If you try to sell your existing bond to someone else, no one will pay you the full one thousand dollars for it, because they can buy a new bond that yields a higher payout. To convince someone to buy your five percent bond, you would have to drop its price. Therefore, when market interest rates go up, existing bond prices go down.

For those who want a more active stake in corporate growth rather than a steady loan repayment, there are stocks. A share of stock represents fractional ownership in a corporation. When you buy a share of stock in an automobile manufacturer, you are not lending the company money; you are buying a tiny piece of the company itself. If the company invents a popular electric vehicle and its profits soar, the value of your share rises, and the company might pay you a portion of those profits, known as a dividend. If the company stumbles, your share price can fall.

This brings us to the fundamental trade-off of the financial world: the risk-return tradeoff. Every financial choice involves balancing how much risk you are willing to take against the return you expect to receive. Putting your money in a federally insured savings account carries almost zero risk, but it offers a very low return. Buying corporate bonds involves higher risk because the company could theoretically go bankrupt, so bonds generally offer higher returns than bank accounts. Owning individual stocks is riskier still, because stock prices swing wildly day to day, but over the long run, stocks have historically delivered higher average returns than bonds or savings accounts.

Interest rates tie all of these pieces together. They act as the price of money, signaling to savers whether it is worth parting with their cash today, and signaling to borrowers whether it is affordable to invest in tomorrow. As we turn our attention next to central banks and the Federal Reserve, we will see how monetary authorities pull the levers that influence these very interest rates across the entire economy.

## Key ideas

- Financial markets channel savings from households to borrowers through banks, bonds, and stocks.
- A bond is a formal IOU issued by governments or corporations, and existing bond prices move inversely to market interest rates.
- A share of stock represents partial ownership in a corporation, offering potential dividends and capital gains in exchange for higher volatility.
- The fundamental rule of finance links risk and return, meaning that safer assets offer lower yields while riskier investments demand higher potential returns.
