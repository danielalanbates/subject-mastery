# Equations of Constant Acceleration

Imagine standing by a runway as a heavy passenger jet begins its takeoff roll. The engines roar, and the aircraft surges forward, building up speed with a steady, relentless push. In our last lesson, we defined acceleration as the rate at which velocity changes over time. When that acceleration remains completely constant, physics gives us a powerful set of tools to predict the future. We can look at how far the jet will travel down the runway and exactly how fast it will be moving the moment its wheels leave the ground, without having to measure every single second in between.

To describe motion along a straight line, we must first establish a sign convention. We pick one direction along the line as positive and the opposite direction as negative. If a car moves forward to the right, we might define right as positive. If it moves to the left, its velocity is negative. An acceleration pointing in the negative direction will reduce a positive velocity or increase a negative velocity. Keeping consistent track of positive and negative signs is essential whenever you solve a kinematic problem.

These constant acceleration formulas are derived directly from the fundamental definitions of velocity and acceleration. They connect five key quantities: displacement, initial velocity, final velocity, constant acceleration, and elapsed time. Because these equations are algebraic relationships, you can rearrange them to solve for any single variable. Each of the four main equations omits one of the five quantities. In a real physical scenario, you usually know three values and need to find a fourth. You simply select the equation that leaves out the value you do not need, and solve directly. In cases where you must find time when initial velocity is not zero, rearranging the displacement equation gives a quadratic equation that you can solve with the quadratic formula.

The first equation builds directly on the definition of acceleration. It states that the final velocity of an object equals its initial velocity plus the product of its acceleration and the elapsed time. If a car starts from rest and accelerates forward at two meters per second squared for five seconds, you can picture its speed climbing step by step. Multiplying that acceleration of two meters per second squared by five seconds gives a speed increase of ten meters per second. Because the car started from rest, its final velocity is ten meters per second.

Our second equation lets us find displacement when time and velocities are known. It tells us that the displacement of an object equals its average velocity multiplied by the elapsed time. When acceleration is constant, the average velocity is simply the exact mathematical midpoint between the initial velocity and the final velocity. Picture a runner crossing a stretch of track. If her starting speed was two meters per second and her ending speed was eight meters per second, her average speed during that stretch was five meters per second. Multiply that average of five meters per second by the time she spent running, and you obtain her total displacement.

The third equation combines the first two to calculate displacement from acceleration and time, bypassing the final velocity entirely. This relationship states that displacement equals initial velocity multiplied by time, plus one-half of the acceleration multiplied by the square of the time. Imagine a bicycle rolling down a gentle slope from a complete stop. Because the initial velocity is zero, the initial velocity term vanishes, leaving us with half of the acceleration times time squared. If the acceleration is two meters per second squared and three seconds pass, three squared is nine. Multiply nine by two meters per second squared to get eighteen, and take half to find that the bicycle has traveled nine meters.

The fourth and final equation connects velocities and displacement without using time at all. It states that the final velocity squared equals the initial velocity squared plus two times the acceleration times the displacement. This is exceptionally useful when you are calculating how much distance is required to stop a vehicle or how far a push will carry a sliding box. If you know the initial speed, the acceleration, and the distance traveled, this equation reveals the final speed instantly without needing a stopwatch.

As you apply these tools, remember that they assume acceleration never wavers. They are idealized models for smooth, predictable motion along straight paths. When an object drops through the air and encounters the pull of gravity, these exact same constant acceleration formulas will guide us once again, provided we assign the proper sign convention to the downward pull of our planet.

## Key ideas
- A sign convention establishes which direction along a straight line is positive and which is negative, ensuring consistent algebraic signs for displacement, velocity, and acceleration.
- The four kinematic equations are derived from the definitions of velocity and acceleration and can be rearranged to solve for any single unknown variable, sometimes requiring a quadratic equation for time.
- Each kinematic equation connects a different combination of five motion variables, allowing you to select the formula that omits the unknown quantity you do not need.
- When acceleration is constant, average velocity equals the exact mathematical midpoint between initial velocity and final velocity.
- These equations serve as idealized models for straight-line motion at steady rates of change, establishing the foundation for studying free fall and gravity.
