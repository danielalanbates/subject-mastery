# Half-Life and Radiometric Dating

Imagine you are walking through an old forest and you find a piece of wood buried deep in the soil. You wonder how long ago this tree fell. To find out, you cannot simply look at it; you need a clock that started ticking the moment the tree died. In chemistry, we have such a clock, powered by the steady, predictable decay of unstable atomic nuclei. This process is known as radioactive decay, and it provides the foundation for determining the age of ancient artifacts and geological formations.

In our previous lesson, we explored why some nuclei are unstable and how they emit particles to reach a more stable state. This decay is a random event for any single atom, but for a large collection of atoms, it follows a precise mathematical pattern called first-order kinetics. We describe the speed of this process using the term half-life. The half-life is the amount of time it takes for exactly one half of a radioactive sample to decay into a different, stable element. Because this rate remains constant regardless of temperature or pressure, it acts as a reliable natural stopwatch.

To calculate the amount of material remaining, we use an exponential decay relationship. If we let the initial amount of a substance be represented by the letter N sub zero, the amount remaining after a certain time is equal to N sub zero multiplied by one half raised to the power of the elapsed time divided by the half-life. Alternatively, we use the decay constant, which is a value represented by the Greek letter lambda. This constant is equal to the natural logarithm of two divided by the half-life. The amount remaining is then equal to the initial amount multiplied by the mathematical constant e raised to the power of negative lambda multiplied by time.

Let us walk through a calculation. Suppose you start with a sample containing 1,000 grams of a radioactive isotope with a half-life of 10 years. After 10 years, which is one half-life, you have 500 grams. After 20 years, or two half-lives, you have 250 grams. If you want to know the amount remaining after 15 years, you use the decay constant. First, calculate lambda by dividing the natural logarithm of two, which is approximately zero point six nine three, by 10 years, giving you a decay constant of zero point zero six nine three per year. Then, multiply that by 15 years to get an exponent of negative one point zero four. Raising the base e to the power of negative one point zero four gives approximately zero point three five three. Multiplying this by your initial 1,000 grams leaves you with approximately 353 grams remaining.

Radiometric dating uses this principle to peer into the past. Carbon dating is a common example. Living organisms constantly exchange carbon with the environment, maintaining a specific ratio of radioactive carbon 14 to stable carbon 12. When an organism dies, it stops taking in new carbon. The carbon 14 already in its tissues begins to decay into nitrogen 14 at a fixed rate, with a half-life of about 5,730 years. By measuring the ratio of carbon 14 to carbon 12, scientists calculate how long ago the organism died. However, atmospheric carbon 14 levels have fluctuated over time due to solar activity and climate changes. Therefore, scientists must calibrate these dates using independent records like tree-ring dendrochronology or reservoir corrections to ensure accuracy.

For much older objects, such as rocks that are millions or billions of years old, we use isotopes with longer half-lives, such as uranium decaying into lead. These methods rely on the assumption that the sample remained a closed system, meaning no parent or daughter isotopes leaked in or out. To test this, scientists use the isochron method. By measuring multiple samples from the same geological source, they can mathematically determine the initial amount of the daughter isotope present at the time of formation, which verifies if the system truly remained closed.

Furthermore, we must consider the closure temperature. Different mineral systems only begin to retain their daughter isotopes once the rock cools below a certain temperature. If a rock is reheated, the isotopes may diffuse out, effectively resetting the clock. This means a radiometric date often records the last time a rock cooled below its specific closure temperature, rather than the original formation of the mineral. By understanding these variables, we turn the chemistry of the nucleus into a historical record, reconstructing the timeline of life on Earth and the history of the planet itself.

## Key ideas

- The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the constant time interval required for half of the atoms in a sample to undergo decay.
- Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics, where the amount of substance remaining is calculated using an exponential decay function based on the decay constant.
- Radiometric dating requires calibration against independent records because the concentration of isotopes like carbon 14 in the atmosphere has varied throughout history.
- The isochron method and the concept of closure temperature allow scientists to test for closed-system integrity and interpret the specific geological events recorded by isotopic clocks.
