# Polar Area and Calculus

Imagine slicing a pie not with straight cuts from the center to the crust, but by sweeping out tiny wedge-shaped sectors as you rotate around a central point. When we studied areas under curves in rectangular coordinates, we built our sums out of vertical rectangles. But when a shape loops around the origin, like a flower petal or a spiral, vertical rectangles become clumsy. In our last lesson, we mapped polar curves by plotting radius values against angles of rotation. Today, we turn those sweeping angles into a tool for measurement, using the definite integral to find the exact area enclosed by polar curves.

To see how this works, let us think about the geometry of a single circular sector. If you have a circle of radius r, and you open a wedge by an angle theta, the area of that slice is one half of the radius squared times the angle theta. Now imagine a polar curve where the radius is not constant, but instead changes as a function of the angle theta. We want to find the area of the region swept out by the radius vector as theta moves from a starting angle b to an ending angle c. We can approximate this curved region by dividing the total angle interval into many tiny subintervals. Over each small change in angle, the radius stays roughly constant, so the region looks like a stack of narrow circular sectors.

If we add up the areas of all these tiny sectors, we get a Riemann sum. Each individual sector has an area equal to one half of the radius function evaluated at a sample angle, all squared, multiplied by the tiny change in angle. As we let the number of sectors grow infinitely large and the width of each angle change shrink to zero, that Riemann sum turns into a definite integral. The formula for the area of a polar region is the integral from the angle b to the angle c of one half of the radius function squared, with respect to theta.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Suppose we want to find the area enclosed by one loop of the four petaled rose given by the radius equals the cosine of two theta. First, we need to figure out the bounds of integration by finding where the radius equals zero, which marks the start and end of a single petal. The cosine of two theta equals zero when the angle two theta equals negative pi over two or positive pi over two, as well as at other odd multiples of pi over two. Solving for theta in the interval containing zero, we find that the angle two theta runs from negative pi over two to positive pi over two, meaning theta runs from negative pi over four to positive pi over four.

Now we set up our integral. We integrate from negative pi over four to positive pi over four of one half of the quantity cosine of two theta, all squared, with respect to theta. To evaluate this, we factor out the one half and use a trigonometric half angle identity to rewrite the squared cosine as the quantity one plus the cosine of four theta, all divided by two. We pull out another factor of one half, leaving a quarter outside the integral. The antiderivative of one with respect to theta is theta, and the antiderivative of the cosine of four theta is one fourth of the sine of four theta. Combining these, our antiderivative is one fourth of the quantity theta plus one fourth of the sine of four theta.

To check this antiderivative, we differentiate it with respect to theta. The derivative of one fourth theta is one fourth, and the derivative of one sixteenth of the sine of four theta is one fourth of the cosine of four theta, using the chain rule. Factoring out the one fourth leaves one fourth of the quantity one plus the cosine of four theta, which matches our integrand after applying the half angle identity.

Evaluating this antiderivative at the upper bound of pi over four and subtracting the value at the lower bound of negative pi over four, we find that the upper bound yields pi over four plus zero, because the sine of pi is zero. The lower bound yields negative pi over four minus zero. Subtracting the lower bound from the upper bound gives pi over four plus pi over four, which equals pi over two. Multiplying by the outer factor of one fourth, our final area is pi over eight.

Sometimes, finding the area where two polar curves intersect requires extra care. Just as we found areas between Cartesian graphs by subtracting lower functions from upper functions, we find areas between polar curves by integrating the outer radius squared minus the inner radius squared, all multiplied by one half. For instance, to find the area inside the circle given by the radius equals one and outside the cardioid given by the radius equals the quantity one minus the cosine of theta, we first set the two radius equations equal to each other to find their intersection points. One equals one minus the cosine of theta, which means the cosine of theta equals zero, so theta equals pi over two and three pi over two. We must also remember that the origin can be reached by different curves at different angles, so we inspect each curve independently at the pole. We then integrate one half of the difference between the square of the circle radius and the square of the cardioid radius from negative pi over two to positive pi over two. As we move forward into our final module, we will see how these tools of integration and geometry open the door to the vast landscapes of higher mathematics.

## Key ideas
- Polar area is calculated by summing the areas of infinitely many thin circular sectors swept out from the origin.
- The area enclosed by a polar curve is given by the definite integral from the starting angle to the ending angle of one half of the radius function squared, with respect to theta.
- Finding the correct limits of integration requires determining where the radius equals zero or where two polar curves intersect, noting that the origin can be reached at different angles.
- Areas between two polar curves are found by integrating one half of the difference between the outer radius squared and the inner radius squared.
