# The Nature and Mission of the Church

Imagine walking into a massive ancient stone cathedral in Europe, or a storefront gathering in a bustling modern city with electric guitars and folding chairs. Both groups of people would look around, open the same scriptures, and call themselves by the exact same ancient word: church. Yet behind that single word lies a vast, fascinating history of theological debate over how a community of believers should be organized, who should lead them, and what on earth they are supposed to be doing in the world. In our last lesson, we explored how the Holy Spirit breathes life into individual believers. Today, we step outward to examine how those individuals are woven together into a corporate body: the nature and mission of the church.

The word most frequently translated as church in the New Testament is the Greek term ekklesia, which simply means an assembly or a calling out. From its earliest days in the book of Acts, the church was never meant to be a mere club or a private gathering for self-improvement. It was designed to be a living signpost of God's kingdom. Christian theology describes the church using rich metaphors drawn from scripture. It is called the body of Christ, where every member has a vital function, and the temple of the Spirit, built of living stones. Across the centuries, theologians have also distinguished between the visible church—the messy, earthly collection of all professing Christians with both saints and sinners—and the invisible church, which is God's true spiritual community known fully only to Him.

To understand how this community functions day-to-day, we must look at how different Christian traditions organize their leadership, a field known as ecclesiology or church government. There are three primary models that have shaped Christian history. The first is the episcopal model, a word derived from the Greek term episkopos, meaning overseer or bishop. In this hierarchical structure, authority flows downward from bishops to priests or pastors and then to the congregation. This model is preserved in the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Anglican traditions, where apostolic succession is understood to be maintained through an unbroken line of bishops ordained down through the centuries.

The second model is the presbyterian model, coming from the Greek word presbuteros, which means elder. In this system, authority rests with a representative council of elected elders. This council is often called a session or a presbytery. It connects local congregations to a broader regional and national governing body. This form of government, championed during the Protestant Reformation by figures like John Calvin, emphasizes communal leadership and shared accountability among local churches rather than rule by a single bishop.

The third model is the congregational model, which stresses the radical autonomy of the local church. In this tradition, practiced by Baptists, Congregationalists, and many independent evangelical churches, the ultimate authority on earth rests with the local membership under the lordship of Christ. Congregations vote on their own budgets, call their own pastors, and manage their own affairs, often cooperating voluntarily with other churches for missions and charity. Pentecostal and charismatic communities often blend elements of these models with a strong emphasis on the immediate, dynamic leadership of the Holy Spirit through spiritual gifts.

Beyond internal governance, what is the fundamental mission of the church? Christian theology generally frames this mission around three great responsibilities. First is worship upward, offering praise, prayer, and the administration of sacred rites to God. Second is nurture inward, building up believers in faith, holiness, and love so they grow into maturity. Third is witness outward, proclaiming the good news of Jesus Christ in word and deed. This outward mission includes both evangelism, sharing the message of salvation, and cultural engagement, working for justice, mercy, and peace in a broken world. As the early Christians discovered, the church is not just an institution to be maintained, but a movement sent on a divine rescue mission. As we move to our next lesson, we will explore the tangible ways this community enacts its faith through the sacraments and ordinances observed across diverse traditions.

## Key ideas
- The Greek word ekklesia means an assembly, defining the church not as a private club but as a corporate body called by God.
- The episcopal model of church government relies on a hierarchical structure of bishops, preserved in Catholic, Orthodox, and Anglican traditions.
- The presbyterian model distributes leadership among councils of elected elders, emphasizing shared accountability and regional connection.
- The congregational model stresses the autonomy of the local church where the final earthly authority rests with the local membership.
- The mission of the church encompasses upward worship, inward spiritual nurture, and outward witness through evangelism and acts of mercy.
