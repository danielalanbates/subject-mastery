# Integration by Substitution

Imagine you are trying to measure the flow rate of a river, but the water runs through a winding, narrow pipe before it reaches your gauge. You cannot measure the velocity directly where it matters most, so you have to relate what happens inside the pipe to what emerges at the end. In calculus, when we want to find the integral of a composite function, we face a similar puzzle. We need a way to look inside the compound structure. In our last lesson, we used the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to evaluate definite integrals using antiderivatives. Today, we tackle integration by substitution, which is nothing less than the chain rule read backward.

This powerful technique rests on the monumental work of seventeenth-century mathematics, though it emerged from a period of fierce and bitter rivalry. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed the core ideas of calculus independently, and their priority dispute over who deserved the credit for inventing the subject divided the mathematical world for generations. Each also left us enduring marks on our page: Leibniz introduced the differential notation we use today, writing d x to represent an infinitesimal change and using the elongated letter s for the integral sign, while Newton favored dot notation for rates of change. Leibniz notation makes substitution wonderfully intuitive because the differentials behave like algebraic fractions during our calculations.

Think back to how the chain rule works when we take a derivative. If we have a function composed of an inner function and an outer function, the derivative multiplies the derivative of the inner function by the derivative of the outer function evaluated at the inner function. Integration by substitution reverses this choreography. When we encounter an integral where part of the integrand is the exact derivative of another part, we can make a substitution that simplifies the whole expression into something we already know how to integrate.

Let us watch this work with a concrete example of an indefinite integral. Suppose we want to find the antiderivative of two x multiplied by the quantity x squared plus three, to the fourth power, with respect to x. Notice the structure of this integrand. We have an inner function, which is x squared plus three. Its derivative is two x, and look at that, two x is sitting right there outside the parenthesis as a multiplier. This is the hallmark of a substitution problem.

To clear away the complexity, we choose a new variable to represent the inner function. Let us name our new variable u, so that u equals x squared plus three. Now, we must also determine how the differential d x relates to a new differential d u. We take the derivative of u with respect to x, which gives us two x. Writing this in differential form, we find that d u equals two x times d x.

Look back at our original integral. The two x and the d x together form the exact expression for d u. Our complicated integral transforms instantly. It becomes the integral of u to the fourth power, with respect to u. This new integral is remarkably straightforward. By the power rule for integration, the antiderivative of u to the fourth power is one fifth times u to the fifth power, plus our constant of integration.

We are not quite finished with an indefinite integral until we substitute our original expression back into the result. Replacing u with the quantity x squared plus three, our final antiderivative is one fifth times the quantity x squared plus three, to the fifth power, plus the constant of integration. We should always check our work by differentiating our result. If we take the derivative of our answer using the chain rule, the five comes down and cancels with the one fifth, leaving the quantity x squared plus three to the fourth power, multiplied by the derivative of the inside, which is two x. The derivative matches our original integrand perfectly, confirming our substitution was correct.

Substitution applies just as powerfully to definite integrals, where we must account for the boundaries of integration. Suppose we want to evaluate the definite integral from zero to one of two x multiplied by the quantity x squared plus three, to the fourth power, with respect to x. We use the exact same substitution, setting u equal to x squared plus three, which gives us d u equals two x times d x. But now, we must change our limits of integration to match our new variable u.

We take our lower limit of x equals zero and substitute it into our substitution equation, finding that our new lower limit for u is zero squared plus three, which is three. Then we take our upper limit of x equals one and substitute it in, finding that our new upper limit for u is one squared plus three, which is four. Our entire definite integral transforms into the integral from three to four of u to the fourth power, with respect to u. We evaluate this using our antiderivative, one fifth times u to the fifth power. We do not need to substitute x back in because we already updated our limits. We evaluate one fifth times u to the fifth power from three to four. At the upper limit, we have one fifth times four to the fifth power, which is one fifth times one thousand twenty-four. At the lower limit, we have one fifth times three to the fifth power, which is one fifth times 243. Subtracting the lower value from the upper value gives us one fifth times seven hundred eighty-one, which equals seven hundred eighty-one fifths.

This method unlocks a vast universe of integrals that would otherwise remain opaque, preparing us smoothly to tackle exponential and logarithmic integrals next.

## Key ideas
- Integration by substitution reverses the chain rule to help us find antiderivatives of composite functions.
- Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz introduced the differential notation that makes substitution intuitive, while Isaac Newton developed parallel foundational ideas during their historic priority dispute.
- For indefinite integrals, we substitute the inner function back into our final result, while definite integrals require us to calculate new limits of integration for our substituted variable.
- Introducing a temporary variable transforms a complicated composite integral into a standard form that is easy to evaluate.
