# Personality Assessment

Imagine you are applying for a high-stakes job. As part of the screening process, you are asked to sit at a computer and answer three hundred questions about your habits, your worries, and your preferences. Later that same afternoon, a different company asks you to look at a series of abstract inkblots and describe what you see. These two experiences represent the divide in personality assessment: the difference between objective self-reporting and subjective interpretation. Building on our look at how biology and culture influence our unique traits, we must now ask how psychologists measure those traits in a way that is both reliable and valid.

The most common way psychologists assess personality today is through self-report inventories. These are standardized questionnaires where you rate yourself on various statements. The Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory, or MMPI, is a classic example. It was designed to identify psychological difficulties by comparing a person's answers to those of people with known clinical diagnoses. Because it is highly structured and scored against established norms, it offers a high degree of reliability, meaning it produces consistent results over time. Crucially, this inventory includes validity scales designed to detect inconsistent or dishonest responding. These scales help clinicians determine if a person is answering randomly or trying to present themselves in an overly favorable light. This is vital because self-report measures are often susceptible to social desirability bias, where individuals might answer in a way they think makes them look better rather than answering truthfully.

Another popular approach is the Big Five questionnaire. Unlike clinical tests, this tool measures five broad dimensions of personality: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism. This model was derived from factor analysis, a statistical method that identifies clusters of related language descriptors people use to describe themselves and others. Because these traits are stable across many cultures, the Big Five has become the gold standard for personality research. It is generally high in validity, meaning it accurately predicts real-world outcomes. For instance, people who score high in conscientiousness are statistically more likely to perform well at work and maintain better physical health. The evidence suggests that these traits are not just labels, but meaningful descriptions of how people navigate their lives.

However, not all personality tests are built on such solid scientific ground. You have likely encountered popular type-based tests, such as the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator. These tests attempt to sort people into distinct categories. While they are often used in casual settings, they lack empirical support for their dichotomous type categories. They fail to predict important life outcomes, and they suffer from poor reliability. If you take such a test today and again in a month, there is a high chance you will be assigned a different personality type. They categorize people into rigid boxes, ignoring the fact that most human traits exist on a spectrum.

Then there is the other end of the spectrum: projective tests. These tools, most famously the Rorschach inkblot test, ask you to interpret vague, ambiguous images. The theory behind them is that your subconscious mind will project your hidden desires or conflicts onto the stimulus. While they have a rich history in the psychodynamic tradition, their scientific standing is weak. Because the interpretation of your answers depends heavily on the clinician’s subjective judgment, these tests suffer from poor reliability. Furthermore, they demonstrate limited incremental validity, meaning they rarely provide useful information that cannot be gathered more accurately through standard self-report measures. As a result, many psychologists view projective tests as outdated for diagnostic purposes, although defenders point to newer Rorschach scoring systems that show better reliability and some validity for a few scores, such as signs of disordered thinking.

To be useful, a personality assessment must be more than just interesting; it must be backed by data. A test is only as good as its ability to provide consistent, verifiable information that correlates with how a person actually acts in the real world. We prefer objective inventories precisely because they minimize the bias of the observer. When we rely on scientific measurement rather than intuition or interpretation, we gain a much clearer picture of what makes people tick.

## Key ideas

- Self-report inventories like the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory and the Big Five are considered the most reliable tools for measuring personality because they rely on standardized data and include validity scales to detect bias.
- The Big Five model is derived from factor analysis of language and shows robust cross-cultural replication, making it a gold standard for predicting real-world outcomes.
- Popular type-based tests like the Myers-Briggs lack empirical support for their categories and fail to predict important life outcomes or provide consistent results over time.
- Projective tests like the Rorschach are scientifically weak because they rely on subjective interpretation and offer little value beyond what is already captured by objective measures.
