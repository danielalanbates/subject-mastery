# Nonconservative Forces and Power

Imagine you slide a heavy wooden crate across a rough floor and let it come to a stop. In our last lesson, we saw how mechanical energy stays conserved inside an isolated system when only conservative forces like gravity are at play. Yet that crate does not slide forever, and its kinetic energy vanishes entirely. Where does that energy go? It does not simply vanish from the universe. Instead, friction works behind the scenes to transform organized mechanical motion into random thermal motion at the microscopic level of wood fibers and floorboards.

Forces whose work depends on the path taken are known as nonconservative forces, and they can drain mechanical energy from a system. Friction and air resistance are classic examples. When a nonconservative force acts on an object, the total mechanical energy of that object is no longer constant. Instead, the final mechanical energy minus the initial mechanical energy equals the work done by the nonconservative forces. That lost mechanical energy does not disappear; it transforms into an increase in the internal thermal energy of the objects in contact, which connects directly to the first law of thermodynamics.

To see how this works in practice, suppose you push a ten kilogram box across a horizontal floor over a distance of five meters. The kinetic friction force opposing your push is a constant twenty newtons. How much mechanical energy is converted into heat by friction? The work done by friction equals the friction force multiplied by the distance, which gives twenty newtons times five meters, resulting in one hundred joules. Because friction acts in the direction opposite to the motion, it does negative work, meaning the system loses one hundred joules of mechanical energy to thermal energy.

Now, consider a different question about that same movement. How fast did you get the job done? Physics uses the term power to describe the rate at which work is performed or energy is transferred. Power is a scalar quantity, meaning it has a magnitude but no direction, and its sign indicates whether energy is being added to a system or removed from it. Power is measured in watts, where one watt equals one joule of energy transferred per second.

We can express power in two main ways depending on how the energy transfer unfolds over time. Average power describes the total work done divided by the total time interval required for that work. Instantaneous power describes the limit of that average power as the time interval shrinks toward zero, which is also equal to the force multiplied by the velocity of the object at that exact moment. If you pushed that box across the five meter floor in two seconds, what was your average power output? First, you performed one hundred joules of work to overcome friction. Dividing that work by the time of two seconds yields fifty joules per second, or fifty watts.

Let us walk through another quick example to cement the idea of power. Suppose an electric motor hoists a five hundred kilogram elevator straight upward at a steady speed through a height of ten meters in five seconds. First, we find the weight of the elevator by multiplying its mass of five hundred kilograms by the acceleration due to gravity of nine point eight meters per second squared, giving four thousand nine hundred newtons. The work required to lift it is that force multiplied by the height of ten meters, totaling forty-nine thousand joules.

To find the power of the motor, we divide that total work of forty-nine thousand joules by the time of five seconds. Doing the arithmetic gives nine thousand eight hundred watts, or nine point eight kilowatts. As a quick sense check, nine thousand eight hundred watts is roughly equivalent to thirteen horsepower, a reasonable output for a small commercial elevator lifting a heavy load. We could reach the exact same power result by multiplying the upward force of four thousand nine hundred newtons by the steady upward velocity of two meters per second, illustrating how force and velocity combine directly to determine power.

Real systems constantly juggle conservative gravity and nonconservative friction. Recognizing how nonconservative forces bleed energy away as heat, and how power tracks the speed of energy transfer, allows engineers to design efficient machinery and helps us understand why perpetual motion machines are impossible. As we turn our focus next toward the realm of biology, we will see how living organisms harness these exact principles of energy transfer to survive and move.

## Key ideas
- Nonconservative forces, such as friction and air resistance, remove mechanical energy from a system and convert it into internal thermal energy, linking mechanical loss to the first law of thermodynamics.
- The change in a system's total mechanical energy equals the work done by all nonconservative forces acting upon it.
- Power is a scalar quantity that measures the rate of energy transfer, with its sign indicating whether energy is added to or removed from a system.
- Power can be calculated as average power over a time interval or as instantaneous power by multiplying force by velocity.
