# Zeros of Polynomial Functions

Imagine you are handed a large polynomial expression, say, a degree four polynomial where the highest term is four times x to the fourth power, and you need to find every single input that makes the whole expression evaluate to zero. In our last lesson, we discovered complex numbers, learning that numbers can have imaginary parts made of multiples of the square root of negative one. Today, we bring polynomial arithmetic and complex numbers together to see how every polynomial behaves when we search for its zeros. We will learn how to narrow down our hunt for rational numbers, how the factor theorem links zeros directly to binomial factors, and why complex zeros never appear all by themselves.

Finding zeros of high degree polynomials can feel like searching for a needle in a haystack, but we have a powerful tool called the rational zero test. Suppose we have a polynomial with integer coefficients, such as two times x cubed plus three times x squared minus eight times x plus three. The rational zero test tells us that any rational zero, written as a fraction in lowest terms, must have a numerator that evenly divides the constant term at the end, and a denominator that evenly divides the leading coefficient at the front. For our example, the constant term is three, whose integer divisors are plus or minus one and plus or minus three. The leading coefficient is two, whose divisors are plus or minus one and plus or minus two.

By testing the possible fractions formed by dividing a divisor of three by a divisor of two, we limit our search to a manageable list of numbers, including one, negative one, three, negative three, one over two, negative one over two, three over two, and negative three over two. Let us test the number one in our polynomial to see what happens. We substitute one for every x in our expression, working through the arithmetic step by step. First, cube one to get one, and multiply by two to get two. Next, square one to get one, and multiply by three to get three. Then, multiply eight by one to get eight. Combining these pieces, we take two plus three, which gives five, then subtract eight to get negative three, and finally add the constant term three, which leaves us with zero. We can verify this result by substituting one back into the expression: two times one cubed plus three times one squared minus eight times one plus three evaluates to two plus three minus eight plus three, which equals zero.

Because substituting one yields a result of zero, the factor theorem tells us immediately that the quantity x minus one must be a factor of our polynomial. Once we find one factor, we can divide our cubic polynomial by the quantity x minus one using polynomial division, leaving behind a quadratic expression. For our example, dividing two times x cubed plus three times x squared minus eight times x plus three by the quantity x minus one leaves the quadratic factor two times x squared plus five times x minus three. We can then factor this quadratic or use the quadratic formula to find its remaining roots. Setting two times x squared plus five times x minus three equal to zero yields two more roots, which turn out to be one over two and negative three. So our cubic polynomial has three real zeros, located at one, one over two, and negative three.

Not every polynomial has only real zeros, of course. Some polynomials leave us searching in the realm of complex numbers, where imaginary parts live. This brings us to the fundamental theorem of algebra, first proven completely by Carl Friedrich Gauss. The fundamental theorem states that every single variable polynomial with complex coefficients of degree one or higher has at least one zero in the complex number system. By extension, if we count multiplicity, a polynomial of degree n has exactly n complex zeros. A quadratic equation of degree two always has two complex zeros, a cubic has three, and a quartic has four.

When a polynomial has real coefficients, a striking pattern emerges among its complex zeros. If a complex number containing an imaginary part is a zero of the polynomial, its complex conjugate, where the imaginary part has the opposite sign, is guaranteed to be a zero as well. Complex zeros always come in conjugate pairs. Why does this happen? When you substitute a complex number into a polynomial with real coefficients, every operation of raising to a power and multiplying by a real number preserves the conjugate relationship. If the final result is zero, applying the rules of conjugates means the conjugate input must also produce a zero. This neat symmetry ensures that imaginary roots never stand alone.

Knowing how many zeros a polynomial possesses and where to look for them gives us complete control over algebraic equations. As we turn our attention toward the next lesson, we will use these polynomial behaviors to explore rational functions, examining how fractions made of polynomials behave near their vertical and horizontal asymptotes.

## Key ideas
- The rational zero test restricts potential rational roots of a polynomial with integer coefficients to fractions formed by divisors of the constant term divided by divisors of the leading coefficient.
- The factor theorem guarantees that if a number makes a polynomial evaluate to zero, then the quantity x minus that number is a factor of the polynomial.
- The fundamental theorem of algebra states that every polynomial of degree n with complex coefficients has exactly n complex zeros when counted with multiplicity.
- Complex zeros of polynomials with real coefficients always occur in conjugate pairs because the algebraic operations preserve conjugation.
