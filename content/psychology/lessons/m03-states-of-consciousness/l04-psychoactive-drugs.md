# Psychoactive Drugs

Imagine you are standing in a crowded city. Your brain is a bustling hub of neural traffic, where chemical messengers called neurotransmitters act like couriers, handing off signals between neurons to keep your thoughts, moods, and movements running smoothly. Now, imagine a foreign substance enters this city. It might act like a counterfeit courier, intercepting messages or flooding the streets with signals that were never meant to be sent. This is the fundamental way psychoactive drugs work. They are chemical substances that alter perceptions and moods by changing the brain’s chemistry.

These substances generally fall into four main categories based on their primary effects: depressants, stimulants, opioids, and hallucinogens. Our understanding of how these drugs interact with the brain is supported by extensive evidence from both animal models and human pharmacological studies. This research provides a strong, consistent picture of how these substances alter synaptic transmission.

Depressants are drugs that calm neural activity and slow down body functions. Alcohol is the most common example. It acts as an agonist for a neurotransmitter called gamma-aminobutyric acid, or GABA, which is the brain’s primary inhibitory messenger. By boosting GABA, alcohol slows down brain processing and reduces self-awareness. Stimulants, on the other hand, do the opposite. Drugs like caffeine, nicotine, and cocaine increase neural activity and rev up the body, raising heart rate and alertness. Cocaine, for instance, prevents the reuptake of dopamine, leaving more of that chemical in the synapse and creating a temporary surge of energy.

Then we have opioids, a group that includes morphine, heroin, and many prescription painkillers. These drugs depress neural functioning, but their most notable effect is their ability to mimic the brain’s own natural pain-relieving chemicals, called endorphins. By binding to the same receptors, they provide intense pain relief and a sense of calm. Finally, hallucinogens are drugs that change perception itself, so that a person may see or hear things that are not there. The classic hallucinogens, such as LSD and psilocybin, act mainly as agonists at one particular type of serotonin receptor. This mechanism is a key factor in how they disrupt the brain’s ability to filter information, leading to experiences that feel disconnected from objective reality.

The danger of these substances often lies in the brain’s remarkable ability to adapt. When you repeatedly introduce a drug, the brain begins to compensate for the artificial chemical surge. It might reduce the number of its own receptors for that substance or produce less of the natural neurotransmitter. This leads to tolerance, where the user requires larger and larger doses to achieve the same initial effect. As the brain adjusts its chemistry to accommodate the drug, the user may develop physical or psychological dependence. This is a state where the body and mind struggle to function normally without the substance.

Addiction is a severe stage of this cycle. It is a compulsive craving for a substance despite the adverse consequences it brings to one’s physical health, relationships, and daily responsibilities. It is important to note that addiction is a complex interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors. While the physiological changes in the brain are significant, they do not tell the whole story. Genetic predispositions, environmental stress, and underlying mental health conditions all play a role in how a person interacts with these substances.

When someone tries to stop using a drug after dependence has developed, they often experience withdrawal. This is the body’s painful reaction to the absence of the drug, as the brain struggles to recalibrate its chemical environment. The physical and psychological symptoms can be severe, which is why clinical support is often necessary for recovery. If you or someone you know is struggling with substance use, please remember that help is available. In the United States, you can call or text nine eight eight to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, which provides free, confidential support for people in distress.

Understanding these chemical interactions helps us see that consciousness is not a static state, but a delicate balance of neurochemistry. While drugs can temporarily shift our perspective, they often do so at the cost of the brain’s natural regulatory systems. As we move away from the chemistry of the brain, we will next explore how focused attention and intentional mental states might alter our experience of consciousness.

## Key ideas

- Psychoactive drugs alter consciousness by mimicking, blocking, or influencing the brain's natural neurotransmitters, a process supported by extensive pharmacological research.
- The four main categories of psychoactive drugs are depressants, stimulants, opioids, and hallucinogens, with hallucinogens primarily acting as agonists at serotonin receptors.
- Tolerance occurs when the brain adapts to repeated drug use by reducing its sensitivity, requiring larger doses to achieve the same effect.
- Dependence and addiction involve complex biological, psychological, and social changes that make stopping substance use difficult, often resulting in withdrawal symptoms.
