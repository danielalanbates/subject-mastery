# Mendelian Patterns of Inheritance

Have you ever wondered why children sometimes look remarkably like one parent while seeming to miss out on features from the other, or why certain family traits seem to skip a generation entirely? In our last lesson, we explored how meiosis halves our chromosome number and shuffles genetic information to produce unique gametes, or sex cells. But how does that shuffling actually translate into the physical traits we see around us? To answer that, we turn to Gregor Mendel, an Augustinian friar in Brno, in today’s Czech Republic, who spent years crossbreeding pea plants in his monastery garden and uncovered the foundational rules of heredity. His results, published in 1866, were largely ignored until other scientists rediscovered them in 1900.

Mendel did not know about DNA or chromosomes; instead, he tracked physical characteristics like plant height and seed color by counting thousands of individual offspring. He focused on traits with clear-cut alternative forms, such as purple versus white flowers. When he crossbred true-breeding plants with purple flowers with true-breeding plants with white flowers, something fascinating happened. The first generation of offspring, which we call the first filial generation, did not display a blend of both colors. Every single plant had purple flowers. The white color seemed to have vanished completely.

To understand why, Mendel allowed those hybrid purple plants to self-fertilize, producing a second generation of offspring. This time, the hidden trait reappeared. About seventy-five percent of the new plants had purple flowers, while twenty-five percent had white flowers, a consistent three-to-one ratio. Mendel reasoned that traits must be controlled by discrete units of inheritance, which we now call genes. Furthermore, because individuals inherit two copies of each gene—one from each parent—these alternative versions of a gene, known as alleles, can interact in specific ways.

This brings us to Mendel's first major concept, the Law of Segregation. During the formation of gametes in meiosis, the two alleles for a gene separate from each other so that each sperm or egg carries only a single allele. When fertilization occurs, the offspring receives one allele from each parent, restoring the pair. To describe an organism's genetic makeup, we use the term genotype, while the observable physical expression of those genes is called the phenotype. If an organism carries two identical alleles for a gene, it is homozygous for that trait. If it carries two different alleles, it is heterozygous.

In Mendel's pea plants, the allele for purple flowers was dominant, meaning it masks the presence of another allele when both are present in a heterozygous individual. The white flower allele was recessive, meaning its physical expression is hidden unless the organism inherits two copies of it. We can easily track these probabilities using a simple grid system called a Punnett square. If you cross a heterozygous purple plant with a homozygous recessive white plant, the Punnett square predicts an even fifty-fifty split in the offspring's phenotypes, neatly matching physical reality.

Mendel also asked whether different traits are inherited together or independently. To find out, he tracked two characteristics at once, such as seed shape and seed color. He discovered that the alleles for one gene sort into gametes independently of the alleles for another gene, as long as the genes are on different chromosomes. This is Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. Because chromosomes line up randomly along the cell center during meiosis, the inheritance of a gene for seed shape does not influence the inheritance of a gene for seed color.

While Mendel's laws provide a remarkably clear predictive framework, living systems are rarely quite this simple. Most real-world traits do not follow a strict dominant and recessive pattern, and many genes interact in complex ways influenced by the environment. As we will see in our next lesson, exploring complex inheritance patterns and chromosomal theory reveals an even richer layer of genetic diversity built on top of Mendel's foundational rules.

## Key ideas
- Gregor Mendel discovered the foundational rules of heredity by tracking physical traits across generations in garden pea plants.
- The Law of Segregation states that allele pairs separate during gamete formation so that each sperm or egg carries only one allele for each gene.
- Dominant alleles mask the expression of recessive alleles in heterozygous individuals, a relationship easily tracked using Punnett squares.
- The Law of Independent Assortment explains that alleles for separate genes are distributed to gametes independently of one another during meiosis.
