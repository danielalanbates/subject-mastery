# The Mean Value Theorem

Imagine you take a road trip in your car and drive one hundred miles in exactly one hour. Common sense tells us your speedometer must have hit one hundred miles per hour at least once during the journey. Even if you spent some time waiting at a red light, you had to speed up to make up for lost time. The Mean Value Theorem is the mathematical bridge that proves this everyday intuition. Building on our previous work with extreme values, this theorem connects the average rate of change of a function over an interval to the instantaneous rate of change at a specific point inside that interval.

To understand the mechanics, let us picture a smooth curve representing a function that is continuous on a closed interval from a starting point, say, the number one, to an ending point, say, the number four, and differentiable everywhere in between. If you draw a straight line connecting the point on the curve at the starting value to the point at the ending value, you have created a secant line. The slope of this secant line represents the average rate of change of the function over the entire interval. In our road trip analogy, this slope is your average speed over the whole hour.

The Mean Value Theorem rests on a foundation known as Rolle's Theorem. Rolle's Theorem proves that if a differentiable function has equal values at the two ends of an interval, its derivative must be zero at some point in between. The Mean Value Theorem expands this idea to any sloping secant line. It states that somewhere between the starting value and the ending value, there must exist at least one point where the tangent line to the curve is perfectly parallel to that secant line. Sometimes, there is more than one such point. For example, a wave shaped curve might have several spots where the tangent matches the average slope.

In the language of calculus, the instantaneous derivative at that hidden point equals the average rate of change across the whole interval. Think about what this means for slopes. The slope of the tangent line is the derivative, f prime of c, where the letter c represents a number strictly between our starting value and our ending value. The slope of the secant line is the quantity f of b minus f of alpha, divided by the quantity b minus alpha, where alpha represents the left endpoint and b represents the right endpoint.

Let us walk through a concrete example using simple numbers to see this in action. Consider the function f of x equals x squared, on the closed interval from the number zero to the number three. First, let us calculate the average rate of change. At our starting value of zero, f of zero is zero. At our ending value of three, f of three is three squared, which is nine. The change in the function values is nine minus zero, which is nine. The change in the independent variable is three minus zero, which is three. Dividing nine by three gives an average rate of change of three.

Now, we want to find if there is a number c inside our open interval from zero to three where the derivative, f prime of c, equals that average rate of change of three. We know from our derivative rules that the derivative of x squared is two times x. Therefore, f prime of c is two times c. Setting this equal to our average rate of change, we write two times c equals three. Solving for c, we divide both sides by two, giving c equals one and one-half. Notice that one and one-half sits comfortably between zero and three. The theorem holds true, and we have found the exact spot where the instantaneous speed matches the average speed.

Why does this fundamental truth work so reliably? It relies heavily on the continuity of the function and the existence of a derivative at every interior point. The standard proof tilts the picture: subtract the secant line from the function. The difference is continuous, differentiable, and equal to zero at both endpoints, so Rolle's Theorem gives a point where its derivative is zero, which is exactly where the tangent slope equals the secant slope.

One important consequence of this theorem is that if the derivative of a function is zero everywhere on an interval, the function must be a constant value on that interval. Furthermore, the theorem leads directly to the Mean Value Inequality, which lets us bound the total change in function values by multiplying the length of the interval by the maximum possible derivative. This principle lets mathematicians make powerful guarantees about the behavior of functions over large domains by looking only at their endpoints. As we move forward, we will use these guaranteed slopes to map out the exact shape of graphs and test our curves for increasing and decreasing behavior.

## Key ideas
- The Mean Value Theorem proves that for a smooth and continuous function, the average rate of change over an interval must equal the instantaneous derivative at least once inside that interval, building upon the foundation established by Rolle's Theorem.
- The slope of the secant line connecting the endpoints of the interval represents the average rate of change, and there may be multiple interior points where the tangent line matches this slope.
- In our worked example with the function x squared from zero to three, the average rate of change was three, and the derivative matched this value exactly at the number one and one-half.
- Important corollaries include the rule that a zero derivative everywhere on an interval implies a constant function, as well as the Mean Value Inequality for bounding changes in function values.
