# How Economists Use Evidence

Imagine you hear two politicians arguing about whether raising the minimum wage destroys jobs. One claims it causes businesses to lay off workers by the thousands, while the other insists it boosts employment by putting more money into the pockets of consumers. Both speakers sound utterly convinced, and both cite charts to prove their points. How do we move past competing opinions and figure out what is actually true? In our last lesson, we looked at the major schools of economic thought, which offer different frameworks for understanding how the world works. But theorizing is only half the battle. To turn economics from a philosophy into a science, researchers must test their ideas against the stubborn realities of the real world.

Testing theories in economics is uniquely difficult because we cannot easily run controlled laboratory experiments on an entire national economy. A physicist can drop a lead ball in a vacuum chamber to test gravity, but an economist cannot isolate an entire country in a glass jar, raise taxes, and watch what happens while holding everything else constant. Markets and human societies are constantly shifting. Inflation moves, consumer tastes evolve, and weather patterns disrupt supply chains all at the same time. To separate cause from correlation, modern empirical economists have had to become clever detectives, using data and statistical models to isolate the specific impact of a single policy or event.

To see how this works in practice, let us look at a simple worked example. Suppose researchers want to know if a cash bonus of one hundred dollars encourages workers to complete a safety training program. They study a large factory with one thousand workers doing identical jobs. By random chance, they offer the bonus to five hundred workers, forming the treatment group, while the other five hundred workers form the control group without the bonus. If four hundred workers in the treatment group finish the training, compared to two hundred workers in the control group, the completion rate rose from forty percent to eighty percent, an increase of forty percentage points. Because the bonus was assigned at random, the two groups should be alike in every other way, so the difference can be credited to the bonus.

In the real world, however, raw data rarely speaks for itself, and economists rely heavily on econometrics. Econometrics is the application of statistical and mathematical theories to economic data. Through regression analysis, a statistical technique, researchers can control for multiple variables at once. For instance, if an economist wants to study whether higher levels of education cause higher individual earnings, a simple chart might show that college graduates make more money. But we know that individuals who go to college might also come from wealthier backgrounds or possess higher innate ability. Advanced statistical models allow researchers to control for family income, test scores, and work experience, isolating the economic return of an extra year in the classroom. Controls can only account for what is measured, however, so economists also hunt for natural experiments, situations where chance or an outside rule sorts people much as a randomized trial would. The minimum wage debate offers a famous case. In 1994, David Card and Alan Krueger compared fast-food restaurants in New Jersey, which had just raised its minimum wage, with similar restaurants across the border in Pennsylvania, and found no loss of jobs. Other economists challenged the data, and the debate over larger increases continues, but the method changed the field, and Card shared the 2021 Nobel prize for it.

Yet, interpreting this evidence often sparks lively debates across different traditions in economics. Neoclassical economists tend to emphasize statistical models derived from strict assumptions about rational optimization, looking for broad market equilibria. Keynesian economists often focus on aggregate demand data and macroeconomic time series to evaluate policy interventions like stimulus spending. Monetarists place heavy emphasis on historical statistical data linking the money supply to price levels and nominal income. Austrian economists are generally skeptical of large statistical aggregations and complex econometrics, arguing instead that human action cannot be adequately captured by mathematical models and preferring deductive reasoning from foundational axioms. Behavioral economists, by contrast, focus heavily on experimental data from laboratory settings and randomized trials that highlight human cognitive biases and departures from perfect rationality.

This ongoing quest for better evidence has transformed how public policy is designed around the globe today. Governments and charities increasingly run randomized trials before rolling out major social programs, an approach recognized by the 2019 Nobel prize for Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo and Michael Kremer, testing small-scale interventions in education or healthcare to see what actually works before spending billions of dollars nationwide. By combining rigorous empirical data with careful theoretical models, economists continually refine our understanding of how societies allocate scarce resources. As we move on to our next lesson, we will explore how these empirical tools and theoretical frameworks help us understand the deep institutional foundations that drive long-term economic development across nations.

## Key ideas
- Economists test abstract theories by turning to empirical data, statistical models, and careful observation of the real world.
- Simple controlled comparisons help researchers isolate the direct impact of a specific policy or financial incentive from background noise.
- Econometrics uses statistical techniques like regression analysis to isolate cause and effect while controlling for confounding variables.
- Different economic schools, including neoclassical, Keynesian, monetarist, Austrian, and behavioral perspectives, often interpret empirical evidence through distinct methodological lenses.
