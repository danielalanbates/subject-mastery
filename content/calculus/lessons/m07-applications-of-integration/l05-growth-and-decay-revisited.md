# Growth and Decay Revisited

Imagine holding a warm cup of coffee on a cold morning, watching the steam rise as the temperature slowly drifts downward toward the air in the room. Or think about a small sample of a radioactive element, quietly shedding energy over centuries. In both cases, the rate of change is proportional to a quantity: for the sample, the amount itself, and for the coffee, its temperature difference from the room. Back in our early studies of differentiation, we learned how to model these proportional relationships using basic rates. But the true power of calculus shines when we reverse the process, using integration to look backward from a rate of change to find the total amount accumulated or decayed over time.

To see how integration handles this, let us recall that if a quantity, which we will call y, grows or decays at a rate proportional to itself, its derivative with respect to time is equal to a constant, k, multiplied by y. When k is positive, we have exponential growth; when k is negative, we have exponential decay, such as radioactive decay. While we can solve this relationship using separation of variables, integration provides the foundational accumulation framework that underpins every exponential model. We can think of the total change in our quantity from an initial time zero to a later time t as the integral from zero to t of the rate of change of y with respect to time, d t.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Suppose we start with a population of bacteria containing one hundred cells. After three hours, the population has grown to eight hundred cells. We want to find an expression for the population at any time t, and then use integration to determine the exact total cell-hours of bacteria that accumulate during those first three hours. We begin by setting up our proportional rate equation. The derivative of y with respect to t is k times y. Dividing both sides by y, the left side becomes the derivative of the natural logarithm of y, so integrating both sides from time zero to time t shows that the natural logarithm of y, minus the natural logarithm of our initial amount, equals k times t. Exponentiating both sides reveals that y of t equals our initial amount multiplied by e to the power of k t.

Using our initial condition where y of zero equals one hundred, we see that the initial amount is one hundred. Next, we use our second data point where y of three equals eight hundred. Substituting these values in, eight hundred equals one hundred times e to the power of three k. Dividing both sides by one hundred gives eight equals e to the power of three k. Taking the natural logarithm of both sides, we find that three k equals the natural logarithm of eight, which means k equals one third of the natural logarithm of eight, or roughly zero point six nine three. This gives us our complete population function: y of t equals one hundred times e to the power of the natural logarithm of two times t, which simplifies neatly to one hundred times two to the power of t.

Now, suppose we want to calculate the total cumulative exposure or integrated population over this three hour window. This requires computing the definite integral from zero to three of our function y of t, with respect to t. The antiderivative of one hundred times two to the power of t is the quantity one hundred times two to the power of t, all divided by the natural logarithm of two. We evaluate this antiderivative from zero to three. At the upper limit of three, two cubed is eight, giving eight hundred divided by the natural logarithm of two. At the lower limit of zero, two to the power of zero is one, giving one hundred divided by the natural logarithm of two. Subtracting the lower limit value from the upper limit value gives seven hundred divided by the natural logarithm of two, which is approximately one thousand and ten, in units of cell-hours.

Radioactive decay follows the exact same integral blueprint, except the constant k is negative. Imagine we are studying a sample of carbon-fourteen, which has a half-life of roughly five thousand, seven hundred thirty years. If we integrate the decay rate over a span of ten thousand years, the definite integral gives us the total amount of radiation emitted during that period. By mastering these exponential integrals, we connect abstract rates of change to tangible, accumulated physical reality. As we move forward to our next major module, we will discover powerful new algebraic tools for evaluating more complex integrals that do not fit standard exponential patterns.

## Key ideas
- Exponential growth and decay describe quantities whose rate of change is proportional to their current size.
- Integrating a proportional rate function allows us to calculate both the exact state of a system at any time and the total accumulated quantity over an interval.
- Solving these problems relies heavily on the natural exponential function and logarithmic properties.
- Definite integrals of growth models quantify total accumulated exposure or mass over a specific span of time.
