# Nonlinear Systems and Systems of Inequalities

Imagine aiming a hose at a garden wall. The water traces a curving path through the air, shaped by gravity, while the wall stands as a straight vertical plane. Where the stream strikes the stone, you find a precise point of contact. In mathematics, finding that intersection means solving a nonlinear system. Over the past few lessons, we have explored the graceful geometry of circles, parabolas, ellipses, and hyperbolas. Now, we bring those curving shapes together with straight lines and with each other, finding where their paths cross and exploring the regions they carve out of the plane.

When a system contains at least one equation whose graph is not a straight line, we call it a nonlinear system. Nonlinear systems can have zero, one, two, or even more intersection points, or sometimes infinitely many if overlapping curves match completely. Just as with linear systems, our goal is to find every coordinate pair that satisfies all equations simultaneously. Substitution remains our most powerful tool here. Imagine we want to find where the line given by y equals x plus one intersects the parabola given by y equals x squared minus three. Because both expressions equal y, we can set them equal to each other. We write x plus one equals the quantity x squared minus three.

To solve this new single-variable equation, we gather all our terms on one side to set the expression to zero. We subtract x from both sides and subtract one from both sides. This leaves us with zero equals x squared minus x minus four. This quadratic does not factor easily with small whole numbers, so we turn to the quadratic formula. Let us compute the pieces carefully. The quadratic coefficient on x squared is one. The linear coefficient on x is negative one. The constant term is negative four.

The quadratic formula tells us that the variable x equals the opposite of the linear coefficient, plus or minus the square root of the discriminant, all divided by two times the quadratic coefficient. First, the linear coefficient is negative one, so its opposite is positive one. Next, the denominator is two times the quadratic coefficient one, which gives two. Now let us find the discriminant inside the square root. We take the linear coefficient negative one squared, which is one, and subtract four times the quadratic coefficient one times the constant term negative four. That subtraction of a negative product gives one plus sixteen, which is seventeen.

Putting these intermediate results together, the variable x equals one plus or minus the square root of seventeen, all divided by two. So our two possible values for x are the quantity one plus the square root of seventeen, divided by two, and the quantity one minus the square root of seventeen, divided by two. To find the matching y values, we substitute each x back into our simpler linear equation, y equals x plus one, which gives the quantity three plus the square root of seventeen, divided by two, and the quantity three minus the square root of seventeen, divided by two. Each resulting coordinate pair represents a point where the straight line cuts through the sweeping curve of the parabola.

Sometimes a nonlinear system pairs a circle and a parabola, or two intersecting circles. In those cases, elimination by addition or subtraction often simplifies the work quickly, especially if matching squared terms can be subtracted away to leave a linear relationship. Once you find the coordinates of intersection, you have mapped the exact points where distinct geometric worlds collide.

Beyond single points of intersection, we often need to describe entire regions of the coordinate plane. This brings us to systems of inequalities. Where an equation draws a sharp boundary line or curve, an inequality shades an entire side of that boundary. Strict inequalities use a dashed boundary curve to show that the points on the boundary itself are not included in the solution set. Non-strict inequalities use a solid boundary curve to show that the boundary points are included.

Think of the inequality y is less than or equal to x squared. Because this inequality includes equality, the parabola y equals x squared acts as a solid dividing fence. To see which side satisfies the inequality, we test a point not on the curve, such as zero comma one. Substituting zero for x and one for y gives one is less than or equal to zero, which is false. Therefore, the region containing zero comma one, inside the bowl of the parabola, is excluded, and we shade the region below and outside the parabola instead.

When we combine multiple inequalities into a system, we are looking for the overlap where every condition holds true at once. Picture the inequality x squared plus y squared is less than or equal to nine, which describes a disc of radius three centered at the origin, combined with the inequality y is greater than zero. The system describes the upper half of a disc, bounded below by the x-axis and enclosed by the circular arc. Every point in that shaded half-disc satisfies both the boundary limits of the circle and the height requirement of the linear inequality. Describing these regions accurately allows us to model constraints in economics, physics, and design. As we turn our attention next to sequences and summation, we will shift from continuous curves and shaded regions to discrete patterns and the sums of ordered lists of numbers.

## Key ideas
- A nonlinear system involves at least one equation whose graph is a curve, and such systems can yield zero, one, two, or more intersection points.
- Substitution and elimination remain the primary algebraic methods for finding the coordinate pairs where curves and lines intersect.
- Strict inequalities use dashed boundaries to exclude border points, while non-strict inequalities use solid boundaries to include them.
- A system of inequalities defines a region of the coordinate plane rather than isolated points of intersection.
- Test points are used to determine which side of a boundary curve or line satisfies a given inequality.
