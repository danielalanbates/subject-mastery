# Hess's Law and Enthalpies of Formation

Imagine you are planning a road trip across the country. You know the total distance from your starting point to your final destination. It does not matter if you drive a straight line across the plains or take a winding route through the mountains; the total distance between the start and the finish remains the same. Thermochemistry operates on a similar principle. In our previous lesson, we discussed how chemical reactions release or absorb heat, which we measure as enthalpy change. Sometimes, however, it is difficult or even dangerous to measure the heat of a reaction directly in a laboratory. Fortunately, we can use the path-independent nature of energy to calculate these values indirectly.

This is the essence of Hess’s Law, which states that if a process can be written as the sum of several stepwise reactions, the total enthalpy change of the overall process is equal to the sum of the enthalpy changes for those individual steps. Enthalpy is a state function. This means the energy change depends only on the initial state of the reactants and the final state of the products, not on the specific pathway taken. If we know the enthalpy values for a series of reactions that add up to our target reaction, we can simply add those values together.

When we manipulate these equations, we follow a few simple rules. If we reverse a reaction, the sign of the enthalpy change must also be reversed, because an endothermic process becomes exothermic, and vice versa. If we multiply the coefficients of a chemical equation by a specific factor, we must also multiply the enthalpy change by that same factor. By carefully arranging these known steps, we can cancel out intermediate substances that appear on both sides of our combined equations, leaving us with only the reactants and products of our desired target reaction.

To make these calculations consistent, chemists use standard enthalpies of formation. A standard enthalpy of formation is defined as the enthalpy change that occurs when exactly one mole of a substance is formed from its constituent elements in their most stable, standard states. These measurements are taken under standard conditions, which are defined as a temperature of two hundred ninety-eight Kelvin and a pressure of one bar. By definition, the standard enthalpy of formation for any element in its most stable form, such as oxygen gas or solid carbon in the form of graphite, is zero. These values are compiled in tables, providing us with a standardized set of building blocks for calculating the energy of almost any reaction.

Notice what that zero means. Solid sodium, chlorine gas and helium gas all have a standard enthalpy of formation of exactly zero, however reactive or unreactive they are, because each is already an element in its standard state. Zero is simply the reference point. A compound with a large negative value, such as carbon dioxide, sits far below its elements in energy, so forming it from them releases a lot of heat.

Let us walk through a calculation using these values. Imagine we want to find the enthalpy change for the combustion of methane gas. We look up the standard enthalpy of formation for one mole of methane, which is negative seventy-five kilojoules per mole. We also find the values for the products, carbon dioxide at negative three hundred ninety-four kilojoules per mole, and water in the liquid state at negative two hundred eighty-six kilojoules per mole. The formula to calculate the total enthalpy change for the reaction is to sum the enthalpies of formation of all products and then subtract the sum of the enthalpies of formation of all reactants, taking care to multiply each value by its stoichiometric coefficient.

In this combustion reaction, one mole of methane reacts with two moles of oxygen gas to produce one mole of carbon dioxide and two moles of liquid water. First, we calculate the sum for the products. We have one mole of carbon dioxide at negative three hundred ninety-four kilojoules, plus two moles of water at negative two hundred eighty-six kilojoules each, which is negative five hundred seventy-two kilojoules. Adding these together gives us a total product enthalpy of negative nine hundred sixty-six kilojoules. Next, we sum the reactants. We have one mole of methane at negative seventy-five kilojoules, and two moles of oxygen, which is an element in its standard state, so its value is zero. The total reactant enthalpy is therefore negative seventy-five kilojoules.

To find the final change, we subtract the reactant total from the product total. We take negative nine hundred sixty-six kilojoules and subtract negative seventy-five kilojoules, which is the same as adding seventy-five. This results in an enthalpy change of negative eight hundred ninety-one kilojoules for the reaction. This negative value confirms that the combustion of methane is an exothermic process, releasing significant energy as it proceeds.

## Key ideas

- Hess's Law allows us to calculate the enthalpy change of a reaction by summing the enthalpy changes of individual steps, treating enthalpy as a state function.
- When manipulating thermochemical equations, reversing a reaction flips the sign of the enthalpy change, while multiplying the coefficients requires multiplying the enthalpy value by the same factor.
- Standard enthalpies of formation are measured under standard conditions of two hundred ninety-eight Kelvin and one bar, and they represent the energy change to form one mole of a substance from its elements in their most stable standard states.
- The total enthalpy change for any reaction can be determined by subtracting the sum of the enthalpies of formation of the reactants from the sum of the enthalpies of formation of the products.
- Every element in its most stable standard state, reactive or not, has a standard enthalpy of formation of zero, which serves as the reference point for all other values.
