# Monetary Policy Tools

Imagine you are steering a massive ocean liner. You do not turn the ship by leaning against the hull; you use a small rudder that shifts the flow of water around the entire vessel, gradually altering its course. When a central bank like the Federal Reserve wants to steer an entire national economy, it faces a similar challenge. Building on our previous look at how central banks operate, we now turn to the specific instruments these institutions use to guide the money supply, manage interest rates, and influence economic activity. Central banks typically rely on four main levers to accomplish this task.

The first and most commonly used lever is the interest-rate target. In the United States, the Federal Reserve sets a target for the federal funds rate, which is the interest rate commercial banks charge one another for overnight loans. By raising or lowering this target, the central bank sends a signal rippling through the entire financial system. If the economy is running too hot and inflation is creeping upward, the central bank raises its target rate. This action makes borrowing more expensive for banks, which passes higher costs down to businesses and consumers, cooling off spending and investment.

To make that interest-rate target a reality, central banks use their second tool, known as open market operations. This is the buying and selling of government bonds on the open market. Picture the central bank wishing to inject money into the economy. It purchases government bonds from commercial banks, paying for them by crediting those banks with fresh reserves. With more cash sitting in their vaults, banks find themselves eager to lend out those funds, which drives market interest rates down. Conversely, if the central bank wants to reduce the money supply, it sells bonds to the banks. The banks pay for these bonds by drawing down their reserves, leaving them with less money to lend and pushing interest rates up. Since the 2008 financial crisis, the Federal Reserve has operated with ample reserves and now steers its target mainly by setting the interest rate it pays banks on the reserves they hold, because banks will rarely lend to one another for less than they can earn risk-free at the Fed.

The third tool in the central bank's toolkit involves reserve requirements, which are the rules dictating the minimum fraction of customer deposits that banks must hold in cash rather than lending out. Suppose the central bank sets the reserve requirement at ten percent. For every one hundred dollars a bank receives in deposits, it must keep ten dollars locked away and can lend out the remaining ninety dollars. If the central bank wants to stimulate the economy, it can lower that requirement to five percent, instantly freeing up more money for banks to extend as loans. The Federal Reserve cut its requirement to zero in March 2020, but some other central banks, such as China's, still use reserve requirements actively.

The fourth tool consists of lending facilities, often referred to as the discount window. This is a safety valve where commercial banks can borrow short-term funds directly from the central bank when they face unexpected cash shortages. By adjusting the interest rate charged on these loans, known as the discount rate, the central bank can encourage or discourage emergency borrowing. During times of financial panic, central banks expand these lending facilities well beyond traditional boundaries, acting as a lender of last resort to prevent systemic meltdowns across financial markets.

To see these tools in motion, consider a simple worked example. Imagine an economy experiencing a sluggish job market and low consumer demand. The central bank decides it is time to act. It lowers its federal funds rate target, purchases bonds through open market operations to flood the banking system with reserves, and perhaps trims the reserve requirement. Commercial banks suddenly find themselves with abundant lendable funds and lower borrowing costs. They respond by lowering mortgage rates and business loan rates. Construction companies borrow to build new homes, factories expand their production lines, and consumers finance new car purchases.

Monetarists, Keynesians, and other economic schools often debate how quickly and predictably these tools transmit their effects to the broader economy, yet all agree on their underlying mechanics. As these policy adjustments ripple outward, they alter the cost of credit and reshape the economic landscape. Now that we understand the tools at the disposal of central banks, our next step is to examine how these measures are deployed in real-world scenarios to balance employment and price stability.

## Key ideas
- Central banks steer the national economy and manage the money supply using four primary monetary policy tools.
- Adjusting the target for overnight interbank lending rates sets a benchmark that ripples across all consumer and business loans.
- Open market operations involve buying or selling government bonds to directly influence the level of bank reserves.
- Reserve requirements and lending facilities control how much cash banks must hold versus how much they can safely lend out to the public.
