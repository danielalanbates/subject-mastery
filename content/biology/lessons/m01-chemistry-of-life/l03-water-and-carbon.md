# Water and the Chemistry of Carbon

If you were to search for life on a distant, rocky planet, the first thing you would likely look for is liquid water. There is a reason why space agencies use the phrase "follow the water" as their primary strategy. On Earth, every living cell is roughly seventy to ninety percent water, and all the chemical reactions of metabolism take place in an aqueous environment. But water is only half of the story. While water provides the medium for life, the element carbon provides the structural framework. Together, these two substances create the conditions necessary for biological complexity. To understand how a single cell functions, we must first understand the unique "stickiness" of water and the incredible building capacity of carbon.

In our last lesson, we discussed how polar covalent bonds form when atoms share electrons unequally. In a water molecule, the oxygen atom is far more electronegative than the hydrogen atoms, meaning it pulls the shared electrons toward itself. This creates a partial negative charge near the oxygen and a partial positive charge near the hydrogens. Because of this polarity, water molecules are attracted to one another like tiny magnets. The positive end of one water molecule aligns with the negative end of another, forming what we call a hydrogen bond. While a single hydrogen bond is weak and easily broken, the sheer number of them in a drop of water gives the liquid extraordinary properties that support life.

One of these properties is cohesion, which is the tendency of water molecules to stick to each other. You can see cohesion in action when you watch a water strider insect skate across the surface of a pond without sinking. The hydrogen bonds create a high surface tension that acts like an invisible film. At the same time, water exhibits adhesion, the ability to stick to other types of polar surfaces. In a tall redwood tree, cohesion and adhesion work together to pull water hundreds of feet upward from the roots to the leaves against the force of gravity. As water evaporates from the leaves, it pulls on the column of water below it, much like a chain being hauled up a well.

Water also acts as a massive heat sink, which stabilizes the environment. It has a high specific heat, meaning it can absorb or release a large amount of heat with only a slight change in its own temperature. This is why coastal cities often have milder climates than inland deserts; the ocean absorbs the sun’s heat during the day and releases it slowly at night. On a biological level, this helps organisms maintain a steady internal temperature despite external changes. Furthermore, when water does evaporate, it takes a significant amount of heat energy with it. This process, known as evaporative cooling, is exactly what happens when you sweat. As the moisture leaves your skin, it carries away excess body heat, preventing you from overheating.

Perhaps the most unusual property of water is that it becomes less dense when it freezes. Most substances contract and sink when they turn from a liquid to a solid, but water expands. As the temperature drops, the hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into a rigid, crystalline lattice that keeps them further apart than they were in the liquid state. Because ice floats, it creates an insulating layer on the surface of lakes and oceans. If ice sank, bodies of water would freeze solid from the bottom up, trapping and killing the life within them. Instead, the liquid water beneath the ice remains insulated, allowing fish and other organisms to survive the winter.

While water is the medium of life, carbon is the undisputed backbone of biological molecules. Carbon is unique because of its electron configuration. It has four electrons in its outer shell, which means it seeks four more to become stable. This property, known as tetravalence, allows a single carbon atom to form four sturdy covalent bonds with a variety of other atoms. In the language of chemistry, carbon is the ultimate "Lego" brick. It can bond to other carbon atoms to form long, straight chains, branched structures, or closed rings. This versatility allows for an almost infinite variety of molecular shapes, which is essential because, in biology, the shape of a molecule determines its function.

In the simplest biological molecules, called hydrocarbons, carbon is bonded only to hydrogen. These molecules, like those found in fats or petroleum, store a tremendous amount of energy. However, most biological molecules also contain functional groups. These are specific clusters of atoms that attach to the carbon skeleton and give the molecule distinct chemical properties. For example, the hydroxyl group, consisting of an oxygen and a hydrogen atom, makes a molecule polar and allows it to dissolve in water. The amino group, containing nitrogen, is a fundamental building block of proteins. By swapping out these functional groups, evolution has "designed" a vast library of complex molecules from a simple carbon base.

The relationship between water and carbon is what makes the transition from simple chemistry to complex biology possible. Carbon provides the stable, diverse structures, while water provides the fluid environment where those structures can interact. Carbon-based molecules that are polar, or "water-loving," are called hydrophilic. They dissolve easily in the cell's watery interior. Molecules that are non-polar and repel water are called hydrophobic. The tension between these two types of molecules helps drive the folding of proteins and the formation of cell membranes.

By mastering the properties of water and the flexibility of carbon, we can now look at how these elements are organized into the larger building blocks of the cell. In our next lesson, we will see how carbon chains and functional groups come together to form the first two major classes of biological macromolecules: carbohydrates and lipids.

## Key ideas

- Water is a polar molecule that forms hydrogen bonds, leading to unique properties like cohesion, adhesion, and high surface tension.
- Water’s high specific heat and evaporative cooling help regulate temperatures in both the environment and in living organisms.
- Unlike most substances, water is less dense as a solid than as a liquid, allowing ice to float and insulate aquatic ecosystems.
- Carbon’s ability to form four covalent bonds allows it to create a vast array of complex, stable skeletons for biological molecules.
- Functional groups are specific sets of atoms attached to carbon skeletons that determine the chemical behavior and solubility of organic molecules.
