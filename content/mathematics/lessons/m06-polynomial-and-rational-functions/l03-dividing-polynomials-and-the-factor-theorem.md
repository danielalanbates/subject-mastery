# Dividing Polynomials and the Factor Theorem

Imagine you have a large box of building blocks representing a complicated polynomial function, and you want to know if a smaller set of blocks fits into it evenly, with nothing left over. In arithmetic, if you divide twelve by four, you get three with a remainder of zero, which tells you that four is a factor of twelve. Today, we turn to polynomial long division, a mechanical process that lets us divide one polynomial by another, revealing hidden factors and simplifying expressions just as integer division does.

To see how polynomial division works, let us walk through an example step by step. Suppose we want to divide the polynomial three x cubed plus five x squared minus two x plus four by the binomial x plus two. We set this up just like long division with numbers, placing the dividend under the bar and the divisor on the outside left. We begin by looking at the highest power of our dividend, which is three x cubed, and asking what we must multiply the leading term of our divisor, x, by to get three x cubed. The answer is three x squared. We write three x squared above the division bar as the first term of our quotient.

Next, we multiply that three x squared by our entire divisor, the quantity x plus two. This gives us three x cubed plus six x squared. We subtract this product from our dividend. To subtract, we change the signs and combine terms. The three x cubed terms cancel out completely. For our squared terms, five x squared minus six x squared leaves negative x squared. We then bring down the next term from our dividend, which is negative two x, giving us negative x squared minus two x as our new working line.

We repeat the cycle. What do we multiply x by to get negative x squared? We need negative x. We write negative x above the bar in our quotient. Multiplying negative x by the quantity x plus two yields negative x squared minus two x. When we subtract this by changing signs, both terms cancel out, leaving a zero. Bringing down our final term, positive four, we find that our remainder is simply four.

The underlying principle here is the division algorithm for polynomials. This rule states that any dividend polynomial equals the divisor times the quotient plus the remainder. Furthermore, the division process continues until the degree of the remainder is strictly less than the degree of the divisor. Because our divisor, the quantity x plus two, has degree one, and our remainder, four, has degree zero, our division is complete. The result is three x squared minus x, with a remainder of four over the quantity x plus two.

When our divisor is a linear expression of the form x minus some number, the division algorithm simplifies significantly. Because the degree of the divisor is one, the degree of the remainder must be less than one, meaning the remainder is always a constant. For such linear divisors, we can also use synthetic division, a streamlined shortcut that omits the variables and writes only the coefficients to calculate the quotient and remainder much faster.

This division process naturally leads us to the remainder theorem. When any polynomial is divided by a linear expression of the form x minus some number, the remainder is always equal to the value of the polynomial evaluated at that number. In our previous example, dividing by x plus two is equivalent to dividing by x minus negative two. If we substitute negative two into our original polynomial, three times negative two cubed plus five times negative two squared minus two times negative two plus four, we calculate negative twenty-four plus twenty plus four plus four, which simplifies precisely to four. The remainder matches the function value at that point.

This connection brings us directly to the factor theorem, which states that a binomial of the form x minus some number is a factor of a polynomial if and only if the polynomial evaluated at that number equals zero. If our division leaves a remainder of zero, the divisor is an exact factor, meaning that the number we tested is a root or zero of the polynomial. This gives us a powerful tool for breaking down high degree polynomials into simpler pieces, setting the stage for our next lesson where we will explore complex numbers and discover how they fill in the gaps when zeros do not appear on the real number line.

## Key ideas
- Polynomial long division operates much like numerical long division, following the division algorithm where the dividend equals the divisor times the quotient plus the remainder, and division stops when the degree of the remainder is less than the degree of the divisor.
- When dividing by a linear divisor of the form x minus r, the remainder is always a constant of degree zero, and synthetic division provides a rapid shortcut for this process by working solely with coefficients.
- The remainder theorem states that dividing a polynomial by the quantity x minus r leaves a constant remainder equal to the polynomial evaluated at r.
- The factor theorem connects division to roots by proving that x minus r is a factor of a polynomial if and only if the polynomial evaluated at r equals zero.
