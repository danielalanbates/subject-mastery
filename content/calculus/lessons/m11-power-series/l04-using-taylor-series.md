# Using Taylor Series

Imagine you need to calculate the sine of one radian on a simple mechanical calculator that only knows how to add, subtract, multiply, and divide. How could it possibly find an answer? In our study of calculus, two towering figures, Isaac Newton in England and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in Germany, independently laid the foundations of the subject. Their historical relationship soured into a bitter priority dispute over who invented calculus first. Each also left a lasting mark on our language: Leibniz gave us the integral sign and the differential notation d x, while Newton introduced the dot notation for time derivatives. Building on their heritage, we use Taylor and Maclaurin series, expressing smooth functions as infinite sums of polynomial terms. Today, we put that machinery to work. We will use Taylor polynomials to approximate complex functions with simple polynomials, and we will master how to estimate the remainder error to guarantee the precision of our calculations.

To approximate a function near a chosen center point, we truncate its Taylor series after a finite number of terms. The resulting polynomial gives us a close local approximation. But an approximation is only useful if we know how much it might be wrong. That difference between the true function value and our polynomial approximation is called the remainder, or the error. If we can bound this remainder, we can prove our approximation is accurate to within any desired tolerance, whether that tolerance is one-tenth or one ten-thousandth.

Let us see this in action by approximating the natural logarithm of the quantity one plus x, centered at zero, for an input of zero point one. Recall that the Maclaurin series for the natural logarithm of the quantity one plus x begins with x minus x squared over two, plus x cubed over three, and so on, with alternating signs. If we use a fourth-degree Taylor polynomial, we substitute zero point one for x in those first four terms. We calculate zero point one, minus zero point zero one divided by two, plus zero point zero zero one divided by three, minus zero point zero zero zero one divided by four. Doing the arithmetic gives us a decimal value very close to zero point zero nine five three one.

How trustworthy is this result? We must estimate the remainder error. Taylor's remainder theorem gives us the Lagrange form of the remainder. This theorem states that the error is equal to the next derivative of our function, evaluated at some unknown intermediate point between zero and zero point one. We multiply that evaluated derivative by x to the fifth power, and we divide the result by five factorial. Alternatively, the remainder term can be expressed in an integral form, integrating the derivative from the center point to our input.

To bound the Lagrange form of this error, we find the maximum possible value of that fifth derivative on our interval. For the natural logarithm function, taking successive derivatives reveals a pattern where the fifth derivative is twenty-four divided by the quantity one plus x, all to the fifth power.

On the interval from zero to zero point one, the worst case for that denominator happens at zero, where the denominator is one. So the maximum value of the fifth derivative is twenty-four. Multiplying twenty-four by zero point one to the fifth power, and dividing by one hundred twenty, which is five factorial, gives us an absolute maximum error of two times ten to the negative sixth power.

For alternating Taylor series, such as our natural-logarithm series, we also have the alternating-series error bound. This handy rule tells us that the error is strictly less than the magnitude of the first omitted term. That first omitted term is the fifth term, which is x to the fifth power over five. Substituting zero point one gives us one hundred-thousandth divided by five, which confirms our tight bound. This rigorous math guarantees that our approximation is correct to at least five decimal places, showing the immense power of polynomial approximations in practical computation.

## Key ideas
- Truncating a Taylor series after a finite number of terms yields a Taylor polynomial used for practical function approximation.
- The remainder error measures the exact difference between the true function value and the polynomial approximation at a given point, and it can be expressed in either the Lagrange form or an integral form.
- Taylor's remainder theorem bounds this error by analyzing the next unused derivative of the function over the interval of approximation.
- For alternating Taylor series, the alternating-series error bound states that the error is less than the magnitude of the first omitted term.
- Bounding the remainder allows us to guarantee that a numerical approximation meets any required standard of precision.
