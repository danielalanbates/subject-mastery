# Power Series and Convergence

Imagine you want to calculate the sine of an unfamiliar angle, or evaluate an integral that defies every standard technique you have ever learned. How do calculators and computers manage these seemingly impossible tasks? They do it by transforming difficult, complicated functions into infinite polynomials that are wonderfully easy to handle. In our last journey through the ratio and root tests, we learned how to determine whether an ordinary series of numbers converges to a finite sum. Today, we take a giant leap forward by introducing a variable into the mix, creating an infinite polynomial where every term depends on a power of x.

We call this mathematical object a power series. Think of it as an infinite polynomial centered at a specific value, say zero, where the terms look like a constant coefficient multiplied by x raised to the power of n, running from zero to infinity. Just like an ordinary polynomial, you can add, subtract, and even differentiate power series term by term. But because they stretch on forever, a power series does not converge for every possible value of x. For some numbers, the sum explodes toward infinity, while for other numbers, it settles down neatly to a single, stable value.

The set of all numbers for which a power series converges is called its interval of convergence. At the exact center of this interval, the series is always safe and convergent, because every term involving x vanishes, leaving just the first constant coefficient. But as x moves outward, we reach a boundary. The distance from the center to this boundary is known as the radius of convergence. Inside this radius, the series converges absolutely. Outside, it diverges. Exactly on the boundary, at the endpoints of the interval, anything can happen, and we must test those points individually.

Let us find the radius and interval of convergence for a concrete example. Consider the power series where the n-th term is x raised to the power of n, divided by the product of two to the power of n and the square root of the quantity n plus one. The center of this series is zero, because the powers are simply x to the n. To find where this series converges, we can deploy the ratio test from our previous lesson. We look at the absolute value of the ratio of the n plus first term to the n-th term as n grows very large.

Writing out this ratio, we take the absolute value of x raised to the quantity n plus one, divided by the product of two raised to the quantity n plus one and the square root of the quantity n plus two, and we multiply by the reciprocal of the n-th term. After carefully simplifying the fractions, we are left with the absolute value of x over two, multiplied by the square root of the quantity n plus one divided by the quantity n plus two. As n approaches infinity, that square root fraction approaches the square root of one, which is just one.

The limit of our ratio simplifies to the absolute value of x divided by two. For the series to converge by the ratio test, this limit must be strictly less than one. Multiplying both sides by two tells us that the absolute value of x must be strictly less than two. This means our radius of convergence is two, and the open interval of convergence stretches from negative two to positive two.

Now we must check the two boundary endpoints, negative two and positive two, to see if the series converges right on the edge. If we plug in positive two for x, our series becomes one over the square root of the quantity n plus one, summed from zero to infinity. This is the series of one over the square root of n, shifted by one place, and it diverges by the integral test, because the integral of one over the square root of x from one to infinity grows without bound. If we plug in negative two, we get an alternating series whose terms decrease in magnitude and approach zero, which converges by the alternating series test. Thus, the full interval of convergence includes negative two but excludes positive two, running from negative two inclusive to positive two exclusive. In our next lesson, we will build on these sturdy foundations to differentiate and integrate power series, unlocking brand new ways to represent functions.

## Key ideas
- A power series is an infinite polynomial containing a variable, allowing us to represent complicated functions as sums of simpler powers.
- The radius of convergence measures the distance from the center of the series to the boundaries where convergence breaks down.
- The ratio test is the primary tool used to determine the radius of convergence by examining the limit of adjacent term ratios.
- Endpoint testing is always required to determine whether the boundaries of the interval of convergence should be included or excluded.
