# Sequences and Summation

Imagine walking down a staircase where each step down is twice as wide as the one before, or watching a population of rabbits double every single month. These orderly progressions of numbers appear everywhere, from the rhythm of music to the growth of financial investments. In our last module, we explored systems of equations and nonlinear curves, finding where different relationships intersect. Now, we turn our attention from static intersections to dynamic lists of numbers that march forward in a definite order. Welcome to the study of sequences, where every number has its place and its own rule of generation.

A sequence is simply an ordered list of numbers, stretching out either to a final end or marching on infinitely. Each number in the list is called a term. Think of a row of numbered mailboxes, where box one holds the first term, box two holds the second term, and box n holds the term in the n-th position. Sometimes we define a sequence using an explicit formula, a direct rule that tells us how to calculate the n-th term using only the position number n itself. For example, if the rule for the n-th term is three times n plus two, we can find the first term by substituting one for n, giving three times one, plus two, which is five. The second term is three times two, plus two, which is eight. The third term is eleven, and the fourth term is fourteen.

Other times, sequences are defined recursively. A recursive rule does not give you a direct shortcut to the hundredth term. Instead, it tells you how to find any term by looking backward at the terms that came immediately before it. You must always be given a starting point, like the first term, and then a rule for the next step. Suppose the first term is two, and every subsequent term is found by taking the previous term and adding four to it. To find the second term, we take the starting two, add four, and get six. For the third term, we take that six, add four, and get ten. By chaining these steps together, the entire sequence unfolds from the ground up.

As we work with lists of numbers and their growth patterns, we frequently encounter a special shorthand called factorial notation. The factorial of a non-negative whole number n is the product of all positive whole numbers from one up to n, and we write it as the number followed by an exclamation point. We read that aloud as n factorial. By convention, zero factorial equals one. For example, four factorial means one times two times three times four, which evaluates to twenty-four. Factorials grow with astonishing speed, outpacing even exponential growth because each new term multiplies by a larger and larger integer. They count the number of ways we can line up distinct items in a row, a concept we will rely on when we study counting and probability later in this module.

Often, rather than just looking at a sequence as a list, we want to know what happens when we add all those terms together. The sum of the terms of a sequence is called a series. To write these large sums compactly without writing out endless plus signs, mathematicians use summation notation. We write a large capital Greek letter sigma, which looks like a stylized E, to represent the instruction to add. Below the sigma, we place an index variable starting at a lower bound, and above the sigma, we place an upper bound telling us where to stop. Next to the sigma goes the formula for the terms we are adding. If we have the sigma with an index starting at one and ending at four, applied to the term formula two times n, we read this aloud as the sum from n equals one to four of two times n. This means we substitute one, two, three, and four into two times n, getting two, four, six, and eight, and then we add them all together to get twenty.

Mastering these foundational rules of sequences and summation gives us a sturdy bridge into the heart of discrete mathematics. Armed with explicit formulas, recursive thinking, factorials, and summation symbols, we are fully prepared to examine specific, highly structured types of lists. In our next lesson, we will investigate arithmetic sequences and series, where the difference between consecutive terms remains constant.

## Key ideas
- A sequence is an ordered list of numbers where each position corresponds to a specific term.
- An explicit formula calculates any term directly from its position number, while a recursive rule finds a term using the values of preceding terms.
- Factorial notation represents the product of all positive integers up to a given number, with zero factorial defined as one, and grows with extreme rapidity.
- A series is defined as the sum of the terms of a sequence.
- Summation notation uses a capital sigma to compactly represent the addition of terms in a sequence from a starting index to an ending index.
