# Constantine and the Edict of Milan

Imagine a world where your deepest identity—your faith—is a crime punishable by death. For the Christians we studied in our previous module, this was not a theoretical fear; it was the reality of the Roman Empire. Under the emperor Diocletian, the church faced the Great Persecution, the most systematic and brutal attempt by Rome to eradicate the faith. Diocletian believed that the stability of the empire depended on the traditional Roman gods. To him, the Christians were not just a nuisance; they were a dangerous, secretive group that invited the wrath of the heavens by refusing to offer sacrifice. Churches were destroyed, scriptures were burned, and believers were forced to choose between renouncing Christ or facing the sword, the wild beasts, or the labor mines.

Then, everything changed with a single man: Constantine. In 312 CE, Constantine was a contender for the imperial throne, leading his army toward a decisive battle at the Milvian Bridge, which is located just outside Rome to the north of the city. According to the historian Eusebius, Constantine saw a vision of a cross of light in the sky above the sun, accompanied by words promising victory. Whether through a genuine religious experience or a political calculation, Constantine attributed his subsequent triumph to the God of the Christians. The era of the martyrs had ended almost overnight.

The following year, in 313 CE, Constantine met with his co-emperor, Licinius, in Milan, in what is now northern Italy. Together, they agreed on a policy known as the Edict of Milan, which Licinius then published as a letter to the governors of the East. No original copy survives; its text is preserved in the writings of Lactantius in his work, Concerning the Deaths of the Persecutors, and Eusebius in his Ecclesiastical History. This agreement did not make Christianity the official religion of the empire—that status would come much later, under Theodosius the First in 380 CE. However, the Edict of Milan did something revolutionary. It granted full religious freedom to Christians and all other citizens. It ordered the immediate return of property confiscated from churches during the persecutions and ensured that the church could once again exist as a recognized, legal entity.

It is important to note that the peace was fragile. After initially agreeing to the Edict, Licinius later violated its terms and renewed the persecution of Christians in the East. This betrayal led to a civil war between the two emperors, resulting in Constantine’s victory and his eventual sole rule over the empire.

The transition from a persecuted underground movement to an imperial favorite was jarring. Suddenly, bishops who had been hiding in caves were invited to the emperor’s table. Church buildings rose with imperial funding, and the clergy received legal exemptions and honors. For many Christians, this felt like the dawn of a new age, a sign that God had finally vindicated the faith. They saw Constantine as a new Moses or an instrument of divine providence.

However, this newfound favor sparked a debate that has echoed through the centuries: did the imperial embrace help or harm the church? Those who argue it helped point to the end of state-sponsored slaughter and the ability to preach the Gospel without fear of execution. It allowed the church to organize its structure, hold councils to define theology, and influence the laws of the empire toward a more humane standard. The church could finally breathe, grow, and institutionalize its mission.

On the other hand, critics argue that the alliance with the state compromised the church’s soul. When the church becomes a tool of the empire, it often begins to adopt the empire’s methods: power, hierarchy, and political maneuvering. The prophetic edge of a faith that once stood apart from the world began to soften. As the church became the Imperial Church, it became fashionable to be a Christian. This led to mass conversions, but it also brought in people who were interested in political advancement rather than spiritual transformation. The church transitioned from a community of the committed to a broad, public institution, blurring the lines between the Kingdom of God and the kingdoms of men.

We must look at this period with nuance. Constantine’s policies fundamentally altered the landscape of history, moving the church from the margins to the center of Western civilization. But this move brought new, complex problems that the early martyrs never had to face. As the church gained political power, it soon found itself needing to resolve deep internal disagreements about the very nature of Christ, leading directly to the first great ecumenical councils.

## Key ideas

- The Great Persecution under Diocletian was the final and most intense effort by the Roman state to destroy the church through violence and the destruction of holy books.
- The Edict of Milan in 313 CE established religious freedom for all, allowing the church to own property and operate openly, though the document itself is known only through the records of ancient historians.
- Constantine’s conversion transformed the church from a persecuted, minority group into a public institution favored by the state, sparking a lasting debate about the dangers of political power for the Christian mission.
- Christianity did not become the official state religion until 380 CE under Theodosius the First, long after the initial legalization of the faith.
- The shift to an imperial status forced the church to define its theology more precisely as it sought to maintain unity across an empire that now identified itself as Christian.
