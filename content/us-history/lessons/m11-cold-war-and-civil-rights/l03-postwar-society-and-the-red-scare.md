# Postwar Society and the Red Scare

Imagine walking into a typical American living room in 1955. You would likely find a television set flickering with a variety show, a new refrigerator humming in the kitchen, and a glossy magazine on the coffee table. This scene was a reality for millions of Americans who had survived the Great Depression and the global trauma of World War Two. Following the victory in 1945, the United States entered an era of unprecedented economic prosperity. While the previous lesson discussed the global tensions of the Cold War and the conflict in Korea, this lesson shifts our focus to the domestic front. Here, the American experience was defined by a surge in consumerism, a dramatic shift in where people lived, and a climate of deep suspicion regarding political loyalty.

The most visible sign of this postwar boom was the growth of the suburbs. With the help of the Servicemen’s Readjustment Act of 1944, commonly known as the G.I. Bill, millions of veterans gained access to low-interest home loans and college tuition. Federal housing programs, through the Federal Housing Administration and the Veterans Administration, incentivized the construction of planned communities like Levittown, New York. These rows of mass-produced houses offered a slice of the American dream to the middle class. However, this prosperity was not shared equally. Federal housing policies often utilized redlining, a practice that denied mortgages to residents in minority neighborhoods, while developers like those in Levittown enforced whites-only policies. These actions systematically excluded Black families from the wealth-building opportunities of the suburban boom.

This migration was accompanied by the baby boom, a period of sharply rising birth rates that lasted from 1946 until 1964. As the population swelled, the demand for cars, appliances, and new schools fueled a cycle of growth that made the American economy the envy of the industrialized world. This era also solidified rigid gender expectations. The cult of domesticity encouraged women to prioritize home life and child-rearing, even as many women sought to maintain the independence they had gained while working in wartime industries. Meanwhile, television became the centerpiece of domestic life, standardizing American culture and promoting a new, pervasive consumer identity.

Yet, this domestic comfort existed alongside a profound sense of anxiety. The Second Red Scare was fueled by the genuine Soviet threat abroad and the discovery of espionage within the United States. The government responded with legislative tools to suppress suspected communists. The Smith Act of 1940 made it a crime to advocate the violent overthrow of the government, while the McCarran Internal Security Act of 1950 required communist organizations to register with the government and authorized the detention of suspected subversives during national emergencies. This climate of fear also targeted gay government workers in what became known as the Lavender Scare, as officials claimed that their sexual orientation made them vulnerable to blackmail by foreign agents.

The most aggressive face of this anticommunist fervor was Senator Joseph McCarthy of Wisconsin. In early 1950, McCarthy claimed he possessed a list of known communists working in the State Department. While he never produced a credible list of actual spies, his accusations dominated national headlines. This period, now called McCarthyism, saw a climate where political dissent was often equated with treason. The House Committee on Un-American Activities held highly publicized hearings, famously targeting the Hollywood Ten, a group of writers and directors who refused to answer questions about their political affiliations. By the mid-1950s, the fever began to break. When McCarthy turned his sights on the United States Army and began bullying decorated officers on national television, the public saw his tactics for what they were. His colleagues in the Senate eventually censured him, and his influence evaporated.

The conformity of the 1950s masked deep fissures within American society. Black Americans, who had fought for democracy abroad, returned to a segregated nation where the economic boom remained largely out of reach. These underlying tensions, alongside the stifling social expectations of the era, would soon ignite the massive social changes of the coming decades.

## Key ideas

- The G.I. Bill and federal housing programs fueled suburban expansion and college attendance, though discriminatory practices like redlining and whites-only policies systematically excluded Black families from these benefits.
- The baby boom placed immense pressure on public infrastructure, necessitating a massive expansion of schools and services to accommodate the growing population.
- Television and consumer culture became central to 1950s domestic life, reinforcing social norms and the cult of domesticity for women.
- The Second Red Scare utilized legislative tools like the Smith Act and the McCarran Internal Security Act to suppress dissent, while the Lavender Scare targeted gay government employees as part of a broader purge.
- McCarthyism created a climate of fear that equated political nonconformity with treason, a trend that only began to recede after Senator Joseph McCarthy lost public support by attacking the United States Army.
