# Romans, Galatians and Justification

Imagine standing before a judge with a ledger of debts stretching back years, completely unable to pay a single coin. This was the existential weight carried by the early followers of Jesus as they wrestled with the relationship between human actions and divine acceptance. In our last lesson, we traced the missionary journeys and early letters of the apostle Paul. Today, we step inside two of his most intellectually formidable letters, Romans and Galatians, to examine his central arguments regarding faith, law, and righteousness. These texts lay out a sweeping framework for how humanity stands before God.

To understand these letters, we must first encounter the righteousness of God, a foundational theme Paul introduces in the opening chapters of Romans. In biblical theology, justification describes how a morally compromised human being is put right with a holy God. In the ancient world, righteousness was often status achieved through civic virtue or keeping specific laws. But Paul radically upends this expectation, arguing that righteousness is an unearned gift from God, received solely through trust in Jesus Christ.

Christian traditions understand this gift of justification in different ways. In the Protestant Reformation, theologians emphasized a forensic view, where God declares a believer righteous by imputing the righteousness of Christ to them while leaving inner transformation as a lifelong process. By contrast, Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions traditionally understand justification as a process that includes inner healing and renewal. In Catholic theology, justification involves an infusion of grace that genuinely transforms the believer, making them inwardly righteous. Orthodox theology similarly emphasizes the concept of deification, wherein believers are united to Christ and share in the divine life by grace. All these traditions agree that salvation begins entirely with God's unearned grace, and in 1999 the Catholic Church and the Lutheran World Federation signed a Joint Declaration affirming a shared basic understanding of justification while noting remaining differences.

In his letter to the Galatians, written with an urgent and sharp tone, Paul addresses a crisis of identity. Jewish believers had come to the newly planted Galatian churches insisting that gentile converts must undergo ritual circumcision and keep the ceremonial requirements of the Mosaic law to belong to the family of God. Paul responds with passionate defiance, declaring in Galatians chapter two, verse twenty-one, in the King James Version: "If righteousness come by the law, then Christ is dead in vain." He argues that trying to earn God's favor through works of the law is a dead end. In his words from Galatians chapter two, verse sixteen, in the King James Version: "Knowing that a man is not justified by the works of the law, but by the faith of Jesus Christ." Paul insists that adding human rituals to the cross of Christ strips the gospel of its power.

If Galatians is an urgent battlefield dispatch, Romans is Paul's masterwork, written calmly and systematically to a church he had not yet visited. Here, Paul builds a universal diagnosis of the human condition, arguing that both the rebellious pagan and the upright religious person share the same fundamental flaw: all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God. Therefore, God intervened. In Romans chapter three, Paul explains that God put forward Jesus as a sacrifice of atonement, received by faith, to demonstrate His righteousness while justifying the one who has faith. To illustrate this, Paul points to Abraham in Romans chapter four as the prime historical example of justification by faith apart from works, noting that Abraham was counted righteous because he trusted God's promise before the ritual of circumcision was ever given.

A central debate in modern biblical scholarship concerns what Paul actually means by the phrase works of the law. For centuries, many Protestant theologians read Paul through the lens of the Reformation, seeing him argue against legalism, which is the human attempt to earn salvation through moral effort. However, since the late twentieth century, scholars associated with the new perspective on Paul, such as E. P. Sanders, James Dunn and N. T. Wright, have argued that works of the law primarily refers to Jewish identity markers like circumcision and dietary laws, which separated Jews from Gentiles. In this view, Paul argues against ethnic exclusion, insisting that in Christ, the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile has been torn down. Traditional scholars respond that moral legalism is still firmly in Paul's sights, especially in the opening chapters of Romans. Both viewpoints agree that human effort cannot bridge the moral chasm between humanity and God.

To picture this, imagine two people trying to cross a deep canyon. One person builds a small wooden bridge of good deeds, while the other sits down and admits they have no materials at all. In Paul’s picture, no bridge of deeds can span that canyon, and God alone lowers a rescue line from above. This rescue line is grasped by faith, which is simply trusting reliance on God's character and the finished work of Christ.

## Key ideas
- The gospel reveals the righteousness of God, which is a free gift of grace resulting in peace with God for all who believe.
- Paul uses Abraham as the prime example in Romans of justification by faith apart from works.
- The Mosaic law's purpose was to reveal sin and lead people to Christ, because if righteousness came through the law, Christ died for nothing.
- Protestant traditions view justification primarily as a forensic legal declaration, whereas Catholic and Orthodox traditions understand it as an infusion of grace that makes the believer inwardly righteous.
- Modern biblical scholarship debates whether Paul's phrase works of the law targets general moral legalism or specific Jewish boundary markers that excluded Gentiles.
