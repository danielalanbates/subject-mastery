# Magnetic Fields and Forces

Imagine walking through a bustling metal recycling facility where an enormous crane hovers overhead. Instead of a mechanical claw, a thick cable dangles a circular steel plate that swings silently down into a mountain of mixed scrap. With the flip of a switch, hundreds of pounds of iron and steel leap upward, clinging tightly to the plate. Another flip of the switch, and the metal drops neatly into a sorting bin. That crane is not powered by sticky glue or mechanical fingers. It is harnessed to the invisible, reaching influence of a magnetic field, an entity that can exert powerful pushes and pulls without ever touching its target.

In our recent study of electrical power and household safety, we looked at charges that flow steadily through stationary wires to run our appliances. But when those electric charges start moving through space, or when we examine permanent magnets made of aligned atomic loops, a brand new phenomenon emerges. A magnetic field is a region of space where a magnetic force is exerted on moving charges, on current-carrying wires, and on other magnets. Unlike electric fields, which can push or pull on stationary charges, magnetic fields are completely indifferent to stationary targets. An electron sitting entirely still feels nothing at all in a magnetic field. It is only when that charge begins to move that the magnetic world wakes up and exerts its influence.

To picture how this works, imagine shooting a tiny, positively charged particle horizontally across a room from left to right. Now suppose a strong magnetic field is directed straight upward from the floor toward the ceiling. As the particle enters this field, the magnetic force does not push it straight along the field lines or backward against its motion. Instead, it pushes it sideways, forcing it into a smooth curve. The direction of this force always sits at a right angle to both the velocity of the moving charge and the direction of the magnetic field. To determine this sideways push without relying on ambiguous words, we use the right-hand rule. If you point the fingers of your right hand along the horizontal velocity of the particle, and then curl those fingers upward toward the magnetic field lines, your thumb points directly outward toward the observer standing in front of the room.

This exact geometric rule also governs how magnetic fields interact with electric currents flowing inside wires. The magnitude of the magnetic force on a current-carrying wire equals the current multiplied by the length of the wire, multiplied by the magnetic field strength, and multiplied by the sine of the angle between the wire and the magnetic field. When we write this mathematically, the force equals the current times the length times the field strength times the sine of theta. This principle explains why power lines running near large electrical transformers occasionally vibrate or experience mechanical stress when heavy currents pass through them.

Let us walk through a quick calculation to see how strong this push can be for a single moving particle. The magnetic force on a moving charge equals the charge multiplied by its velocity, multiplied by the magnetic field strength, and multiplied by the sine of the angle between the velocity vector and the magnetic field. Suppose a single proton carrying a tiny charge of one point six times ten to the negative nineteen coulombs travels at a speed of two million meters per second. It enters a uniform magnetic field with a strength of zero point five teslas, moving at a clean ninety degree angle to the field lines, where the sine of ninety degrees is one.

Let us break down the multiplication step by step. First, we multiply the charge of one point six times ten to the negative nineteen coulombs by the speed of two million meters per second. Multiplying one point six by two gives three point two, and adjusting the exponent for the six zeros of two million leaves us with three point two times ten to the negative thirteen. Next, we multiply that intermediate result by the magnetic field strength of zero point five teslas, which is the same as dividing by two. Half of three point two is one point six, giving a final force magnitude of one point six times ten to the negative thirteen newtons.

While that number sounds microscopic, remember that a proton has an almost negligible mass of about one point six seven times ten to the negative twenty-seven kilograms. If we divide that force by the mass of the proton, we find an astonishing acceleration of nearly one times ten to the fourteenth meters per second squared. That tiny force produces a tremendous acceleration, whipping the proton into a tight, rapid circle. As a sense check, this acceleration is trillions of times greater than the standard gravity we experience on Earth, which makes complete sense for a subatomic particle moving at high speeds in a strong laboratory magnet.

This sideways nature of the magnetic force means it does something remarkable to moving energy. Because the force is always perpendicular to the direction of motion, it never does any work on the particle. It can change the direction of a charge's velocity instantly, but it can never change its speed or its kinetic energy. The particle simply loops around and around in a circular path, held captive by a field that guides it without ever draining its speed. This exact principle allows modern particle accelerators to steer beams of high energy particles safely through enormous underground rings.

As we look ahead to our next lesson, we will take these fundamental rules of moving charges, current-carrying wires, and magnetic fields and combine them into working technology, exploring how coiled wires and magnetic forces build the electromagnets and electric motors that power our modern world.

## Key ideas
- A magnetic field exerts a force only on moving electric charges, current-carrying wires, and other magnetic materials, leaving stationary charges completely unaffected.
- The magnetic force acts at a right angle to both the velocity of the moving charge and the direction of the magnetic field lines.
- Because the magnetic force is always perpendicular to the motion of a charge, it can change the particle direction but cannot alter its speed or do work on it.
- The magnitude of the magnetic force on a moving charge depends on the strength of the charge, its speed, the strength of the magnetic field, and the sine of the angle between the velocity vector and the field.
