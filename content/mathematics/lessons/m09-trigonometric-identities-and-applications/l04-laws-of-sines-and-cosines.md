# The Laws of Sines and Cosines

Imagine you are a surveyor standing on a flat field, looking at two distant trees. You can easily pace out the distance between yourself and the first tree, and you can measure the angle between the two trees from where you stand. However, you cannot walk through the dense brush to measure the distance to the second tree or the distance between the two trees directly. You have a triangle formed by your position and the two trees, but it is not a right triangle. Since the Pythagorean theorem and basic trigonometric ratios only apply to right triangles, you need more powerful tools to solve for the missing sides and angles of this oblique triangle.

We use the law of sines and the law of cosines. The law of sines states that for any triangle, the ratio of the length of a side to the sine of its opposite angle is constant for all three sides. If we label our sides as lengths p, q, and r, and their opposite angles as capital P, Q, and R, the law of sines tells us that the side p divided by the sine of angle P equals the side q divided by the sine of angle Q, which also equals the side r divided by the sine of angle R. This law is ideal when you know two angles and one side, or two sides and an angle opposite one of them. To find a missing angle using the law of sines, you use the inverse sine function. Remember that the sine of an angle is the same as the sine of its supplement, found by subtracting the angle from one hundred eighty degrees.

The law of cosines is a generalization of the Pythagorean theorem. It states that for any triangle with sides p, q, and r, the square of one side equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides minus twice the product of those two sides times the cosine of the angle between them. For instance, p squared equals q squared plus r squared minus two times q times r times the cosine of angle P. We use this when we know two sides and the included angle, or when we know all three sides of a triangle. If you know all three sides, you can solve for an angle by rearranging the formula to isolate the cosine of the angle and then applying the inverse cosine function.

Let us work through an example. Suppose we have a triangle where side q is six, side r is eight, and the angle P between them is sixty degrees. Since we know two sides and the included angle, we use the law of cosines to find side p. We calculate six squared, which is thirty-six, plus eight squared, which is sixty-four, giving us one hundred. Then we subtract two times six times eight times the cosine of sixty degrees. Since the cosine of sixty degrees is one half, two times six times eight times one half is forty-eight. One hundred minus forty-eight is fifty-two. Thus, p squared is fifty-two, and side p is the square root of fifty-two, which is approximately seven point two one.

Sometimes we encounter the ambiguous case, which occurs when we are given two sides and an angle that is not between them. To determine the number of possible triangles, you must compare the side opposite the given angle to the altitude of the triangle, which is the other given side times the sine of the given angle. If the side opposite the given angle is shorter than the altitude, no triangle exists. If it equals the altitude, exactly one right triangle exists. If the side opposite the given angle is longer than the altitude but shorter than the other given side, two different triangles exist. In this case, you must check both the acute angle provided by the inverse sine function and the obtuse angle found by subtracting that result from one hundred eighty degrees. If the side opposite the given angle is longer than or equal to the other side, only one triangle exists.

When you approach a problem, first identify what you know. If you are given a side and its opposite angle, or two angles and a side, the law of sines is usually the most direct path. If you are given three sides or two sides and the angle between them, the law of cosines is your best choice. Always keep in mind that the sum of the interior angles of any triangle must be one hundred eighty degrees. Use this property to find the final angle once you have determined the others. By mastering these two laws, you can calculate the dimensions of any triangular path or structure.

## Key ideas

- The law of sines states that the ratio of a side length to the sine of its opposite angle is equal for all three sides of a triangle.
- The law of cosines acts as a generalized Pythagorean theorem, allowing us to solve for missing sides or angles when we have two sides and an included angle or three sides.
- In the side-side-angle case, the number of possible triangles is determined by comparing the side opposite the given angle to the altitude, which is the product of the other side and the sine of the given angle.
- When solving for an angle in the ambiguous case, you must consider both the acute angle from the inverse sine and its obtuse supplement.
- The angle-sum property states that the three interior angles of a triangle must total one hundred eighty degrees, which is essential for finding the final missing angle.
