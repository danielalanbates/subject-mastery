# Reflection and Mirrors

Stand in front of a shiny bathroom mirror and raise your right hand. Your reflection raises its left hand. This familiar reversal has fascinated humanity for millennia, marking an early encounter with controlled optics. In our last journey, we mapped out the invisible waves of the electromagnetic spectrum, discovering that visible light is just a narrow band of a vast radiation family. Today, we narrow our focus to how those light waves behave when they strike a smooth boundary, bouncing back to form the crisp images we rely on every day.

To understand this bouncing act, we treat light as a ray traveling in a straight line until it hits an obstacle. Imagine rolling a billiard ball across a smooth floor directly into a sturdy wall. The angle at which the ball approaches the cushion equals the angle at which it rebounds. Light follows this exact geometric rule. The law of reflection states that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. When light rays strike a flat, polished surface like a plane mirror, they bounce off in an orderly fashion rather than scattering wildly.

A plane mirror produces an image of the same size, meaning the magnification equals one, and the image distance equals the object distance. If you stand three feet in front of a flat mirror, your reflection appears to stand three feet behind the glass, yet no light rays actually meet behind the mirror. Your brain traces the divergent rays backward in straight lines. These rays intersect in your mind, creating the illusion of depth and forming what physicists call a virtual image. The image is upright and reverses left to right.

Curved mirrors bend these rules in fascinating ways by altering the angles of incoming rays. Imagine the inside of a polished metal spoon. This concave shape causes parallel light rays to converge toward a single focal point in front of the mirror. For any spherical mirror, the focal length is half its radius of curvature. We use specific sign conventions in the mirror equation. Object distance is positive when the object is in front of the mirror. Image distance is positive for real images in front of the mirror and negative for virtual images behind the mirror. Focal length is positive for concave mirrors and negative for convex mirrors.

We can understand concave mirrors by using three principal rays in our ray diagrams. The first is the parallel ray, which travels parallel to the principal axis and reflects through the focal point. The second is the focal ray, which passes through the focal point first and reflects parallel to the axis. The third is the centre of curvature ray, which passes through the centre of curvature and reflects straight back along itself.

The image types formed by a concave mirror change as the object moves. When the object sits beyond the centre of curvature, the mirror forms a real, inverted, and smaller image between the centre and the focal point. When the object is right at the centre of curvature, the image forms at the centre, and it is real, inverted, and the exact same size as the object. Move the object between the centre and the focal point, and the image forms beyond the centre as a real, inverted, and enlarged projection. Place the object right at the focal point, and the reflected rays travel parallel, meaning no image forms at all. Finally, if you place the object between the focal point and the mirror, the rays diverge, and you see an enlarged upright virtual image behind the glass.

Convex mirrors bulge outward toward you, behaving in the exact opposite manner. Instead of gathering light together, they spread incoming rays apart. Because the rays diverge, they can never form a real image, but they do produce a diminished upright virtual image located behind the mirror surface. This wide dispersal is precisely why passenger side mirrors on automobiles are convex, carrying the printed warning that objects in the mirror are closer than they appear. The mirror shrinks a wide field of view to fit into a small glass area, tricking your brain into thinking distant traffic is farther away than reality dictates.

Let us trace a quick numerical example using a concave makeup mirror with a focal length of twenty centimeters. If you place a small object sixty centimeters in front of this mirror, where does the image form? Using the mirror equation relating focal length, object distance, and image distance, we find that the light rays converge to form a real image thirty centimeters in front of the mirror. Because the image distance is half the object distance, the magnification factor is negative zero point five, telling us the image is half the original size and inverted.

Mirrors do more than just groom our hair or park our cars; they lie at the heart of giant astronomical telescopes that peer back toward the birth of the cosmos. By replacing heavy glass lenses with massive curved mirrors, astronomers avoid the color distortion that plagues refractors, capturing pristine light from distant galaxies. As we close our look at reflection, we prepare to cross the boundary from bouncing light to bending light, discovering what happens when rays plunge straight through transparent materials.

## Key ideas
- The law of reflection dictates that the angle at which a light ray strikes a surface equals the angle at which it bounces away.
- A plane mirror produces an image of the same size with a magnification of one, where the image distance equals the object distance.
- The focal length of a spherical mirror is half its radius of curvature, and the mirror equation uses specific sign conventions for distances and focal lengths.
- Concave mirrors form different image types depending on object position, ranging from magnified virtual images to inverted real images of varying sizes.
- Convex mirrors curve outward to diverge light rays, producing a wide field of view with smaller, upright virtual images.
