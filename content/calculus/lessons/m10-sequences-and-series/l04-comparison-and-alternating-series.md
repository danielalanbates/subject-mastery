# Comparison and Alternating Series

Imagine you are trying to weigh a heavy box, but your scale only goes up to ten pounds. You cannot read the exact weight on your own scale, but you can place your box alongside a second box whose weight you already know for certain. If your heavy box rests below the known box, and the known box is light enough to stay manageable, then your unknown box must also be manageable. Calculus works in much the same way when we cannot evaluate an infinite series directly. When direct methods fail, we turn to comparison and alternating series tests.

To understand the direct comparison test, consider two sequences of positive numbers where every term in the first sequence is smaller than or equal to the corresponding term in the second sequence. Both series must have positive terms for this logic to hold. If the larger series adds up to a finite number, then the smaller series, bounded from above by a convergent giant, must also add up to a finite number. Conversely, if the smaller series stretches out toward infinity, then the larger series, sitting proudly above it, must also diverge to infinity.

To put this into practice, consider the series whose terms are one divided by the quantity two to the power n, plus one, where n starts at one. We look at our given series and compare its terms to a simpler geometric series we already understand, namely one divided by two to the power n. For every positive integer n, the denominator two to the power n, plus one, is strictly larger than the denominator two to the power n. Because a larger denominator makes a fraction smaller, each term of our new series is smaller than the corresponding term of the geometric series whose common ratio is one half. Since that geometric series converges, our new series must also converge by the direct comparison test.

Sometimes, an inequality does not hold for every single term right from the start. It is enough if the comparison inequality eventually holds for all large values of n beyond some point. That leads us to the limit comparison test, which also requires both series to have positive terms. Here, we take the limit as n approaches infinity of the ratio of our two series terms. If that limit turns out to be a positive finite number, both series share the exact same destiny, either both converging or both diverging together. If the limit is zero and the comparison series converges, then our original series also converges. If the limit is infinity and the comparison series diverges, then our original series also diverges. This gives us immense flexibility because we can strip away messy lower-order terms and compare our complex expression to a clean power like one over n to the power p.

What happens, though, when our series terms do not all share the same positive sign, but instead bounce back and forth between positive and negative values? An alternating series is one where the signs alternate between positive and negative, such as the alternating harmonic series, where the term is negative one raised to the quantity n plus one, all over n. The alternating series test, studied by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, tells us that such a series will converge if two conditions are met. First, the absolute value of the terms must decrease steadily toward zero as n grows larger, though this decrease only needs to hold eventually for all large n rather than from the very first term. Second, the limit as n approaches infinity of the n-th term must be zero.

Let us trace the alternating harmonic series. The terms decrease monotonically in magnitude, and the limit as n approaches infinity of one over n is zero. Therefore, the alternating harmonic series converges, even though the standard harmonic series without alternating signs diverges to infinity. The alternating signs cause partial sums to overshoot and undershoot their ultimate limit in shrinking oscillations, trapping the sum in a tightening net. We must note, however, that the alternating series test proves convergence, not absolute convergence. We must check absolute convergence separately by testing the series formed by taking the absolute value of every term. For the alternating harmonic series, that series of absolute values is the divergent harmonic series, so we say the alternating harmonic series converges conditionally.

## Key ideas
- The direct comparison test proves the convergence or divergence of a series with positive terms by bounding it term by term with a known series.
- The limit comparison test examines the limiting ratio of two positive term series to determine if they share the same convergence behavior, and yields further conclusions when the limit is zero or infinity.
- An alternating series features signs that switch between positive and negative with each successive term, such as negative one raised to the quantity n plus one, all over n.
- The alternating series test guarantees convergence if the absolute terms eventually decrease monotonically and approach zero in the limit, though passing the test does not by itself show absolute convergence.
