# American Empire

Imagine standing on a dock in San Francisco in the late 1890s, watching steamships depart for distant Pacific ports. For decades, the United States had focused its energy on settling the vast interior of the North American continent. But as the frontier officially closed and industrial production surged, American business leaders and politicians began to look outward. They were heavily influenced by the naval strategist Alfred Thayer Mahan, whose 1890 book, The Influence of Sea Power Upon History, argued that national greatness depended on a powerful navy, overseas bases, and control of global trade routes. This shift from continental expansion to global influence marked the birth of the American empire, a period where the United States stepped onto the world stage as a major power, often at a steep cost to others.

The Pacific Ocean became the first major theater of this new ambition. Hawaii, a sovereign kingdom with a deep history and a strategic location, became the target of American commercial interests. Sugar planters, largely of American descent, exerted increasing control over the island’s economy. When Queen Liliuokalani attempted to restore power to the Hawaiian monarchy and reduce the influence of these foreign interests in 1893, a small group of businessmen, backed by United States Marines, orchestrated a coup. After years of political maneuvering, the United States formally annexed Hawaii in 1898. This act secured a vital coaling station for the navy, but it left a lasting scar on the Hawaiian people, who saw their independence erased by foreign power.

While the Pacific drew the gaze of some, the Caribbean remained the focus of intense debate, particularly regarding the Spanish colony of Cuba. In early 1898, the sinking of the United States ship Maine in Havana harbor served as the immediate catalyst for war. Public outcry, amplified by sensationalist journalism, pushed the nation into the Spanish-American War. The conflict lasted only a few months and resulted in a decisive American victory. In a letter to Theodore Roosevelt in July 1898, John Hay, then the American ambassador to Britain and soon to become secretary of state, famously called it a "splendid little war." Yet, the consequences were far from small. Through the Treaty of Paris, Spain relinquished control of Cuba and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States.

The acquisition of the Philippines proved to be the most contentious outcome of the war. While many Americans believed in a duty to civilize or uplift the Filipino people, others saw the move as a betrayal of the democratic principles upon which the United States was founded. This internal divide birthed the Anti-Imperialist League, which counted writers like Mark Twain among its members. They argued that a republic built on the consent of the governed could not rightfully hold distant colonies against their will.

The military campaigns in these territories included the service of Black soldiers, known as Buffalo Soldiers. These men participated in the fighting in both Cuba and the Philippines. However, their experience was complex. Many Black soldiers and civilians back home saw a troubling irony in fighting to expand an empire abroad while facing systemic racism and disenfranchisement at home. Some Black soldiers expressed deep sympathy for the Filipino people, noting that they were fighting against the same kind of white supremacy and colonial oppression that they faced in the United States.

The resulting Philippine-American War, which began in 1899, was far more brutal and prolonged than the conflict against Spain. Filipino forces, led by Emilio Aguinaldo, fought a fierce guerrilla campaign to secure their independence. The United States military responded with overwhelming force, leading to a conflict that lasted until 1902. Estimates of the death toll vary widely, but historians suggest that about 200,000 to over one million Filipino civilians died, along with thousands of combatants. The war exposed the harsh realities of empire: the occupation required a large military presence, the use of torture, and the concentration of civilian populations into camps.

The debate over empire was a fundamental clash over the identity of the United States. Proponents argued that the nation needed to compete with European powers and secure new markets. Critics warned that imperialism would corrupt American democracy at home and entangle the nation in foreign conflicts. As the smoke cleared, the United States found itself a global player with overseas territories and a growing navy. This new reality demanded a different kind of government, one that was more active in managing both the economy and the nation's social health.

## Key ideas

- The rise of the American empire was fueled by the strategic theories of Alfred Thayer Mahan and a desire for new international markets and naval bases.
- The sinking of the United States ship Maine in 1898 served as the immediate catalyst for the Spanish-American War, which ended with the United States acquiring multiple overseas territories.
- Black soldiers, known as Buffalo Soldiers, served in these conflicts, often grappling with the moral contradiction of fighting for an empire that denied them full equality at home.
- The Philippine-American War resulted in a devastating loss of life, with estimates ranging from 200,000 to over one million civilian deaths, highlighting the brutal costs of imperial expansion.
- A fierce national debate emerged between imperialists, who saw expansion as essential to national strength, and anti-imperialists, who feared that holding colonies would undermine American democracy.
