# Trigonometric Integrals and Substitution

Imagine you are trying to find the area under a curve that dips below a circle, or perhaps you are measuring the physical span of a hanging cable. In your last lesson, you learned integration by parts, which lets you take apart products of functions like algebraic terms multiplied by logarithms. But what happens when your integrand contains powers of sines and cosines tangled together, or a square root of a constant minus an unknown variable squared? Those shapes resist standard substitution and integration by parts. To untangle them, we reach for trigonometric identities and a clever technique called trigonometric substitution.

Let us start by looking at powers of trigonometric functions. Suppose you need to integrate the cube of the sine of x, with respect to x. If you try a basic substitution by letting your variable of substitution be the cosine of x, its derivative is the negative sine of x, which does not quite match the single sine factor you have left over. The trick is to peel off one factor of the sine of x, leaving behind an even power of the sine of x multiplied by that single sine factor. That leftover sine factor is waiting to absorb the derivative when you make a substitution. Then, you use the fundamental Pythagorean trigonometric identity, which states that the square of the sine of x plus the square of the cosine of x equals one, to rewrite the remaining even power entirely in terms of the cosine of x.

Let us walk through this specific example step by step in full words. We want to find the antiderivative of the cube of the sine of x, with respect to x. First, rewrite the integrand as the product of the square of the sine of x and the sine of x. Next, replace the square of the sine of x with the quantity one minus the square of the cosine of x, all multiplied by the sine of x, with respect to x. Now, let your substitution variable u equal the cosine of x. The differential d u equals the negative sine of x, with respect to x. This means our integral transforms into the negative of the integral of the quantity one minus u squared, d u. Integrating this new polynomial term by term gives us the negative of the quantity u minus the cube of u divided by three, plus our constant of integration c. Substituting back our original expression for u, our final result is the cube of the cosine of x divided by three, minus the cosine of x, plus the constant c. To check this work, we can differentiate our answer using the chain rule. The derivative of the negative of the cosine of x is the sine of x. The derivative of the cube of the cosine of x divided by three is the negative of the square of the cosine of x multiplied by the sine of x. Factor out the sine of x, and you are left with the sine of x multiplied by the quantity one minus the square of the cosine of x, which is precisely the cube of the sine of x we started with.

What if your integral contains a square root of the form of a constant squared minus an unknown variable squared, such as the square root of the quantity four minus the square of x? Here, ordinary algebraic substitution fails because there is no x on the outside to act as a derivative. Instead, we use trigonometric substitution. We exploit the exact same Pythagorean identity by letting x equal two multiplied by the sine of an angle, which we will call theta. When you substitute this into your square root, the square root of the quantity four minus the square of x becomes the square root of the quantity four minus four multiplied by the square of the sine of theta. Factor out the four, and you have the square root of four multiplied by the quantity one minus the square of the sine of theta, which simplifies neatly down to two multiplied by the cosine of theta. By changing variables from x to theta, the troublesome square root vanishes, leaving behind a manageable trigonometric integral.

Trigonometric substitution transforms geometric obstacles into manageable trigonometric puzzles, opening the door to functions that once seemed impossible to integrate. As you move forward to your next lesson, you will discover how to handle rational functions by factoring their denominators and splitting them into simpler fractions, using the method of partial fractions.

## Key ideas
- Trigonometric integrals rely on identities like the fundamental Pythagorean trigonometric identity to rewrite powers of sines and cosines so that basic substitution rules apply.
- Peeling off a single trigonometric factor leaves behind an even power that can be easily converted to the complementary function.
- Trigonometric substitution replaces algebraic variables with trigonometric functions to eliminate troublesome square roots involving sums or differences of squares.
- Checking your work by differentiating the resulting antiderivative ensures that sign changes and chain rule applications are correct.
