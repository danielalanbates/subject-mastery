# The Laws of Logarithms

Imagine you are working with numbers so large that adding them together is useless, yet multiplying them requires writing pages of zeros. Centuries before electronic computers, astronomers and navigators faced this exact barrier. They discovered that if you can translate multiplication into simple addition, heavy calculation becomes effortless. In our last lesson, we met logarithms as the inverse operations of exponential functions, asking the question: what exponent gives us this specific number? Today, we unlock the true power of logarithms by discovering how exponents behave when we translate them into log scale. Because logarithms are exponents, every single rule we have for exponents has a mirror image in the laws of logarithms.

Let us start with domain restrictions before we look at operations. For any logarithmic expression, the base must be a positive number other than one, and the argument inside the logarithm must be strictly greater than zero. These conditions are necessary because negative numbers and zero do not have real-valued logarithms in these systems.

With those boundaries in place, let us examine multiplication. When you multiply two exponential terms with the same base, you add their exponents. For example, two to the power of three times two to the power of four equals two to the power of seven, because three plus four is seven. What happens when we apply a logarithm to a product? Let us name our base `b`, and let the number `x` and the number `y` be two positive real numbers. The logarithm of the product of `x` and `y` to the base `b` can be rewritten. Since `x` is `b` to the power of some exponent, and `y` is `b` to the power of another exponent, multiplying them means we add those exponents. Therefore, the log of a product is equal to the sum of the logs of its individual factors. The log of `x` times `y` equals the log of `x` plus the log of `y`. Multiplication inside the logarithm turns into addition outside.

Division follows the exact same logic. When you divide exponential expressions with the same base, you subtract the exponent in the denominator from the exponent in the numerator. Following this back to logarithms, the logarithm of a fraction, or quotient, turns into subtraction. The log of `x` divided by `y` equals the log of `x` minus the log of `y`, provided that `x` and `y` are both positive. Notice how subtraction mirrors division just as addition mirrors multiplication.

The third major rule deals with powers. When you raise an exponential expression to another power, you multiply the exponents, as seen when the square of `x` is cubed, yielding `x` to the sixth power. In the logarithmic world, this means an exponent sitting inside a logarithm can be brought out front as a simple multiplier. The log of `x` raised to the power of `p` equals `p` times the log of `x`, for any positive number `x`. Let us see this in action with numbers. Suppose we want to find the base ten logarithm of the number one thousand squared. One thousand is ten to the third power, and squaring it gives ten to the sixth power, so its log is six. Using our power rule, we can take the exponent two, multiply it by the log of one thousand, which is three, and two times three gives us six. The rules match perfectly.

These three rules allow mathematicians to expand a complex expression into several simpler terms or condense several terms back into a single logarithm. Suppose we have the base ten logarithm of the quantity formed by `x` squared times `y`, divided by ten. First, we apply the quotient rule, separating the numerator and denominator with subtraction. Then we apply the product rule to split the numerator into the log of `x` squared plus the log of `y`. Finally, we use the power rule to bring the exponent two down in front of the log of `x`. Since the base ten logarithm of ten is one, the result is two times the log of `x`, plus the log of `y`, minus one. We have successfully expanded a single tangled expression into an easy sum and difference of simpler terms. Condensing goes in reverse, gluing separate logs back together.

Sometimes you encounter a logarithm with a base your calculator does not have buttons for, such as base three or base seven. To evaluate these, we use the change of base formula. This formula states that the logarithm of `x` to the base `b` is equal to the common logarithm of `x` divided by the common logarithm of `b`, or equivalently, the natural logarithm of `x` divided by the natural logarithm of `b`. This means any valid logarithm can be converted into base ten or base `e` with a simple division. With these tools in hand, we are now fully prepared to use our logarithmic laws to dismantle and solve complex equations where the unknown variable is trapped safely inside an exponent.

## Key ideas
- The product rule for logarithms states that the log of a product of positive numbers equals the sum of the individual logs, mirroring the exponent rule for multiplication.
- The quotient rule for logarithms turns division inside a log into subtraction outside, corresponding to the exponent rule for division.
- The power rule allows an exponent inside a logarithm to be pulled out front as a multiplicative coefficient.
- Domain restrictions require that logarithmic bases must be positive and not equal to one, while arguments must always be strictly greater than zero.
- The change of base formula allows any valid logarithmic expression to be evaluated by dividing the log of the argument by the log of the base using a standard calculator base.
