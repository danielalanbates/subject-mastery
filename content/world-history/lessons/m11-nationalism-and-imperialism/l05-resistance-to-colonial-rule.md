# Resistance Movements to Colonial Rule

Imagine standing in your own fields, watching foreign merchants and soldiers stake out boundaries, plant telegraph poles, and demand taxes in a currency you do not use. Across the nineteenth century, as industrial powers rushed to carve up the globe through what we call the New Imperialism, the people living under the shadow of foreign domination did not simply surrender. In our last lesson, we saw how Japan industrialized and turned outward to build its own empire. Today, we turn to the vast regions of India, Africa, and China to explore how indigenous populations fought back, adapted, and tried to reclaim their sovereignty against overwhelming technological and military odds.

Resistance took many forms. People launched massive armed revolts, formed religious movements, wrote intellectual critiques, and organized labor strikes. Scholars often divide these responses into primary resistance, which meant immediate armed clashes against foreign intrusion, and secondary resistance, which involved long-term political organizing, cultural preservation, and modern nationalist movements. Crucially, colonized people were never passive victims. They constantly weighed the risks of open rebellion against the daily indignities of colonial administration, choosing the strategies they believed offered the best chance of survival or victory.

In South Asia, the British East India Company had spent a century tightening its grip through economic exploitation and administrative manipulation. By 1857, tensions boiled over into the Indian Rebellion, sometimes called the Sepoy Mutiny. Indian soldiers in the East India Company's army, known as sepoys, rose up after hearing that their rifle cartridges were greased with animal fat that offended both Hindu and Muslim religious laws. What began as a military mutiny rapidly transformed into a widespread civil uprising involving peasants, artisans, and dispossessed traditional rulers who wanted to restore Mughal authority. Although the British brutally crushed the rebellion by 1858 and abolished the East India Company to rule India directly from London, the event shattered British complacency. It also laid the groundwork for long-term secondary resistance, such as the founding of the Indian National Congress in 1885, which provided a platform for organized political opposition to colonial rule.

On the African continent, where European powers raced to partition territory following the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885, resistance was equally varied and fierce. In West Africa, leaders like Samori Toure built a powerful state and used guerrilla warfare, diplomatic maneuvering, and the relocation of entire populations to frustrate French conquest for more than fifteen years before he was finally captured in 1898. The most famous success came in 1896, when Emperor Menelik II's army defeated an Italian invasion at the Battle of Adwa, keeping Ethiopia independent. In East Africa, the Maji Maji Rebellion of 1905 to 1907 erupted against brutal German forced labor and cotton cultivation policies. Believing a sacred water tonic could protect them from German bullets, diverse ethnic groups united in a spiritual and military uprising. The tragic failure of the uprising, which resulted in devastating famine and loss of life, demonstrated the terrifying lethality of machine guns against traditional weaponry. Over the following decades, African resistance increasingly took the form of political organizing, including early nationalist associations such as the National Congress of British West Africa founded in 1920.

In East Asia, the Qing Dynasty faced a combination of domestic decay and aggressive European and Japanese encroachment. Initially, Chinese officials and intellectuals attempted reform from within, launching the Self-Strengthening Movement to adopt Western military technology while preserving Confucian values, followed by the Hundred Days' Reform of 1898, which sought sweeping modernization of government and education. When these official efforts stalled, some populations turned to traditional martial societies. The Boxer Uprising, which reached its peak in 1900, was driven by rural peasants known as the Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists. They attacked foreign missionaries, thousands of Chinese Christians, railway lines, and the foreign legations in Beijing, aiming to drive out foreign influence and rid China of exploitation. An international force from eight powers captured Beijing in August 1900, suppressed the movement, and imposed crushing financial indemnities on the Qing court, accelerating the dynasty's collapse.

These diverse struggles show that wherever industrial powers imposed their rule, they generated immediate and determined pushback. From the battlefields of India and the savannahs of East Africa to the walled cities and reform halls of China, local populations defended their ways of life with immense courage. Understanding these varied forms of resistance helps explain how modern national identities were forged under pressure, setting the stage for the global conflicts of the twentieth century that would finally challenge the entire imperial system.

## Key ideas
- Resistance to colonial rule across India, Africa, and China took many forms, including armed revolts, guerrilla warfare, and spiritual movements.
- The Indian Rebellion of 1857 transformed local military grievances into a massive civil uprising against British control, eventually leading to organized political bodies like the Indian National Congress founded in 1885.
- African leaders like Samori Toure used mobile warfare and diplomacy to stall European conquest, Ethiopia kept its independence at Adwa, and later resistance turned to political organizing such as the National Congress of British West Africa founded in 1920.
- Chinese reform efforts like the Self-Strengthening Movement and the Hundred Days' Reform gave way to the peasant-led Boxer Uprising, a desperate campaign to purge the country of foreign imperialism.
