# Impulse and Linear Momentum

Imagine catching a speeding baseball with your bare hand. If you keep your hand rigid and stationary, the impact stings sharply, stopping the ball in a fraction of a second with a massive jolt of force. But if you pull your hand backward as the ball lands, letting it slow down over a longer stretch of time, the sting vanishes entirely. The ball ends up with the same mass and the same velocity changes, yet your hand feels an entirely different experience. That everyday contrast between a sudden smash and a gentle cushion gets to the very heart of linear momentum and the forces that change it.

In our earlier study of work and energy, we tracked how forces move through space to shift kinetic and potential energy around. Now we shift our focus from space to time. Linear momentum is a vector quantity, meaning it has both a magnitude and a direction that matches the velocity of the object. Linear momentum measures how hard it is to stop a moving object, depending on both its mass and its velocity. To calculate an object's linear momentum, you simply multiply its mass in kilograms by its velocity in meters per second. A heavy truck rolling slowly down a back street and a tiny bullet flying from a barrel can share the exact same momentum if the low mass of the bullet is balanced by its immense speed, or the low speed of the truck is balanced by its massive mass.

When you want to change an object's momentum, you must apply a net force over a duration of time. Physics calls the product of that average force and the time interval it acts upon the object an impulse. Like force, impulse is a vector quantity whose direction points along the net force. The impulse momentum theorem states that the impulse delivered to an object equals the exact change in its linear momentum. If a large force acts for a very short time, the momentum shifts abruptly. That is what happens in a car crash or a tennis smash. If that exact same total change in momentum is spread out over a longer duration, the required force drops dramatically.

Let us trace a concrete example with round numbers. Suppose a one thousand kilogram car rolls along a straight road at ten meters per second, giving it a linear momentum of ten thousand kilogram meters per second. The driver applies the brakes, bringing the car to a complete stop over a time span of five seconds. To find the braking force, we look at the change in momentum, which is zero minus ten thousand, leaving a negative ten thousand kilogram meters per second. Dividing that change in momentum by the five seconds of braking time gives a constant force of negative two thousand newtons. The negative sign simply indicates that the force points opposite to the direction of motion.

What happens if the brakes fail and the car hits a concrete wall, stopping in just zero point one seconds instead of five seconds? We divide that same change in momentum of negative ten thousand kilogram meters per second by zero point one seconds. The resulting force leaps up to negative one hundred thousand newtons. That massive spike in force explains why sudden impacts cause severe damage while gradual stops allow structures and bodies to handle the load safely. Seatbelts and airbags save lives precisely by extending the collision time, turning a lethal force into a manageable one.

As we move forward, we will see how these rules of force and time apply when multiple objects interact with each other, leading directly to the powerful conservation laws that govern all collisions.

## Key ideas
- Linear momentum is a vector quantity defined as the product of an object's mass and its velocity, pointing in the same direction as the velocity.
- An impulse is a vector quantity defined as the average net force applied to an object multiplied by the time interval during which that force acts.
- The impulse momentum theorem proves that the total impulse delivered to an object is always equal to its resulting change in momentum.
- Extending the time interval over which a collision or stop occurs significantly reduces the magnitude of the force experienced by the object, turning a lethal force into a manageable one.
