# The Binomial Theorem

Imagine you need to expand the expression the quantity x plus y raised to the fifth power. You could certainly multiply the quantity x plus y by itself five times over, distributing every term step by tedious step. But as the exponents grow larger, that multiplication turns into a marathon of algebra. Fortunately, a powerful shortcut exists. Building directly on the combinations we explored in our last lesson, the binomial theorem lets you write out any power of a binomial instantly, revealing a gorgeous hidden structure of numbers that has fascinated mathematicians for centuries.

To see how this works, let us look at smaller powers first. The quantity x plus y raised to the zeroth power is simply one. The quantity x plus y raised to the first power is just x plus y. If you multiply x plus y by itself to get the square, you get x squared plus two x y plus y squared. Multiply by x plus y once more, and the cube becomes x cubed plus three x squared y plus three x y squared plus y cubed. Notice two rules emerging right away. In every single term, the sum of the exponents on x and y always equals the power to which the binomial is raised. Furthermore, the powers of x start at the maximum and count down to zero, while the powers of y start at zero and count up to the maximum.

The real secret lies in the numerical coefficients attached to each term. For the cube, those coefficients are one, three, three, and one. Where do these numbers come from? They are precisely the combinations we studied recently, counting how many ways we can choose items from a group. When we expand the quantity x plus y raised to the nth power, the coefficient of any particular term is a combination number. Specifically, for the term containing x raised to the power of n minus k times y raised to the k, the coefficient is the number of combinations of n items taken k at a time. The reason is that multiplying out n copies of the quantity x plus y means picking either x or y from each copy, and the number of ways to pick y from exactly k of the n copies is the number of combinations of n items taken k at a time. For the cube, there are three ways to pick y from exactly one of the three copies, which is why three x squared y appears. This links the algebra of polynomials directly to the arithmetic of counting.

This pattern of coefficients arranges itself into one of the most famous arrays in mathematics, known as Pascal's triangle. Picture a triangle starting with a single one at the peak. Each row below begins and ends with a one. To find any interior number in the triangle, you simply add the two numbers sitting directly above it in the previous row. The second row reads one and one. The third row reads one, two, and one, giving us the coefficients for our squared binomial. The fourth row reads one, three, three, and one, giving us the cube. Row by row, this triangle generates every binomial coefficient you will ever need, showing how deeply addition and combination are intertwined.

Let us put this theorem into practice by finding a single term in a larger expansion without writing out the whole thing. Suppose we want the term containing x raised to the fifth power in the expansion of the quantity x plus two y raised to the eighth power. First, our total power n is eight. Since our variable x has an exponent of five, the exponent on our second term, two y, must be eight minus five, which is three. That means we need the combination of eight things taken three at a time.

That combination is fifty-six. Now we assemble our full term by taking that coefficient fifty-six, multiplying it by x raised to the fifth power, and multiplying it by the quantity two y cubed. Remember to cube both the two and the y, which gives eight y cubed. Finally, we multiply fifty-six by eight, yielding four hundred forty-eight. So our complete term is four hundred forty-eight x to the fifth y cubed. By focusing only on the specific piece we need, we bypass pages of redundant multiplication.

Understanding how to break down sums and powers gives us a vital tool for analyzing more complex functions and infinite series. As we move ahead to our next lesson, we will turn our attention to basic probability, seeing how counting arrangements and combinations allow us to measure the likelihood of everyday events.

## Key ideas
- The binomial theorem provides a direct shortcut for expanding any power of a binomial without performing extensive manual multiplication.
- In every term of a binomial expansion, the sum of the exponents on the two variables always equals the original power of the binomial.
- The coefficients in a binomial expansion are combinations that count how many ways terms can be arranged.
- Pascal's triangle generates binomial coefficients row by row by adding adjacent numbers from the row above.
