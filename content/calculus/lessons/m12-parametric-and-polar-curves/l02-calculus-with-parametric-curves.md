# Calculus with Parametric Curves

Imagine tracking a roller coaster car as it dips, climbs, and loops through the air. In our last lesson, we learned to describe the path of that car using a pair of equations where both the horizontal and vertical positions depend separately on time. We watched the car move by calculating its velocity and acceleration vectors. But once we know the path of a moving object, natural questions follow. How much ground did the car cover from the start of the ride to the finish, and what is the total area enclosed between its looping path and the horizontal ground below? Today, we answer those questions by bringing the tools of integration to parametric curves.

To find the area under a parametric curve, we build on the standard definite integral we mastered in earlier modules. Recall that the area under a traditional curve is the integral of the height function multiplied by a tiny change in the horizontal position, which we call d x. When our curve is given parametrically, both the vertical height and the horizontal position are functions of our parameter, usually time, which we call t. Let us say the horizontal position is given by x of t, and the vertical height is given by y of t, as t moves from a starting value we call alpha to an ending value we call beta.

Using substitution, we can rewrite that area integral in terms of our parameter t. The tiny change in horizontal position, d x, becomes the derivative of x with respect to t, multiplied by d t. So, the total area under our parametric curve is the definite integral from alpha to beta of y of t, multiplied by the derivative of x of t with respect to t, d t. This formula lets us sweep across the parameter domain, gathering up vertical slices just like we did with single-variable functions, even though the curve itself is defined dynamically.

Let us trace a concrete example. Suppose our horizontal position is given by two t, and our vertical position is given by three t minus t squared, as our parameter t runs from zero to three. Let us find the area under this curve. First, we need the derivative of our horizontal function, x of t, with respect to t. The derivative of two t is simply the constant two. Now we set up our integral from zero to three. The integrand is our vertical function, three t minus t squared, multiplied by our derivative, which is two, all with respect to t.

We can factor out the constant two from the integral. Inside, we integrate term by term. The antiderivative of three t is three halves t squared, and the antiderivative of minus t squared is minus one third t cubed. Let us check this antiderivative by differentiating it with respect to t. The derivative of three halves t squared is three t, and the derivative of minus one third t cubed is minus t squared, which brings us right back to our integrand of three t minus t squared. Evaluating this expression from zero to three, we first plug in the upper limit of three. Three squared is nine, so three halves of nine is twenty-seven halves. Three cubed is twenty-seven, so twenty-seven divided by three is nine. Subtracting nine from twenty-seven halves leaves nine halves. Evaluating at zero yields zero. Finally, we multiply this result of nine halves by the constant two we pulled out earlier, giving us a final area of nine.

Beyond area, we often need to measure the physical length of a path traced by a parametric curve, much like measuring the exact length of a curved wire. The arc length formula comes from the Pythagorean theorem applied to infinitesimal steps along the curve. A tiny hypotenuse of arc length, which we call d s, is the square root of the sum of the squared tiny change in x and the squared tiny change in y. Dividing through by our parameter differential d t, we find that the arc length of a parametric curve from parameter alpha to beta is the definite integral from alpha to beta of the square root of the sum of the squared derivative of x plus the squared derivative of y, all with respect to t.

Consider the path where x of t is the cosine of t and y of t is the sine of t, as t ranges from zero to pi over two. This describes a quarter of a circle. The derivative of the cosine of t is the negative sine of t, and its square is the sine squared of t. The derivative of the sine of t is the cosine of t, and its square is the cosine squared of t. The sum of the sine squared of t and the cosine squared of t is identically one. The square root of one is one. Integrating one with respect to t from zero to pi over two simply gives us pi over two, which is the correct length of a quarter of a unit circle.

Now that we can measure both the area beneath parametric curves and the exact lengths of their paths, we are ready to leave Cartesian and parametric coordinates behind for a while and explore the geometry of angles and radii in polar coordinate systems.

## Key ideas
- Parametric area is calculated by integrating the vertical function multiplied by the derivative of the horizontal function with respect to the parameter.
- The arc length of a parametric curve is found by integrating the square root of the sum of the squared derivatives of both coordinate functions.
- Differentiating an antiderivative before evaluating a definite integral is a quick check that catches errors.
- Both formulas translate traditional single-variable integration techniques into the dynamic world of parameter-driven motion.
