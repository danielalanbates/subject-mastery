# Prenatal Development and Infancy

Think back to your own earliest memory. Most of us find that our mental timeline hits a wall somewhere around age three or four. Before that, we exist in a state of rapid, invisible transformation. Developmental psychology is the field dedicated to understanding this journey. To study this, researchers use two primary designs. In a longitudinal study, they follow the same group of people over many years. This is the gold standard for tracking individual development, though it is time-consuming. Alternatively, researchers use a cross-sectional study, comparing people of different ages at a single point in time. While faster, this method is correlational and can be tricky because it compares people born in different generations who may have had different life experiences.

Our story begins long before we can speak. Prenatal development is divided into three distinct stages. First is the germinal stage, spanning the first two weeks after conception. Next is the embryonic stage, lasting from about two weeks until the end of the second month. This is a critical period; the timing of exposure to environmental hazards, or teratogens, during these weeks determines the specific type of developmental abnormality that might occur. Finally, from the ninth week until birth, we enter the fetal stage. The fetus grows rapidly, and by about six months, the organs are sufficiently developed to provide a chance of survival if the baby is born prematurely.

Throughout these months, the environment matters immensely. Any agent—such as a virus, a drug, or a chemical—that can reach the embryo or fetus and cause harm is a teratogen. Alcohol is a particularly dangerous example. When a pregnant person consumes alcohol, it crosses the placenta, potentially causing fetal alcohol syndrome. This can lead to lifelong physical and cognitive impairments. Because we cannot ethically run experiments on human pregnancies, our knowledge here comes from observational and correlational research. These studies consistently show a strong link between prenatal alcohol exposure and developmental outcomes, making abstinence the safest path.

When an infant arrives, they enter the world with a sophisticated set of tools. Newborns are equipped with reflexes, which are automatic, unlearned responses to specific stimuli. For instance, if you stroke a newborn’s cheek, they will turn toward the touch and begin to suck. This is a survival reflex. Similarly, the grasp reflex causes them to curl their fingers tightly around an object. These reflexes are well-documented through direct observation, providing strong evidence for innate biological programming.

Their senses are also more developed than we once assumed. Newborns show a clear preference for human faces and voices. Their vision is somewhat blurry at birth, but they focus well on objects about eight to twelve inches away. Over the first year, motor skills develop in a predictable sequence. Infants roll over, sit up, crawl, and eventually walk. While this sequence is largely universal, the exact timing varies. It is important to note that while we observe a correlation between physical milestones and brain maturation, these milestones are influenced by a complex interplay of biology and environmental opportunity.

This physical maturation is driven by the brain. At birth, the brain has most of the neurons it will ever have. During the first few years, the number of synapses—the connections between neurons—explodes in a process called synaptogenesis. This is followed by synaptic pruning, where the brain clears away unused connections to increase neural efficiency. Simultaneously, the brain undergoes myelination, a process where fatty sheaths insulate neural pathways. This improves the speed of signals, supporting the maturation of motor and sensory systems. These findings are supported by longitudinal neuroimaging studies, which provide strong evidence for the structural changes occurring throughout infancy. As infants gain control over their bodies, they lay the foundation for the bonds they will form with those around them. These early physical and sensory milestones serve as the primary laboratory for a child’s later emotional and social growth.

## Key ideas

- Developmental psychologists rely on longitudinal and cross-sectional studies to track how physical and cognitive changes unfold from the prenatal period through infancy.
- The embryonic stage is a critical period where the timing of exposure to teratogens determines the nature of potential developmental abnormalities.
- Newborns possess innate, unlearned reflexes that are essential for immediate survival and early interaction with their environment.
- Postnatal brain development involves a sequence of synaptogenesis, synaptic pruning, and ongoing myelination, which together support the maturation of motor and sensory systems.
