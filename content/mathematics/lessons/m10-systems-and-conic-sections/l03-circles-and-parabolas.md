# Circles and Parabolas

Imagine you are standing in an open field, tethered to a single stake by a rope exactly five feet long. If you walk in a complete circle while keeping that rope pulled tight, you are tracing a path where every single point is exactly five feet away from that central stake. This geometric definition is the heart of a circle. In the coordinate plane, we use the distance formula to translate this physical reality into algebra. The distance between any point, which we call x and y, and a fixed center, which we call h and k, must always equal a constant radius, r. By squaring both sides of the distance formula, we arrive at the standard equation of a circle: the quantity x minus h, all squared plus the quantity y minus k, all squared equals r squared.

This equation is elegant because it mirrors the Pythagorean theorem. It tells us that for any point on the edge of the circle, the horizontal distance from the center squared plus the vertical distance from the center squared must equal the radius squared. In our last lesson, we used matrices to solve complex systems of linear equations. Now, we shift our focus from linear systems to these curved shapes, which are technically known as conic sections because they can be formed by slicing through a cone with a plane.

Sometimes, the equation of a circle is not given in that neat, factored form. You might see an expression like x squared plus y squared minus six x plus four y minus three equals zero. To identify the center and radius here, we use the technique of completing the square. First, group the x terms together and the y terms together, moving the constant three to the other side. For the x terms, x squared minus six x, we take half of negative six, which is negative three, and square it to get nine. We add nine to both sides. Adding the same value to both sides preserves equality because it maintains the balance of the equation. For the y terms, y squared plus four y, we take half of four, which is two, and square it to get four. We add four to both sides. Now, the equation reads the quantity x minus three, all squared plus the quantity y plus two, all squared equals sixteen. We can immediately see the center is three and negative two, and because sixteen is r squared, the radius must be four. After completing the square, we should always verify the center and radius by substituting them back into the original equation to ensure they satisfy it.

While a circle is defined by a single point, a parabola is defined by a relationship between a point and a line. Imagine a fixed point called the focus and a fixed line called the directrix. A parabola is the set of all points that are equidistant from the focus and the directrix. If you move closer to the focus, you must move closer to the directrix to maintain that balance. This creates the classic U-shape that opens upward, downward, left, or right.

Consider a simple vertical parabola where the focus is at zero and two, and the directrix is the horizontal line y equals negative two. A point x and y on this parabola must be the same distance from the focus as it is from the line y equals negative two. The distance to the focus is the square root of x squared plus the quantity y minus two, all squared. The distance to the directrix is the absolute value of the vertical distance, which is the absolute value of the quantity y minus negative two, or the absolute value of the quantity y plus two. If we set these distances equal and square both sides, we are validly squaring because both sides represent non-negative distances. This gives x squared plus the quantity y minus two, all squared equals the quantity y plus two, all squared. Expanding both sides gives x squared plus y squared minus four y plus four equals y squared plus four y plus four. By subtracting y squared and four from both sides, we are left with x squared minus four y equals four y, which simplifies to x squared equals eight y. Dividing by eight, we find y equals one eighth x squared. More generally, for a vertical parabola's equation y equals the fraction one over four p times x squared, the coefficient shows that p is the distance from the vertex to the focus and also to the directrix.

Whether we are pinning down the radius of a circle or tracking the path of a projectile defined by a focus and directrix, we are describing how points behave under specific geometric constraints. These shapes form the foundation of our study of non-linear relationships. In our next lesson, we will extend these ideas to define ellipses and hyperbolas, which describe even more complex paths in the coordinate plane.

## Key ideas
- A circle is defined as the set of all points in a plane that remain at a fixed distance, called the radius, from a central point.
- The standard equation of a circle, the quantity x minus h, all squared plus the quantity y minus k, all squared equals r squared, is derived directly from the distance formula.
- Completing the square allows us to transform a general quadratic equation into the standard form of a circle, revealing its center and radius, which can be verified by substitution.
- A parabola is defined as the set of all points that are equidistant from a fixed point known as the focus and a fixed line known as the directrix, where the distance to the directrix is an absolute value.
