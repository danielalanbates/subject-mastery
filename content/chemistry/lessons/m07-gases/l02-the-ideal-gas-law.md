# The Ideal Gas Law

Imagine you are filling a balloon with air for a party. As you pump more air inside, the balloon expands. If you pinch the neck of the balloon, you can feel the pressure pushing back against your fingers. In our last lesson, we explored how pressure, volume, and temperature behave when you hold certain variables constant. But what happens when all of these factors change at the same time? To answer this, we need a single, powerful equation that connects them all: the ideal gas law.

The ideal gas law states that pressure times volume equals the number of moles times the universal gas constant times the temperature. This equation works perfectly for a hypothetical substance called an ideal gas, which consists of particles that occupy no volume and have no attractions for one another. While real gases are not truly ideal, they behave very similarly under most everyday conditions, making this law an essential tool for chemists. When you use this law, you must always express temperature in kelvin and use absolute units for pressure, such as atmospheres or bars. The constant in this equation, the ideal gas constant, acts as the bridge that relates these physical quantities. Its value depends on the units we choose for pressure and volume, but a very common value is zero point zero eight two one liter atmospheres per mole kelvin.

To use this equation effectively, we must agree on a standard reference point for our measurements. We call this standard temperature and pressure. You should be aware that there are two common definitions for these conditions. The older convention defines standard temperature as zero degrees Celsius and standard pressure as exactly one atmosphere. A more modern definition from the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry sets the standard pressure at one bar. Under these conditions, the molar volume of an ideal gas is approximately twenty-two point four liters per mole at one atmosphere, or twenty-two point seven liters per mole at one bar. Always check which definition your source is using, as it can subtly change your calculations.

We can use the ideal gas law to find properties of a gas that are not immediately obvious, such as its density or its molar mass. Density is simply the mass of a substance divided by its volume. By rearranging the ideal gas law, we can express density as pressure times the molar mass of the gas, all divided by the gas constant times the temperature. This reveals a pattern: the density of a gas is directly proportional to its molar mass and its pressure, but inversely proportional to its temperature. If you increase the pressure, you squeeze more mass into the same space, increasing density. If you heat the gas up, the particles spread out, decreasing density.

Let us walk through a calculation to see this in action. Suppose we have a sample of helium gas with a mass of four grams at a temperature of three hundred kelvin and a pressure of one atmosphere. To find the volume, we first convert the mass of helium to moles. Since one mole of helium has a mass of four grams, we have exactly one mole of gas. Using our ideal gas law, we multiply the number of moles, one, by the gas constant, zero point zero eight two one, and then by the temperature, three hundred kelvin. This gives us a product of twenty-four point six three. We then divide this by the pressure of one atmosphere, which tells us that the volume of our gas is twenty-four point six three liters.

We can also determine the identity of an unknown gas by finding its molar mass. If we know the mass, pressure, volume, and temperature of a gas, we rearrange the ideal gas law to solve for moles. Once we have the number of moles, we divide the original mass of the sample by that number. The result is the molar mass in grams per mole. By comparing this value to the periodic table, we can identify our unknown gas. This method is useful in the laboratory when you are trying to characterize a new substance or confirm the identity of a gas collected during an experiment.

## Key ideas

- The ideal gas law relates pressure, volume, moles, and temperature through the universal gas constant to describe the state of a gas.
- Calculations using the ideal gas law require temperature to be in kelvin and pressure to be in absolute units.
- Standard temperature and pressure is a reference point that defines the molar volume of an ideal gas as approximately twenty-two point four liters per mole at one atmosphere or twenty-two point seven liters per mole at one bar.
- The ideal gas law can be rearranged to calculate the density of a gas or to determine the molar mass of an unknown substance.
