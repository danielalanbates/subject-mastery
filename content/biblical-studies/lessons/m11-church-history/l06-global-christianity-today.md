# Global Christianity Today

Imagine walking into a bustling Sunday morning church service in Lagos, Nigeria, or Seoul, South Korea, or São Paulo, Brazil. The music is vibrant, the sanctuary is packed to overflowing with people of all ages, and the energy is palpable. Now, compare that image with the quiet, historic cathedrals of Western Europe, where attendance has steadily declined over the past century. As we discussed in our previous studies, the Enlightenment and subsequent modern intellectual shifts introduced deep secularizing pressures across the West. But while Western Christianity was wrestling with rationalism and institutional decline, a breathtaking demographic revolution was quietly unfolding across the rest of the world.

This brings us to the defining turning point of modern church history, which is the massive shift of Christianity's geographic and cultural center of gravity from the Global North to the Global South. For centuries, following the European expansions of the sixteenth century, Christianity was largely identified with Western civilization. European colonial powers often exported the Christian faith alongside political empire. By the dawn of the twentieth century, the vast majority of the world's Christians lived in Europe and North America. Today, that old map has been completely redrawn. Sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and parts of Asia now host the majority of the world's over two billion Christians, altering the face of the religion forever.

This historic transformation was driven by several powerful engines, chief among them being the phenomenal rise of Pentecostalism and charismatic Christianity. Beginning with movements like the Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles in 1906, Pentecostalism emphasized the immediate, experiential presence of the Holy Spirit, dynamic spiritual gifts, divine healing, and passionate, expressive worship. While traditional mainline Protestant denominations and historic Roman Catholicism grew slowly or shrank in the West, Pentecostal and charismatic movements exploded across the Global South. They proved remarkably adaptable, fusing easily with local cultures, idioms, and worldviews while offering a resilient faith that addressed the immediate spiritual and physical struggles of everyday people. Scholars of religion note that this experiential focus resonated deeply with non-Western cultures that already possessed a rich appreciation for the supernatural.

Today, the global Christian landscape stands as a rich, complex mosaic of traditions. Broadly speaking, global Christianity is anchored by three massive historic branches. First is the Roman Catholic Church, which remains the single largest Christian body in the world, with explosive growth now occurring in African and Asian dioceses rather than in its traditional European heartland. Second is Eastern Orthodoxy, deeply rooted in Eastern Europe, Greece, Russia, and an expanding global diaspora, preserving ancient liturgical worship and patristic theology across changing political borders. Third is Protestantism, which itself represents a vast universe of theological expressions, ranging from historic Lutheran, Reformed, and Anglican bodies to the surging tide of independent Pentecostal and evangelical churches. Alongside these stand the ancient Oriental Orthodox churches, such as the Coptic, Ethiopian and Armenian churches, which trace their roots to the first Christian centuries.

This decentralized landscape means that Christian theology and practice are no longer dictated primarily by Western academic institutions. Instead, theologians and church leaders in Nairobi, Seoul, and Buenos Aires are asking fresh questions, engaging deeply with local religious traditions, and reading the scriptures through new cultural lenses. Issues of social justice, poverty, economic survival, and spiritual warfare take center stage in sermons preached from Seoul to Santiago, reshaping the global Christian witness for the twenty-first century. This decentralized reality reminds us that Christianity has always possessed a remarkable capacity for cultural translation, taking root in every soil it encounters.

Understanding how the church arrived at this vibrant, diverse global reality helps us appreciate the resilience of the Christian movement across two millennia. Yet, as we trace this vast historical narrative from ancient apostolic communities to modern global networks, fundamental questions remain about how Christians defend the rationality of their faith in an increasingly interconnected world. Next, we will turn our attention to the classical philosophical arguments for the existence of God.

## Key ideas
- The geographic and cultural center of Christianity has shifted dramatically from the Global North to the Global South over the past century.
- The rise of Pentecostal and charismatic movements has been a primary engine of this explosive growth, emphasizing experiential faith and spiritual gifts.
- The global church today is a diverse mosaic composed of Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and a vast spectrum of Protestant and independent traditions.
- Theology in the twenty-first century is increasingly shaped by non-Western perspectives, addressing local cultural contexts, poverty, and spiritual warfare.
