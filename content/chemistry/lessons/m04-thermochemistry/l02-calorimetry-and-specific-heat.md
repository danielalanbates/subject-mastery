# Calorimetry and Specific Heat

Imagine you are holding a cold metal spoon that has been sitting in a freezer, and you dip it into a steaming cup of hot coffee. Almost instantly, the spoon warms up, and the coffee cools down slightly. You know intuitively that energy has moved from the hot liquid to the cold metal. But how much energy moved? To answer that, we need a way to measure the flow of heat, which is exactly what the science of calorimetry allows us to do.

In our last lesson, we defined heat as the transfer of thermal energy. To quantify that transfer, we first look at heat capacity. Think of heat capacity as an object’s resistance to changing its temperature. If you have a massive iron skillet, it takes a long time to heat up on the stove. A tiny copper wire, however, heats up in a fraction of a second. The specific heat capacity is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of one gram of a substance by one degree Celsius. Water, for instance, has a high specific heat, which is why it takes so long to boil a pot of pasta compared to heating up an empty metal pan.

We calculate the heat gained or lost using a simple but powerful equation. The heat is equal to the mass of the substance, multiplied by its specific heat capacity, multiplied by the change in temperature. If you have fifty grams of water and you want to raise its temperature by ten degrees Celsius, you multiply fifty grams by the specific heat of water, which is four point one eight joules per gram per degree Celsius, and then by ten degrees. The result is two thousand and ninety joules of energy. This calculation assumes that all the energy added went directly into warming the water, which is the core principle of calorimetry.

A calorimeter is a device designed to measure this heat exchange. The simplest version is often called a coffee-cup calorimeter. It is essentially two nested polystyrene cups with a lid. Because polystyrene is an excellent insulator, it prevents heat from escaping into the room. We place a reaction inside the water, and we measure the temperature change of the water itself. Since we know the mass of the water and its specific heat, we can determine exactly how much energy the reaction released or absorbed. If the water temperature goes up, the reaction released heat, meaning the process was exothermic. If the water temperature drops, the reaction absorbed heat, making it endothermic.

For reactions that involve gases or high-pressure combustion, we use a bomb calorimeter. Instead of an open cup, the reaction takes place inside a sealed, heavy-duty steel container called the bomb. This bomb is submerged in a large, insulated bucket of water. We ignite the sample inside the bomb, and the heat released is transferred to the surrounding water. Because the bomb is rigid, the volume cannot change, and the heat measured represents the total energy released by the combustion. This is how scientists determine the caloric content of the food you eat. By burning a sample of food and measuring the temperature rise in the water bath, they calculate the energy content in kilojoules.

Let us walk through a quick example. Suppose you drop a twenty-gram piece of hot metal into fifty grams of water that is at twenty degrees Celsius. The water temperature rises to twenty-five degrees. The water gained heat, and we calculate that by multiplying fifty grams by four point one eight joules per gram per degree Celsius, multiplied by the five-degree temperature increase. That gives us one thousand and forty-five joules of heat gained by the water. Because the system is insulated, we know the metal lost that exact same amount of heat. By plugging those numbers back into our equation, we could even determine the specific heat capacity of that unknown metal.

Calorimetry provides us with the raw data needed to understand the energy balance of chemical systems. By measuring the heat flow in these controlled environments, we can begin to predict how energy is stored and released in complex chemical bonds. This measurement of heat at constant pressure is the foundation for understanding enthalpy, which we will explore in our next lesson as we learn to write and balance thermochemical equations.

## Key ideas

- Specific heat capacity measures how much energy is required to raise the temperature of one gram of a substance by one degree Celsius.
- The heat gained or lost by a substance is calculated by multiplying its mass, its specific heat, and the change in temperature.
- A coffee-cup calorimeter measures heat at constant pressure, while a bomb calorimeter is used for reactions that occur at constant volume.
- According to the law of conservation of energy, the heat lost by a system must be equal to the heat gained by its surroundings in an insulated calorimeter.
