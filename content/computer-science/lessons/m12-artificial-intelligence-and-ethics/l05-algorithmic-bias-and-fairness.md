# Algorithmic Bias and Fairness

Imagine you are applying for a home loan. You fill out the forms, submit your financial history, and wait. Within seconds, a computer program denies your application. You are left wondering why. Was it your credit score? Your income? Or did the software make a decision based on patterns in historical data that do not actually reflect your ability to repay the loan? As we have seen, artificial intelligence systems learn from vast amounts of existing information. When that information contains human prejudices, the resulting algorithm often mirrors those same flaws, a phenomenon we call algorithmic bias.

Bias enters an algorithm through many channels. While historical data is a primary culprit, we must also consider measurement bias, where the data collected does not accurately represent the reality it intends to measure. We also see aggregation bias, which occurs when a single model is forced to fit diverse populations, ignoring meaningful differences between groups. Furthermore, developers often use proxy variables, which are seemingly neutral data points—like a zip code—that act as stand-ins for protected characteristics like race or socioeconomic status. The computer does not know these past choices were influenced by societal failings; it simply identifies correlations and treats them as the gold standard for future decisions. In this way, the algorithm automates and scales up the inequities of the past, masking them behind a veneer of mathematical objectivity.

Measuring fairness is one of the most difficult challenges in computer science today. There is no single, universal definition of what it means for an algorithm to be fair, and researchers have proven that it is mathematically impossible to satisfy all common fairness metrics simultaneously. Demographic parity requires that the proportion of positive outcomes be equal across different groups. Equal opportunity focuses on ensuring that true positive rates are the same for everyone, regardless of their background. Predictive parity demands that the probability of a positive outcome be the same for individuals with similar risk scores, while calibration seeks to ensure that a predicted probability of success means the same thing for every group. Because these definitions often conflict, choosing one often forces a trade-off that disadvantages another.

Consider a system designed to predict the likelihood of someone committing a crime, which is sometimes used to assist in setting bail. If the training data includes arrest records that are skewed because of over-policing in specific neighborhoods, the algorithm will learn that residents of those neighborhoods are higher risks. The algorithm is not looking at an individual’s actions, but at the statistical profile of their environment. When this happens, we see how a tool intended to make justice more efficient can inadvertently perpetuate systemic harm. The computer is doing exactly what it was told to do, but the patterns it finds are poisoned by the reality of the society that produced them.

To combat these issues, engineers use various mitigation strategies. Pre-processing involves cleaning or re-weighting the training data before the model ever sees it. In-processing strategies involve changing the learning algorithm itself to penalize biased outcomes during the training phase. Finally, post-processing involves adjusting the final outputs of the model to ensure they meet specific fairness criteria.

So, who is responsible when these automated decisions go wrong? This is a question of accountability that spans law, engineering, and ethics. Some argue the responsibility lies with the developers who build the models, while others point to the organizations that deploy these systems, noting that they often prioritize speed over rigorous testing. Then there are the policymakers, who must decide how to regulate technologies that are often too complex for the average person to understand. As of the mid-2020s, there is a growing consensus that we need a combination of technical auditing, diverse development teams, and legal frameworks to ensure that automated systems remain accountable.

Addressing bias is not just about fixing a line of code. It requires us to acknowledge that technology is never truly neutral. Every algorithm reflects the priorities and the blind spots of its creators. By being transparent about how our systems are built and by constantly questioning the data we use, we can work toward tools that are not just efficient, but equitable. As we move forward, we must consider how these technical decisions ripple outward, shaping the broader structures of our community and the ways we interact with one another in a digital world.

## Key ideas

- Algorithmic bias occurs when artificial intelligence systems learn to replicate human prejudices or structural flaws present in the training data.
- Bias arises from multiple sources, including measurement errors, the use of proxy variables, and the aggregation of diverse groups into a single model.
- Fairness metrics such as demographic parity and equal opportunity are mathematically incompatible, meaning developers must make difficult trade-offs when designing systems.
- Mitigation strategies can be applied at different stages of the development process, including pre-processing the data, modifying the algorithm during training, or adjusting the final outputs.
- Accountability for automated decisions is a shared responsibility among developers, organizations, and policymakers, requiring both technical and social solutions.
