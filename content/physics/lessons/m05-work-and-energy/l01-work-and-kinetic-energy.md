# Work and Kinetic Energy

Imagine you are pushing a heavy crate across a smooth, flat floor. In our earlier study of forces and Newton's laws, we focused on the push itself and how it creates acceleration. But today, we want to ask a different question. Where does that effort actually go? When you exert a force on an object over a distance, you are doing physical work on it. In physics, work is not about labor or fatigue. Work is a specific mechanism for transferring energy into or out of a system. By applying a force while something moves, you change its state of motion, feeding energy into the object and altering its speed.

To understand this mathematically, we look at how force and displacement interact. When a constant force pushes an object in the exact direction the object travels, the work done on that object equals the force multiplied by the distance. If the force pushes at an angle to the path of motion, the work equals the force multiplied by the distance multiplied by the cosine of the angle between them. For a force that changes as the object moves, the work equals the area under a graph of force versus position. We measure work in joules. One joule of work is done when a force of one newton moves an object a distance of one meter in the direction of the force.

When you do positive work on an object, you are transferring energy to it. What happens to that energy? If the object is free to move, that transferred energy shows up as an increase in its speed. Physicists call the energy of motion kinetic energy. Kinetic energy is a scalar quantity, meaning it has magnitude but no direction, and its unit, the joule, is identical to the unit of work. The kinetic energy of an object depends on two things, its mass and the square of its speed. Specifically, kinetic energy equals one half multiplied by the mass multiplied by the square of the speed. This relationship tells us that doubling an object's mass doubles its kinetic energy, but doubling its speed quadruples its kinetic energy because speed is squared.

The link between these two concepts is the work-energy theorem. This fundamental principle follows directly from Newton's second law by connecting net force, mass, acceleration, and displacement. The theorem states that the total work done on an object by the net force of all forces acting on the object equals the exact change in that object's kinetic energy. It is vital to consider the net work of every force acting on the object, rather than just a single applied force. If you push something forward while friction resists, the net work is the combined effect of both forces. If you push something forward, you do positive net work, and its kinetic energy increases. If you apply a force against its motion, you do negative work, removing kinetic energy and slowing the object down. Work is the bridge that transfers energy, and kinetic energy is the ledger where that motion is recorded.

Let us walk through a practical example. Suppose you are pushing a sled with a mass of fifty kilograms across a frozen pond, starting from rest. You apply a constant horizontal force of one hundred newtons over a distance of four meters, ignoring any friction. To find the work done, you multiply the force of one hundred newtons by the distance of four meters, giving four hundred joules of work. According to the work-energy theorem, those four hundred joules are transferred directly into the kinetic energy of the sled.

Now we can determine how fast the sled is moving at the end of that push. We know the kinetic energy is four hundred joules, and we know the mass is fifty kilograms. The formula for kinetic energy tells us that four hundred joules equals one half multiplied by fifty kilograms multiplied by the square of the speed. Multiplying one half by fifty gives twenty-five. Dividing four hundred by twenty-five leaves sixteen. Since sixteen is the square of the speed, the final speed of the sled is four meters per second. This is a moderate jogging speed, so our answer makes physical sense. You have successfully used the concept of work to calculate a change in motion.

As we continue exploring this landscape, we will find that energy does not only manifest as motion. Energy can also be stored safely away in arrangements of objects and forces, waiting to be released.

## Key ideas
- Work is the transfer of energy to or from an object, calculated as force multiplied by distance and the cosine of the angle between them, or as the area under a force versus position graph for variable forces.
- The work-energy theorem follows directly from Newton's second law, linking net force, acceleration, and energy.
- Kinetic energy is a scalar quantity representing the energy of motion, and its unit, the joule, is identical to the unit of work.
- The work-energy theorem requires the net work of all forces acting on the object, rather than just a single applied force.
