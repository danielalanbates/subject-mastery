# Proof by Contradiction

Imagine you walk into a kitchen and see a bowl of fruit that you know was empty five minutes ago, now containing a single fresh apple. You look around and see no one else in the house. Your mind immediately runs through the possibilities. If it appeared by magic, the laws of physics are broken. If someone brought it in, they must have entered unseen. By systematically eliminating every other scenario, you are left with an inescapable conclusion. In the previous lesson, we looked at direct proof, where you march straight from a starting assumption to a guaranteed result. But sometimes, marching straight ahead leads to a swamp. Today, we explore a wonderfully clever detour known as proof by contradiction, or reductio ad absurdum, which means reduction to an absurdity.

To prove a statement by contradiction, you begin by assuming the exact opposite of what you want to prove. You take this negation, treat it as true, and follow the rules of logic step by step to see where it leads. If your assumption forces you into a mathematical impossibility—such as a number being both even and odd, or a fraction equalling something it logically cannot—then your initial assumption must have been false. And if the negation is false, the original statement must be true. It is a brilliant form of intellectual jujutsu, using an opponent's premise to throw them down.

Let us look at one of the most famous proofs in the history of human thought, attributed to the ancient Greeks: proving that the square root of two is irrational. An irrational number is simply a number that cannot be written as a simple fraction of two whole numbers in lowest terms. To prove that the square root of two cannot be written this way, we start by assuming the opposite. We assume that the square root of two is rational.

If our assumption is true, then the square root of two can be expressed as the ratio of two whole numbers, let us call them variable p and variable q, where we have already canceled any common factors so that the fraction is in lowest terms. If we square both sides of this equation, we get two equals p squared over q squared. Multiply both sides by q squared, and we find that two times q squared equals p squared. This tells us that p squared must be an even number because it is two times something. For example, two and its square, four, are both even. More generally, the square of an odd integer is odd, so an integer with an even square must itself be even.

Now, because p is even, we can write p as two times another whole number, let us call it variable k. Substitute two times k in place of p in our equation, and we get two times q squared equals the quantity two times k squared, which is four times k squared. Divide both sides by two, and we discover that q squared equals two times k squared. This means q squared is also even, which means q must be even as well. But here is our contradiction. We started by stating that our fraction was in lowest terms, meaning p and q did not share any common factors. Yet our logical chain just proved that both p and q are even, meaning both share a factor of two. Since our assumption led to this impossible collision, the assumption must be wrong. The square root of two cannot be written as a fraction; it is stubbornly, beautifully irrational.

Another masterclass in this technique is Euclid’s proof that there are infinitely many prime numbers. A prime number is a whole number greater than one whose only positive divisors are one and itself. To prove that the supply of these numbers never runs out, we assume the opposite, namely, that there is a finite list of all prime numbers. If the list is finite, we can multiply every single prime on that list together and add one to the product. Look closely at this new number. When you divide it by any prime on your supposedly complete list, you always get a remainder of one. Therefore, this new number cannot be divisible by any prime on your list. It must either be a new prime itself, or it must be divisible by a prime not found on your list. In either case, our finite list was incomplete. Our assumption of a finite list collapses, proving that primes go on forever.

Proof by contradiction forces us to accept truths that our eyes cannot always see by revealing the impossible nature of their alternatives.

## Key ideas
- Proof by contradiction works by assuming the negation of a statement and following logical steps until an impossibility or absurdity is reached.
- Because an assumption cannot lead to a valid impossibility without a flaw, the original negation is proven false, establishing the truth of the original statement.
- Proving that the square root of two is irrational relies on assuming it can be written as a fraction in lowest terms, which forces both parts of the fraction to be even.
- Euclid proved infinitely many prime numbers by showing that assuming a finite list of primes leads to a number not divisible by any of them.
