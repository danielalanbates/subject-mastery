# The Course of the War

Imagine standing on a hillside in Virginia during the summer of 1861. You are watching thousands of men in mismatched uniforms—some blue, some gray, some civilian clothes—clashing in a disorganized frenzy. This was the First Battle of Bull Run. Both sides expected a short, romantic struggle that would end in a single glorious afternoon. They were wrong. The carnage at Bull Run shattered the illusion of a quick victory and signaled the beginning of a long, industrial-scale war that would transform the nation forever.

To understand how this conflict unfolded, we must look at the uneven balance of power. The North, or the Union, possessed immense advantages in resources. It had a larger population, an extensive network of railroads, and the vast majority of the country’s manufacturing capacity. The South, or the Confederacy, started with a smaller population and limited industry. However, the Confederacy fought a defensive war on its own home turf, requiring only that it survive until the North lost its will to fight. It also benefited from talented military leadership early in the war and the hope that European powers might intervene to secure Southern cotton.

The war effort was not merely the work of soldiers. Women played vital roles, serving as nurses in field hospitals and organizing the United States Sanitary Commission, which revolutionized medical care and supply distribution for Union troops. Immigrants, many of whom had recently arrived in the United States, filled the ranks of the Union army in large numbers, providing essential manpower for the industrial North.

The early years of the war were defined by a brutal stalemate in the East and a slow, grinding advance in the West. In September 1862, the Battle of Antietam in Maryland served as a critical turning point. It remains the single bloodiest day in American history, with about twenty-three thousand total casualties. While the battle was tactically inconclusive, the Confederate retreat gave President Abraham Lincoln the political capital he needed to move toward a more radical war goal: the destruction of slavery. Following the Emancipation Proclamation, nearly two hundred thousand African American men joined the Union army and navy. Their presence transformed the conflict into a war for human freedom and provided the Union with a vital influx of soldiers who fought with immense courage.

By 1863, the war shifted in favor of the Union. In the West, General Ulysses S. Grant laid siege to Vicksburg, Mississippi. When the city surrendered on July 4, 1863, the Union gained total control of the Mississippi River, effectively splitting the Confederacy in two. Simultaneously, in the East, General Robert E. Lee led his army into Pennsylvania. At the Battle of Gettysburg, the two armies collided for three days of intense combat. The Union repelled a final, desperate assault known as Pickett’s Charge, forcing Lee to retreat. Gettysburg marked the last time the Confederacy would launch a major offensive in the North.

In 1864, General Grant launched his Overland Campaign in Virginia, a series of relentless, bloody battles against Lee’s forces. Unlike previous commanders, Grant refused to retreat after heavy losses, instead forcing Lee into a defensive posture. This culminated in the Siege of Petersburg, where the two armies dug into trenches for months, foreshadowing the static warfare of the next century. Meanwhile, General William Tecumseh Sherman led his forces through Georgia, marching from Atlanta to the Atlantic coast. His troops destroyed railroads and dismantled the economic infrastructure that supported the Confederate army. This was a psychological blow designed to bring the reality of the war to the Southern population.

Why did the Union ultimately win? The North’s superior industrial output allowed it to replace men and equipment at a rate the South could not match. The Union’s naval blockade, known as the Anaconda Plan, slowly strangled the Confederate economy. By the spring of 1865, the Confederate armies were exhausted. General Lee surrendered to General Grant at Appomattox Court House in Virginia. The war had claimed the lives of about six hundred and twenty thousand soldiers. The nation had been preserved, but the cost was staggering, and the work of reuniting a shattered society was only just beginning.

## Key ideas

- The Union’s victory was built upon its superior industrial capacity, a relentless naval blockade, and the ability to sustain political resolve through years of heavy casualties.
- The Battle of Antietam provided the political opening for the Emancipation Proclamation, which led to the enlistment of nearly two hundred thousand African American soldiers who became essential to the Union war effort.
- Women and immigrant workers were central to the war, providing critical labor in hospitals, supply organizations, and the industrial factories that fueled the Union army.
- The military turning points of 1863, specifically the victories at Vicksburg and Gettysburg, were followed by Grant’s attritional Overland Campaign and Sherman’s total war strategy, which systematically broke the Confederacy’s ability to resist.
