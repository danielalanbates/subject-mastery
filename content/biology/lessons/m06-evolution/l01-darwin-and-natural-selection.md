# Darwin and Natural Selection

Imagine walking through a forest where every single tree trunk is darkened by decades of industrial soot. High up on the bark, a moth rests its wings. If you were a hungry bird searching for a meal, which moth would you spot first—the pale, speckled one, or the pitch-dark one? That simple question captures the observable reality of natural selection. Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace first set out the idea in 1858, and Darwin developed it in his 1859 book On the Origin of Species. The peppered moth became a famous later example, studied in detail by the biologist Bernard Kettlewell in the 1950s. In our last lesson, we looked at how modern biotechnology lets us read and edit the code of life through genomics. Today, we step back to ask how that living code gets shaped over vast stretches of time by the pressures of the natural world.

To understand how evolution happens, we have to look past the individual organism and think about populations. Think of a population as a dynamic group of individuals of the same species living in a particular area, all sharing a pool of genetic variations. In any wild population, individuals are not identical. Thanks to random mutations in their DNA, which we explored in earlier genetics modules, they differ in color, size, speed, and countless other traits. At the same time, nature is full of limits. Food is scarce, predators are hungry, and diseases lurk. Because of these pressures, more offspring are born than can possibly survive and reproduce. This sets the stage for a grand competition.

Darwin realized that this competition is not random. It is filtered through what he called natural selection. When individuals possess heritable traits—traits passed down through genes—that give them even a slight edge in their environment, they are more likely to survive and leave behind offspring. Let us return to our soot-stained forest. Before the industrial revolution, pale moths blended in well with pale lichens on the trees, while dark moths stood out and were eaten. But as pollution darkened the bark, the survival tables turned completely. The rare dark moths suddenly blended into the background, while the pale ones stood out like neon signs. Over generations, the dark moths survived better, reproduced more, and passed their dark-color genes to their descendants. The population shifted.

Notice what is happening here on a biological level. Natural selection acts on the physical traits, or phenotypes, of individuals, but its evolutionary consequence falls on the gene pool of the population. An individual organism does not evolve during its own lifetime. A moth cannot decide to turn dark because the trees are dirty. Instead, the proportions of inherited traits shift across generations as certain genetic variants become more common while others fade away. This differential reproductive success defines what biologists call fitness: the relative ability of an individual to survive and pass its genes on to the next generation compared to others in the population. It is crucial to remember that natural selection does not act for the good of the species as a whole, nor does it produce perfectly optimized organisms. It merely favors whatever variations happen to work best in a given, constantly changing environment at a particular moment in time. Adaptation is both the process by which a population becomes better suited to its habitat and the resulting feature that makes survival possible.

Darwin developed these insights while observing the strange and wonderful wildlife of the Galapagos Islands, noting how mockingbirds and finches varied subtly from one island to another. Yet he lacked a key piece of the puzzle: he knew traits were inherited, but he did not know about genes, DNA, or how mutations arise. Decades later, modern scientists combined Darwinian natural selection with Mendelian genetics to form what we call the modern synthesis. This unified framework shows us that mutation provides the raw, random genetic variation, while natural selection acts as the non-random filter shaping that variation into functional traits. As we move forward into our next lesson, we will examine the physical and biological evidence—from ancient fossils to shared embryonic structures—that allows scientists to trace these historical shifts across the tree of life.

## Key ideas
- Natural selection is the process by which heritable traits that improve survival and reproduction become more common in successive generations.
- Populations evolve over time through shifts in their gene pools, measured by fitness as relative reproductive success, even though individual organisms do not change their own genetic makeup during their lifetimes.
- Natural selection does not act for the good of the species or produce flawless, perfect designs, but simply filters existing variations based on immediate survival and reproduction.
- Environmental pressures such as predators, scarce resources, and disease act as filters that determine which genetic variations lead to differential reproductive success.
- Adaptation refers to both the evolutionary process of matching a population to its environment and the specific traits that result from this process.
