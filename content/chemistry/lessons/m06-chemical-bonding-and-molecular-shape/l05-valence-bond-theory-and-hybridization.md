# Valence Bond Theory and Hybridization

Imagine you are trying to shake hands with someone, but you are both wearing bulky oven mitts. You can still make contact, but the connection is awkward and lacks the precision of bare fingers. In the world of atoms, bonding is a bit like that. We know from our previous look at molecular shapes that atoms arrange themselves in specific geometries, like the perfect pyramid of methane. But why do they choose these shapes? If we look only at the raw atomic orbitals we studied earlier—those spheres and dumbbells—the math does not account for the observed bond angles. To explain how atoms link arms to form stable molecules, we turn to valence bond theory.

Valence bond theory suggests that a covalent bond forms when two atomic orbitals overlap. Think of this overlap as a shared region of space where electrons can reside, effectively acting as the glue that holds the nuclei together. There are two primary ways these orbitals can meet. A sigma bond is the most common type. It forms when two orbitals overlap head to head, directly along the axis connecting the two nuclei. Because the electron density is concentrated right between the nuclei, sigma bonds are very strong and allow the atoms to rotate freely around the bond axis, much like a swivel chair.

Sometimes, however, atoms form double or triple bonds. This is where pi bonds come in. A pi bond forms when side by side, unhybridized p orbitals overlap above and below the internuclear axis. Unlike the sigma bond, the electron density of a pi bond is spread out in lobes, which prevents the atoms from rotating. If you try to twist a double bond, you would have to break that side-by-side overlap, which takes a significant amount of energy.

Now, we still have the problem of those awkward shapes. Consider the carbon atom. In its ground state, it has two electrons in a spherical s orbital and two electrons in separate p orbitals. To bond effectively, carbon must first promote one of its s electrons to an empty p orbital. This excitation creates four unpaired electrons, allowing for four bonds. However, these orbitals are not identical. To create four equivalent bonds, nature performs a clever trick called hybridization. Hybridization is the process of mixing different types of atomic orbitals to create a new set of equivalent hybrid orbitals.

Take methane, which consists of one carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms. Carbon mixes its one s orbital and its three p orbitals to create four identical s p three hybrid orbitals. These orbitals point toward the corners of a tetrahedron, creating a bond angle of approximately one hundred nine point five degrees. Because these four hybrid orbitals are evenly spaced, the hydrogen atoms can attach at these perfect angles, minimizing repulsion between them.

The story changes when we look at molecules like ethylene, which contains a carbon-carbon double bond. In ethylene, each carbon atom bonds with three other atoms. To do this, it mixes its s orbital with only two of its p orbitals, creating three s p two hybrid orbitals. These orbitals lie in a flat, triangular plane with bond angles of approximately one hundred twenty degrees. The remaining p orbital on each carbon stays unhybridized, standing straight up like a flag pole. When the two carbon atoms sit next to each other, their s p two orbitals form a strong sigma bond, while those two leftover p orbitals reach out to touch side by side, forming a pi bond. This explains why ethylene is flat and rigid.

Finally, consider acetylene, which features a triple bond between two carbon atoms. Here, each carbon hybridizes its s orbital with only one p orbital to create two s p hybrid orbitals. These two orbitals point in opposite directions in a straight line, resulting in a bond angle of one hundred eighty degrees. The two remaining p orbitals on each carbon form two separate pi bonds. By mixing and matching their orbitals, atoms create the structural variety we see in the natural world. While these hybrid orbitals give us a fantastic picture of molecular geometry, they are still a model with known limits. Next, we will explore molecular orbital theory, which looks at the molecule as a whole rather than just focusing on individual bonds.

## Key ideas

- Valence bond theory explains bonding as the overlap of atomic orbitals, where sigma bonds overlap head to head and pi bonds overlap side by side.
- Hybridization involves the promotion of electrons to create unpaired electrons, followed by the mathematical mixing of orbitals to form equivalent hybrid orbitals.
- The s p three hybridization results in a tetrahedral geometry with bond angles of approximately one hundred nine point five degrees.
- The s p two hybridization results in a trigonal planar geometry with bond angles of approximately one hundred twenty degrees.
- The s p hybridization results in a linear geometry with bond angles of one hundred eighty degrees.
