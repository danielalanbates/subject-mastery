# Linear and Absolute Value Inequalities

Imagine you have a monthly budget of two hundred dollars for fuel, and every trip to the station costs thirty dollars. You want to know how many trips you can make without overspending. Instead of finding a single exact number of trips, you are looking for a range of possibilities. In our last lesson, we solved linear equations to find exact points where values matched. Today, we step beyond exact equality to study linear and absolute value inequalities, which describe entire ranges of numbers that satisfy a condition.

An inequality compares two expressions using symbols that mean is less than, is greater than, is less than or equal to, or is greater than or equal to. To solve a linear inequality, you isolate the unknown variable much like you would in a standard linear equation. Let us take an example. Suppose three times a variable we will call letter p plus four is less than or equal to nineteen. To solve for letter p, you first subtract four from both sides. The left side leaves three times letter p, and the right side becomes fifteen. Next, you divide both sides by three. Because three is positive, the inequality symbol stays pointing in the same direction, leaving letter p is less than or equal to five.

We can check our work by picking a value from that range, such as four, and substituting it into the original inequality. Three times four plus four is sixteen, and sixteen is indeed less than or equal to nineteen.

There is a famous twist in this process that every student must master. What happens if you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality by a negative number? Let us test a simple true statement, such as two is less than five. If we multiply both sides by negative one, what happens to the relation? If we kept the symbol the same, we would claim negative two is less than negative five, which is false because negative two is actually higher on the number line than negative five. To keep the statement true, multiplying or dividing by a negative number requires us to reverse the inequality symbol. So, negative two is greater than negative five. Whenever you divide by a negative coefficient, reverse the inequality symbol.

Sometimes conditions come in pairs, creating what we call compound inequalities. A compound inequality combines two separate statements with the word and or the word or. For instance, consider the requirement that letter p is greater than negative two and letter p is less than or equal to four. This describes every number sitting strictly between negative two and four, including four but excluding negative two. We can write this solution set in set-builder notation as the set of all numbers letter p such that letter p is greater than negative two and letter p is less than or equal to four. We also describe this solution set using interval notation, which lists the lower and upper bounds. For our example, we write an open parenthesis, negative two, comma, four, and a closed bracket. The parenthesis means negative two is not included, while the square bracket means four is included. Interval notation can also express unbounded intervals using the infinity symbol or negative infinity symbol, paired with parentheses to show that the range continues without end.

Absolute value inequalities require a different lens because absolute value measures distance from zero on the number line. When we say the absolute value of letter x is less than three, we are not just doing arithmetic; we are asking which numbers live at a distance of less than three units away from zero. Both positive two and negative two are within three units of zero. Therefore, an absolute value being less than a number translates directly into a compound inequality trapped between a negative and a positive bound. Here, letter x must be greater than negative three and less than three.

Conversely, if the absolute value of letter x is greater than three, we are looking for numbers whose distance from zero is greater than three units. This means letter x must either be greater than three or less than negative three, pointing outward in opposite directions. Solving these problems relies entirely on translating the absolute value distance statement into a pair of linear inequalities connected by and or or.

As we advance further into our study of mathematics, we will soon take these tools off the flat number line and place them onto the coordinate plane, where inequalities define entire regions of space.

## Key ideas
- Linear inequalities are solved like linear equations, isolating the variable to find a range of valid numbers rather than a single exact value.
- Multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative number reverses the direction of the inequality symbol to maintain a true statement.
- Checking solutions involves substituting a value from the solution set back into the original inequality to confirm it holds true.
- Absolute value inequalities are interpreted as statements about distance from zero on the number line, translating into compound inequalities.
- Solution sets are communicated through set-builder notation and interval notation, using parentheses for exclusive boundaries, brackets for inclusive ones, and infinity symbols for unbounded ranges.
