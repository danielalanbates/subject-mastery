# The Churches Beyond Chalcedon

Imagine you are standing in a bustling marketplace in fifth-century Alexandria, Egypt. You hear merchants arguing not just over the price of grain, but over the nature of Christ. In our last lesson, we explored the Council of Chalcedon in 451 CE, where the church declared that Christ is one person existing in two distinct natures: fully divine and fully human. While this settled the question for the Roman imperial church, it did not bring peace. Instead, it triggered a deep, lasting fracture that shaped the geography of faith across the ancient world.

The churches that felt unable to accept the language of Chalcedon are known today as the Oriental Orthodox churches. These include the Coptic Orthodox Church of Egypt, the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Armenian Apostolic Church, and the Syriac Orthodox Church. A key identifier for these traditions is their acceptance of the first three ecumenical councils—Nicaea, Constantinople, and Ephesus—while they formally reject the Council of Chalcedon. For centuries, Western writers labeled them as Monophysites, a term implying they believed Christ had only one, singular nature. However, these churches have always rejected that label. They view it as a misrepresentation of their faith. Instead, they prefer the term Miaphysite, which emphasizes that Christ’s divinity and humanity are united in one incarnate nature without confusion or change.

For these communities, the language of Chalcedon felt like a retreat toward the views of Nestorius, who had been condemned earlier at the Council of Ephesus for allegedly separating Christ into two loose parts. By insisting on two natures, the Oriental Orthodox worried that the council was undermining the unity of the Savior. They were not trying to deny Christ's humanity, but were fiercely protecting his singular, indivisible identity. They saw themselves as the true guardians of the tradition established by Cyril of Alexandria, who had championed the idea of Christ as one unified being.

Beyond the Roman Empire, in the lands of the Persian Empire—modern-day Iraq and Iran—the Church of the East took a different path. This church had long operated outside the reach of Roman imperial councils. They accept only the first two ecumenical councils: Nicaea and Constantinople. They were often branded as Nestorians by their rivals, accused of dividing Christ into two separate persons. Yet, the Church of the East consistently taught that Christ is one person. They simply used a different vocabulary, focusing on the distinct ways in which his divine and human actions were expressed. They were a missionary-minded community, eventually carrying the gospel all the way to China and India.

For over a millennium, these divisions seemed permanent. The Oriental Orthodox and the Church of the East developed their own vibrant liturgies, monastic traditions, and theological schools, separate from the Byzantine world of Constantinople and the Latin world of Rome. They survived the rise of new powers and changing political borders, keeping their unique identities alive through centuries of challenge. They were not merely dissenters from a central authority; they were ancient, thriving traditions with deep roots in the apostolic soil of the Middle East, Africa, and Asia.

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, a remarkable shift has occurred. Modern theologians and church leaders have engaged in extensive dialogue, looking past the polemical labels of the past. Many scholars now agree that the ancient splits were often driven by misunderstandings of language and culture rather than genuine disagreement on the core mystery of Christ. Through bilateral agreements, such as those between the Catholic and Oriental Orthodox churches and the common Christological declaration of 1994 between the Catholic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East, and separate dialogues between the Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox, leaders have acknowledged that both sides were essentially trying to defend the same truth: that Jesus is truly God and truly man.

While these groups remain distinct in their administration and worship, the harsh condemnations of the past have largely faded. We now recognize that the churches beyond Chalcedon are not rivals to be dismissed, but vital branches of the global Christian family. Understanding their history helps us see that the church has always been broader and more diverse than any single imperial council could define. As these ancient communities navigated their own paths, the Roman world they left behind faced a period of profound transformation and collapse, which we will examine next as we look at the shifting landscape of the West.

## Key ideas
- The Oriental Orthodox churches accept the first three ecumenical councils but reject Chalcedon, while the Church of the East accepts only the first two.
- The Oriental Orthodox describe their faith as Miaphysite, emphasizing the unified nature of Christ, and they reject the historical label of Monophysite as a misunderstanding of their theology.
- The Church of the East, long labeled Nestorian by outsiders, maintained a distinct theological tradition that focused on the unity of Christ while operating outside the jurisdiction of Roman imperial councils.
- Modern bilateral dialogues have significantly narrowed the old divides, leading many churches to recognize that these ancient splits were largely based on misunderstandings of terminology.
