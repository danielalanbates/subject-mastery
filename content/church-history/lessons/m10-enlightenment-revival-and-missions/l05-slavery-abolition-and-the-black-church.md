# Slavery, Abolition and the Black Church

Imagine standing in a colonial-era church in North America. The pews are divided by social standing, but the most profound divide is invisible. In the same building, one man hears a sermon on the equality of souls before God, while another man, sitting in the gallery, is considered legal property. How could the same faith be used to justify human bondage and, simultaneously, to fuel the movement to end it? This paradox is one of the most painful and complex chapters in the history of the church.

In our last lesson, we saw how the modern missionary movement spread the gospel across the globe. Yet, as missionaries carried the message of spiritual freedom to distant lands, many churches in the West remained deeply entangled in the brutal reality of the trans-Atlantic slave trade. For centuries, many Christian leaders defended slavery, often citing Old Testament passages to argue that the practice was divinely sanctioned. They claimed that the social order was fixed by God and that the conversion of enslaved people would make them better, more obedient workers.

However, the evangelical revivals we discussed earlier began to change the moral calculus. As the movement for personal conversion and social reform grew, it sparked a radical realization among many believers: if all humans are created in the image of God, then slavery is a direct affront to the Creator. In the late eighteenth century, religious groups like the Quakers led the charge against the slave trade. They were joined by figures like William Wilberforce in Britain. Wilberforce was an evangelical member of Parliament who believed his political career was a divine calling. For decades, he fought in the halls of government, driven by the conviction that the gospel demanded the liberation of the oppressed. His efforts contributed to the Slavery Abolition Act of 1833, which abolished slavery throughout most of the British Empire effective August 1, 1834, though it introduced an apprenticeship system that lasted until 1838 for many colonies.

The situation in the United States took a different, more tragic path. As the nation expanded, the issue of slavery became a wedge that divided entire denominations. By the 1840s, the tension reached a breaking point. Major groups, including the Baptists and the Methodists, split into northern and southern branches. The northern factions increasingly viewed slavery as a moral sin, while the southern branches argued that the institution was essential to their way of life and protected by scripture. This denominational schism was a grim preview of the American Civil War that would follow two decades later.

While white denominations were fracturing, African American Christians were forging their own path. They were often marginalized or excluded from white church leadership, even when they were allowed to attend. In response, they began to establish their own independent institutions. One of the most significant moments in this history occurred in Philadelphia in the 1790s. Richard Allen, a formerly enslaved man and a Methodist preacher, walked out of Saint George's Methodist Church with Absalom Jones and other Black worshipers after a church trustee pulled Jones up from his knees during prayer and told them they could not kneel in that part of the gallery. Allen founded Bethel Church in 1794, and in 1816 it became the mother church of the new African Methodist Episcopal Church, with Allen as its first bishop.

The founding of the African Methodist Episcopal Church was more than an act of administrative independence; it was a profound assertion of human dignity. These independent Black churches became the bedrock of African American life. They provided education, offered a sanctuary from a hostile society, and served as the primary training ground for leaders who would later fight for civil rights. In these spaces, the gospel was preached as a message of liberation. It was a faith that spoke directly to the experience of those who suffered, emphasizing the God of the Exodus who hears the cry of the enslaved and acts to deliver them.

This history reveals a church that was deeply flawed and often hypocritical, yet also capable of producing the very moral arguments needed to dismantle the systems it had helped build. The story of the Black church is a testament to the resilience of the human spirit when anchored in the hope of the gospel. As we move forward, we must also look at how the Roman Catholic Church was navigating its own period of profound change and internal questioning in the nineteenth century, as it faced the challenges of a modernizing world and the rise of new political ideologies.

## Key ideas

- The church was historically divided on the issue of slavery, with some leaders using scripture to defend the practice while others, driven by evangelical convictions, led the movement for abolition.
- William Wilberforce and the Quakers were instrumental in the British movement to end the slave trade, viewing abolition as a moral and religious necessity.
- In the United States, the moral conflict over slavery led to significant schisms within major denominations, such as the Methodists and Baptists, during the 1840s.
- The founding of independent Black churches, such as Richard Allen’s African Methodist Episcopal Church, provided a necessary sanctuary and a platform for African American leadership, education, and social advocacy.
