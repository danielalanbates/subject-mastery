# General and Special Revelation

Imagine you are standing on a remote coastline at midnight. Above you, the Milky Way stretches across the sky in a brilliant, dusty arc. The rhythmic crash of the waves against the cliffs feels both ancient and intentional. In that moment, most people feel a profound sense of awe. You might find yourself thinking that this vast complexity cannot be an accident. You feel as though you are catching a glimpse of a Grand Architect or a Divine Artist. This universal human experience is the starting point for one of the most important concepts in theology: revelation.

In our previous lesson, we defined theology as the disciplined study of God. But a fundamental problem arises immediately. How can a finite, limited human mind ever hope to know an infinite, unlimited Creator? If God is truly transcendent, we cannot simply discover Him through our own cleverness, the way we might discover a new species of beetle or a mathematical formula. For us to know God, God must first choose to be known. This act of divine self-disclosure is what theologians call revelation. The word comes from the Latin "revelatio," which literally means an "unveiling." To study theology is to look at what God has unveiled.

To frame this unveiling, theologians like Saint Augustine and Thomas Aquinas often used the metaphor of the two books of God. They argued that God has authored two volumes for us to read: the Book of Nature and the Book of Scripture. This distinction leads us to the two primary categories of divine communication: general revelation and special revelation.

General revelation refers to the knowledge of God that is available to all people, in all places, at all times. It is often called natural revelation because its primary theater is the natural world. The Bible itself points to this. In the nineteenth Psalm, verse one, of the King James Version, it says, "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork." Through the beauty, order, and sheer scale of the universe, we can discern that a powerful and intelligent Being exists.

This revelation is not just found in the stars, but also within the human person. Theologians point to the human conscience as a form of general revelation. In the second chapter of the letter to the Romans, verses fourteen and fifteen, the King James Version describes how even those without the written law "shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness." This inner sense of right and wrong suggests a moral lawgiver. In the Reformed tradition, John Calvin described this as the "sensus divinitatis," or a natural sense of the divine that is implanted in every human heart. However, Calvin and later Protestant theologians also emphasized the noetic effects of sin. The word noetic refers to the mind. They argued that while the evidence of God is all around us, sin has so blurred our intellectual and spiritual vision that we often ignore or distort what nature reveals.

Because general revelation is like seeing a silhouette through a frosted window, special revelation becomes necessary. Special revelation is God’s specific communication to particular people at particular times. It moves us from a vague divine force to a personal God with a name and a history. In the Protestant tradition, special revelation is often identified strictly with the sixty-six books of the Bible. However, in the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions, special revelation is understood more broadly. They believe that Sacred Tradition, which is the living transmission of the Word of God through the church’s teaching and liturgy, is a necessary conduit of revelation alongside the Holy Scriptures.

The pinnacle of special revelation is not a book or a tradition, but a person. The author of the book of Hebrews explains this transition. In the first two verses of the first chapter, in the World English Bible, it says: "God, having in the past spoken to the fathers through the prophets at many times and in various ways, has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son." For Christians, Jesus Christ is the definitive unveiling of God's character. If you want to know what the Creator is like, you look at the life and teachings of Jesus.

The relationship between these two types of revelation has been a major topic of debate for centuries. Early church fathers like Augustine and medieval scholars like Aquinas saw a beautiful harmony between them, believing that human reason could build a foundation of natural theology that prepared the way for faith. Modern scholars, such as those in the field of analytic philosophy, continue to develop these arguments today.

In contrast, the twentieth-century theologian Karl Barth famously argued that because of human fallenness, we cannot rely on general revelation at all. He insisted that we can only truly know God through the specific revelation of Jesus Christ. Despite these differing emphases, most traditions agree that general revelation makes us aware that there is a God to whom we are accountable, while special revelation tells us how that God has acted in history to redeem us.

## Key ideas
- Revelation is the act of God intentionally unveiling His nature and will to humanity, often described through the metaphor of the two books of Nature and Scripture.
- General revelation is the universal disclosure of God through the natural world, human reason, and the moral conscience written on the heart.
- Special revelation is God’s specific communication through historical acts, the prophets, the person of Jesus Christ, and, in Catholic and Orthodox views, Sacred Tradition.
- The Reformed concept of the "sensus divinitatis" suggests an innate sense of God exists in all people, though its clarity is diminished by the noetic effects of sin.
- While general revelation establishes the existence of a Creator, special revelation is required to understand God’s personal character and the message of salvation.
