# Empirical and Molecular Formulas

Imagine you find a mysterious white powder in a chemistry lab. You know what elements it contains, but you do not know how many atoms of each element make up its individual molecules. To solve this puzzle, chemists use a process that moves from the percentages of mass to the actual number of atoms. In our last lesson, we learned how to use molar mass to count atoms by weighing them. Now, we take that knowledge a step further to determine the identity of a chemical compound.

We begin with percent composition by mass. This is the percentage of the total mass of a compound that comes from each specific element. If you have a sample, the sum of the percentages of all its elements will always be one hundred percent. When calculating these values, we use the atomic masses from the periodic table, usually rounded to two decimal places to maintain appropriate significant figures.

An empirical formula is the simplest whole number ratio of atoms in a compound. To find this ratio, imagine you have exactly one hundred grams of our mysterious powder. If the analysis shows forty percent carbon, six point seven percent hydrogen, and fifty-three point three percent oxygen, you have forty grams of carbon, six point seven grams of hydrogen, and fifty-three point three grams of oxygen.

We convert these grams into moles by dividing each mass by the element's molar mass. For carbon, we divide forty grams by twelve point zero one grams per mole, giving three point three three moles. For hydrogen, we divide six point seven grams by one point zero one grams per mole, giving six point six three moles. For oxygen, we divide fifty-three point three grams by sixteen point zero zero grams per mole, giving three point three three moles.

To find the ratio, divide each mole value by the smallest one, which is three point three three. This results in one carbon, two hydrogen, and one oxygen. The empirical formula is C H two O. Sometimes, these divisions do not yield whole numbers. If you calculate a ratio like one to two point five, you must multiply the entire set by a small integer, such as two, to reach the smallest whole number ratio of two to five.

Consider another example. Suppose a compound is eighty-five point six percent carbon and fourteen point four percent hydrogen. We assume one hundred grams, giving eighty-five point six grams of carbon and fourteen point four grams of hydrogen. Dividing by their respective molar masses, we get seven point one three moles of carbon and fourteen point two six moles of hydrogen. Dividing both by seven point one three gives a ratio of one to two. The empirical formula is C H two.

The empirical formula is often just a starting point. A molecular formula tells us the actual number of atoms in a single molecule. To find it, we need the molar mass of the actual compound. Suppose our mystery compound with the empirical formula C H two O has a measured molar mass of one hundred eighty grams per mole. We calculate the empirical formula mass by adding the atomic masses: twelve plus two plus sixteen equals thirty grams per mole.

We then divide the actual molar mass by the empirical mass: one hundred eighty divided by thirty equals six. This multiplier, which we call n, must be a whole number. If your result is not a whole number, it often suggests experimental error in the measurement of the molar mass. Since our result is six, we multiply every subscript in our empirical formula by six, giving us a molecular formula of C six H twelve O six.

Determining these formulas is essential for identifying unknown substances. Once we have the correct molecular formula, we can represent that substance accurately in a chemical reaction. Understanding how to derive these formulas prepares us for the next step in our study, which is using those formulas to write and balance the equations that describe how matter changes.

## Key ideas

- Percent composition by mass describes the percentage of a compound's total mass contributed by each individual element.
- The empirical formula provides the simplest whole number ratio of atoms for the elements present in a compound.
- When mole ratios are not whole numbers, you must multiply the entire set by a small integer to obtain the simplest whole number ratio.
- To find a molecular formula, divide the known molar mass of the compound by the mass of its empirical formula unit to find the integer multiplier.
- Determining empirical and molecular formulas allows chemists to identify unknown substances based on their elemental composition and mass.
