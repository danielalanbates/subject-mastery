# Solving Quadratics by Factoring and Square Roots

Imagine launching a small projectile off a ledge, like a stone tossed from a cliff into the ocean below. Its height above the water changes every second, first rising, then pausing at its peak, and finally plunging down until it hits the waves. If you want to know the exact moments when that stone passes a certain height, or when it finally strikes the water, linear equations will not help you. Linear equations deal with straight lines and steady rates of change. To capture the graceful curve of a falling object, or to calculate the maximum area of a fenced garden, we need equations that include a squared variable. These are quadratic equations.

In our last lesson, we translated word problems into algebraic equations, setting up relationships between unknown quantities. Often, those translations led us past linear models and right into the realm of quadratics. A quadratic equation is any equation that can be written in standard form. In standard form, the expression has a squared term, a linear term, and a constant term, all combined on one side of the equation, with zero on the other side. Naming our coefficients by their roles, standard form looks like the expression where the leading coefficient times x squared plus the linear coefficient times x plus the constant term equals zero, with the understanding that our leading coefficient cannot be zero, because if it were, our squared term would vanish and leave us back with a linear equation.

How do we solve such an equation? One of the most elegant methods relies on a fundamental property of ordinary arithmetic: the zero product property. This property tells us that if the product of two numbers is zero, then at least one of those numbers must be zero. If you multiply two things together and get zero, you already know something decisive about one of the factors. We can harness this fact by factoring our quadratic expression into the product of two linear binomials, setting the entire product equal to zero, and then setting each binomial factor equal to zero separately.

Let us walk through an example. Suppose we want to solve the equation where x squared minus five x plus six equals zero. First, we check that it is in standard form with zero on the right. Next, we factor the quadratic expression on the left into two binomials. We look for two numbers that multiply to positive six and add up to negative five. Those numbers are negative two and negative three. So our left side factors into the quantity x minus two times the quantity x minus three, and this entire product equals zero. By the zero product property, either x minus two equals zero or x minus three equals zero. Solving these two simple linear equations gives us our solutions: x equals two and x equals three. We can check our work by substituting either value back into the original equation. If we plug in two, we get four minus ten plus six, which indeed simplifies to zero.

Not every quadratic equation is easily factored with whole numbers, which brings us to our second method: solving by taking square roots. This approach works best when the quadratic equation lacks a linear term, or when the squared expression is already isolated inside grouping symbols. Consider the equation where three times x squared minus twenty-seven equals zero. Instead of factoring, we can isolate the squared term. We add twenty-seven to both sides, giving us three times x squared equals twenty-seven. Then we divide both sides by three, leaving x squared equals nine.

Now we take the square root of both sides. Here is a crucial point where students often make mistakes. When you solve an equation by taking the square root, you must account for both the positive and the negative root. If x squared equals nine, then x could be three, because three times three is nine. But x could also be negative three, because negative three times negative three is also nine. So our complete solution set consists of both positive three and negative three. We can write this compactly by saying x equals plus or minus three. This same principle applies when we have a binomial squared, such as the square of the quantity x minus four equals sixteen. Taking the square root of both sides gives the quantity x minus four equals plus or minus four, which splits into two simple linear equations and yields solutions of eight and zero.

As we move forward, we will encounter quadratic equations that cannot be easily factored and do not lend themselves to simple square root isolation. In our next lesson, we will explore powerful universal tools for handling any quadratic equation whatsoever: completing the square and the quadratic formula.

## Key ideas
- A quadratic equation in standard form is written as an expression with a squared term, a linear term, and a constant term equal to zero.
- The zero product property states that if the product of two factors is zero, then at least one of those factors must be zero.
- Solving by factoring requires writing the quadratic expression as a product of linear binomials and setting each factor to zero.
- Solving by taking square roots requires isolating the squared term and remembering to include both the positive and the negative root.
