# Public Choice Theory

Imagine you run a bakery. Every morning, you decide how many loaves of sourdough to bake by weighing the cost of flour against the price customers pay. You look out for your own interests, but market mechanics end up feeding the neighborhood. Now, imagine a policymaker deciding whether to subsidize wheat production. Traditional economic theory once assumed that while ordinary people act in their own self interest in the marketplace, those same people turn into selfless public servants in government offices. Public choice theory rejects this split personality entirely.

Pioneered by economists like James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock in the middle of the twentieth century, public choice theory applies economic analysis to politics. Instead of viewing government as a benevolent social guardian aiming for the common good, public choice treats politicians, bureaucrats, voters, and interest groups as rational human beings who respond to incentives. Just like the baker, political actors are driven by personal goals like winning elections or securing job security. Understanding government requires looking at the actual rules of the game rather than hoping for angels to run our institutions.

To understand this shift, we must examine the debate between public choice theory and public interest theory. Public interest theory assumes that government policy is designed to protect the broad well being of society. In this traditional view, regulation emerges to correct market failures, protect consumers, and promote social justice. Public choice theory challenges this view directly. It argues that political institutions do not automatically correct market failures. Instead, the public choice perspective suggests that political markets suffer from their own distinct failures because participants pursue private interests using public authority.

In our last lesson, we explored how governments use taxes and transfers to redistribute income. But why do certain industries receive generous tax breaks while broader social programs face endless gridlock? Public choice theory helps explain this through concentrated benefits and dispersed costs. Consider a policy that places a protective tariff on imported sugar. The policy benefits a few domestic sugar producers by millions of dollars each, giving those producers a massive incentive to lobby fiercely. Meanwhile, the cost is spread across millions of consumers in fractions of a cent per grocery bill. Because the individual cost is tiny, no single consumer protests. Politicians approve the policy to secure campaign donations, even though total societal harm outweighs total gain.

This dynamic opens the door to rent seeking, which occurs when firms spend resources to secure special political privileges rather than creating new economic value. When powerful corporations engage in rent seeking, they often succeed in capturing the very regulatory agencies meant to oversee them. This is known as regulatory capture. Over time, an agency originally created to protect the public interest becomes an instrument used by the regulated industry to block new competitors and protect incumbent profits, turning the public interest model upside down.

Rent seeking often requires legislative cooperation, which is where logrolling comes into play. Logrolling is the practice of vote trading among legislators to secure the passage of bills that might otherwise fail. Imagine one lawmaker represents a district wanting a federal cotton subsidy, while another wants a soybean subsidy. Neither has enough individual votes to pass their favored subsidy alone. By agreeing to trade votes, both lawmakers ensure that their preferred local projects pass. While logrolling helps build legislative coalitions, it often results in a collection of narrow spending measures imposing net costs on taxpayers.

This dynamic spills over from politicians to unelected bureaucrats as well. In a standard private firm, managers cut waste to maximize profit for owners. In a government agency, however, there are rarely owners looking for a profit return. Instead, budget maximizing bureaucrats discover that power and career advancement are tied to the size of their agency budget. If an office successfully solves a social problem, its justification for existing and its funding might shrink. Consequently, agencies may face a quiet structural incentive to grow rather than to finish their work. Critics of public choice reply that it underrates genuine public spirit, and that many agencies and reforms do achieve their goals; the theory is best used as a warning about incentives, not a proof that government always fails.

Even voters face incentives leading to what economists call rational ignorance. Because a single vote rarely decides an election, the time and effort required to become fully informed on every complex policy issue yields a low personal return. It is rational for a busy citizen to remain ignorant about intricate trade agreements or monetary rules. Interest groups easily fill this information void, pushing policies that favor narrow niches at the expense of the general public. This relationship highlights a classic principal agent problem. Voters act as principals who hire representatives to manage public affairs. Because monitoring government is costly, political agents often pursue their own goals rather than voter welfare.

Recognizing these realities does not mean government has no useful role. Markets fail in specific ways, and collective action is often necessary. Yet public choice theory reminds us that government is not an automatic corrective mechanism. It is an arena of human interaction governed by specific rules and incentives. As we move into our next lesson, we will examine how these underlying incentives manifest in voting systems and why they can lead directly to systemic government failure.

## Key ideas
- Public choice theory applies economic tools and the assumption of rational self interest to politicians, bureaucrats, and voters, contrasting with traditional public interest theory.
- Elected officials and bureaucrats respond to political incentives, such as winning elections and expanding budgets, rather than acting solely as selfless guardians of the public good.
- Concentrated benefits and dispersed costs, combined with rent seeking and regulatory capture, explain why narrow interest groups secure favorable policies that harm the broader public.
- Logrolling allows legislators to build coalitions through vote trading, which can pass narrow projects that impose net costs on society.
- Rational ignorance and the principal agent problem describe why ordinary citizens find it inefficient to monitor political agents who may pursue private goals.
