# Complex Numbers

Imagine you are trying to solve a simple quadratic equation using the quadratic formula you learned previously. You have the equation where x squared plus four equals zero. If you subtract four from both sides, you get x squared equals negative four. Now, pause for a moment and think about ordinary numbers. If you take any real number, whether it is positive, negative, or zero, and multiply it by itself, the result is never negative. A positive times a positive is positive, and a negative times a negative is also positive. For generations, mathematicians declared that such equations had no solutions. But refusing to stop at that barrier led to one of the most powerful expansions of the number system in history.

To make sense of equations like x squared equals negative four, mathematicians invented a new foundational quantity called the imaginary unit, denoted by the letter i. By definition, i is a number whose square is negative one. This single definition bridges an impossible gap. Instead of throwing our hands up when we encounter the square root of a negative number, we can express it using our new unit. For example, the square root of negative four can be written as the square root of four times negative one, which separates into the square root of four times the square root of negative one, giving us two times i.

When we combine ordinary real numbers with multiples of this imaginary unit, we enter the realm of complex numbers. A complex number is any expression that consists of a real part plus an imaginary part. We write them in the form p plus q times i, where p and q are ordinary real numbers. For instance, three plus five times i is a complex number, where three is the real part and five is the imaginary part. Even a pure real number like seven is technically a complex number with an imaginary part of zero, and a pure imaginary quantity like negative two times i is a complex number with a real part of zero.

Performing arithmetic with complex numbers follows the familiar rules of algebra, treating the letter i much like an algebraic variable, with one crucial upgrade whenever i squared appears. When you add two complex numbers, you simply combine their real parts together and their imaginary parts together. Suppose you want to add two plus three times i to four plus five times i. You add the real parts, two plus four, to get six, and you add the imaginary parts, three times i plus five times i, to get eight times i, yielding a sum of six plus eight times i.

Multiplication works similarly through distribution, but it always brings in our defining rule for the imaginary unit. Let us multiply the quantity one plus two times i by the quantity three plus four times i. Using distribution, we multiply one by three, which is three, and one by four times i, which is four times i. Then we multiply two times i by three, giving six times i, and two times i by four times i, which gives eight times i squared. Because i squared equals negative one, that last term becomes eight times negative one, which is negative eight. Combining our real terms, three plus negative eight, gives negative five, and combining our imaginary terms gives ten times i, resulting in negative five plus ten times i.

Division requires a clever trick involving what we call the complex conjugate. The conjugate of any complex number p plus q times i is simply p minus q times i, where the sign of the imaginary part is flipped. When you multiply a complex number by its own conjugate, the imaginary terms completely cancel out, leaving a pure real number. To divide one complex number by another, say, one plus i divided by one minus i, you multiply both the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate of the denominator, which here is one plus i. Expanding the numerator gives one plus two times i plus i squared, and expanding the denominator gives one minus i squared. Simplifying the numerator with i squared becoming negative one leaves just two times i, while the denominator becomes one minus negative one, which is two. Dividing two times i by two leaves us simply with i.

This entire framework becomes essential when we solve quadratic equations whose discriminant is negative. When the discriminant inside the quadratic formula evaluates to a negative number, the equation yields two complex conjugate solutions rather than real ones. For example, consider the equation where x squared plus four equals zero. Applying the quadratic formula with a leading coefficient of one, a linear coefficient of zero, and a constant term of four, the discriminant evaluates to zero squared minus four times one times four, which is negative sixteen. The formula then gives negative zero plus or minus the square root of negative sixteen, all divided by two times one. The square root of negative sixteen is four times i, and dividing by two gives two times i and negative two times i. These two solutions are complex conjugates of one another. We can picture these numbers on a two-dimensional grid where the horizontal axis tracks the ordinary real part and the vertical axis tracks the imaginary part, creating a coordinate space where every complex number corresponds to a unique point. In our upcoming lesson, we will build upon these foundations to explore how polynomial functions behave when their zeros extend far beyond the real number line into this two-dimensional coordinate space.

## Key ideas
- The imaginary unit i is defined as a number whose square equals negative one, allowing us to take the square root of negative numbers.
- A complex number is written as a real part plus an imaginary part, in the form of p plus q times i.
- Adding complex numbers is done by combining their real parts and their imaginary parts separately.
- Multiplying complex numbers relies on ordinary algebraic expansion combined with the substitution of negative one for i squared.
- Dividing complex numbers uses the complex conjugate of the denominator to clear imaginary numbers from the bottom of the fraction.
- Quadratic equations with negative discriminants yield pairs of complex conjugate solutions that can be tracked on a grid with a real horizontal axis and an imaginary vertical axis.
