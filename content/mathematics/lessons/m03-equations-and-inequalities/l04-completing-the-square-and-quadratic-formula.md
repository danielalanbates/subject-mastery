# Completing the Square and the Quadratic Formula

Imagine you are standing on a bridge, tossing a heavy stone upward into the air. The stone climbs for a moment, slows down, pauses at its peak, and then plunges down into the river far below. If you want to know the exact moment the stone hits the water, you must set its height equation equal to zero and solve for time. In our last lesson, we mastered solving quadratic equations when they could be easily factored, or when taking the square root of both sides did the trick. But what happens when a quadratic equation refuses to factor neatly, leaving you stranded with messy decimals or stubborn roots? Today, we unlock the master keys to solving any quadratic equation whatsoever: completing the square and the quadratic formula.

To understand why these methods work, we begin with a geometric puzzle. A quadratic expression typically looks like a squared term, a linear term, and a constant, such as x squared plus six x. Think of x squared as a large square carpet with an area of x times x, and six x as two rectangular strips of carpet, each three times x. If you try to arrange these pieces into a single, larger square, you find a small corner piece is missing. That missing piece has an area of three squared, which is nine. To complete the square, you must add that nine. Whatever you add to one side of an equation, you must add to the other to keep the balance.

Let us put this geometric insight into practice with the equation x squared plus six x minus seven equals zero. First, we move the constant term to the right side, giving x squared plus six x equals seven. Now we complete the square on the left. We take the linear coefficient, which is six, divide it by two to get three, and square it to get nine. We add nine to both sides of the equation. The left side is now x squared plus six x plus nine, which neatly rewrites as the square of the quantity x plus three. The right side is seven plus nine, which is sixteen. Taking the square root of both sides gives the quantity x plus three equals positive or negative four. Subtracting three from both sides yields our two solutions: x equals one and x equals negative seven.

Mathematicians love efficiency, and completing the square for every single problem can become tedious. So, centuries ago, they applied this exact completing the square technique to the general quadratic equation written with placeholder letters: the leading coefficient times x squared, plus a second coefficient times x, plus a constant equals zero. By carrying out the algebraic steps of completing the square on these letters, the process yields a universal recipe known as the quadratic formula. For any quadratic equation, x equals the opposite of the second coefficient, plus or minus the square root of the quantity the second coefficient squared minus four times the leading coefficient times the constant, all divided by two times the leading coefficient.

This formula contains a built-in crystal ball called the discriminant, which is the expression hiding underneath the square root sign. The discriminant tells you the exact number and type of solutions a quadratic equation possesses before you even finish calculating. If the discriminant is a positive number, the square root yields two distinct real numbers, meaning the equation crosses the horizontal axis twice. If the discriminant is exactly zero, the square root vanishes, leaving you with one repeated real solution where the curve merely kisses the axis. If the discriminant is a negative number, the square root demands the impossible within the realm of real numbers, pointing us toward complex solutions that we will explore soon.

Consider the equation two x squared minus four x minus three equals zero. We identify our leading coefficient as two, our second coefficient as negative four, and our constant as negative three. We calculate the discriminant by squaring negative four to get sixteen, and subtracting four times two times negative three, which is negative twenty-four. Sixteen minus negative twenty-four gives a positive forty. Because forty is positive, we know immediately there are two real solutions. We plug our numbers into the quadratic formula, yielding four plus or minus the square root of forty, all divided by four. Simplifying the radical gives four plus or minus two times the square root of ten, all divided by four, which reduces to two plus or minus the square root of ten, all divided by two.

Mastering the quadratic formula and completing the square gives you absolute confidence when facing any second-degree polynomial equation. In our next lesson, we will pivot from polynomials to tackle radical and absolute value equations, where new types of algebraic traps await.

## Key ideas
- Completing the square transforms any quadratic expression into a squared binomial by adding a carefully chosen constant derived from the linear coefficient.
- The quadratic formula is derived directly by applying the process of completing the square to the general algebraic form of a quadratic equation.
- The discriminant, located under the square root in the quadratic formula, determines whether an equation has two real solutions, one repeated solution, or no real solutions.
- Every quadratic equation can be solved accurately using the quadratic formula, regardless of whether the expression can be factored easily by hand.
