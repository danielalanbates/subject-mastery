# The Byzantine Empire and Orthodox Christianity

Imagine standing on the ancient walls of Constantinople in the year 600 CE, looking out across the bustling waters of the Bosporus strait. While the Western half of the Roman Empire had collapsed about one hundred twenty years earlier into a patchwork of shifting Germanic kingdoms, this eastern half stood proud, wealthy, and remarkably resilient. As we saw in our journey through the classical Americas, sophisticated urban centers were thriving across the globe, yet here on the edge of Europe and Asia, a direct continuation of ancient Rome was adapting to a radically new medieval world. We call this enduring state the Byzantine Empire, named after Byzantium, the ancient Greek colony upon which its magnificent capital was built.

To understand the Byzantine survival, we must look at how it preserved the immense intellectual wealth of antiquity. While Western Europe entered a period where classical Greek and Roman texts were frequently lost or forgotten, Byzantine scholars, scribes, and monastics carefully copied, annotated, and safeguarded philosophy, mathematics, literature, and legal codes. Without this painstaking preservation in monasteries and imperial libraries, works by Plato, Aristotle, and classical physicians like Galen might have vanished entirely. Under the sixth-century emperor Justinian, this classical heritage was synthesized with Roman legal tradition into a monumental achievement known as the Corpus Juris Civilis, or the Code of Justinian. This legal collection streamlined centuries of chaotic imperial edicts and laid foundational principles for jurisprudence that still influence modern legal systems worldwide.

The cultural glue that held this diverse, multi-ethnic empire together was not merely Roman administration, but a distinct form of Christianity. Over centuries, theological, linguistic, and political differences drove a deep wedge between the Christians of the Latin-speaking West and those of the Greek-speaking East. In the Byzantine world, the emperor often held supreme authority over both secular governance and ecclesiastical matters, a close partnership between church and state that older historians called caesaropapism, a label many now think too simple, since bishops and monks often defied emperors. Religious life was visual, deeply mystical, and anchored by ornate icons—sacred paintings of holy figures designed to inspire contemplation. These doctrinal and liturgical divergences eventually culminated in a lasting split, traditionally dated to the Great Schism of 1054 CE and hardened by the crusaders' sack of Constantinople in 1204, dividing Christianity into Western Catholicism and Eastern Orthodoxy.

Beyond preserving texts and defining a distinct religious identity, the Byzantine Empire functioned as a powerful missionary state, systematically spreading its faith and culture across Eastern Europe. Rather than relying solely on military conquest, Byzantine rulers used diplomacy, trade, and conversion as primary tools of statecraft. A prime example of this strategy unfolded along the great river networks connecting the Baltic Sea to the Black Sea. Byzantine missionaries traveled north, translating scripture into newly adapted alphabets; while Saints Cyril and Methodius devised the earlier Glagolitic script, the Cyrillic alphabet was developed soon after by their disciples in the region to allow Slavic populations to read religious texts in their native tongues.

This cultural outreach achieved a monumental milestone in 988 CE with the Christianization of Kievan Rus, a powerful East Slavic state centered around modern Ukraine. According to medieval chronicles, Prince Vladimir of Kiev sent emissaries abroad to evaluate different religions. When these envoys attended a magnificent liturgy inside the great domed cathedral of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, they reported back that they did not know whether they were in heaven or on earth. Prince Vladimir chose Orthodox Christianity, married a Byzantine princess, and mandated baptism for his people. This pivotal decision bound the cultural trajectory of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus firmly to the Byzantine sphere, shaping their architecture, governance, law, and worldview for centuries to come.

Through its grand capital, its vigilant preservation of classical learning, and its patient diplomacy, the Byzantine Empire bridged antiquity and the Middle Ages. It created a durable Orthodox civilization that reshaped the map of Eastern Europe just as dynamic new religious and political movements were rising in the Arabian Peninsula.

## Key ideas
- The Byzantine Empire was the continuation of the Eastern Roman Empire, maintaining Roman statecraft and Greek culture from its capital at Constantinople while the Western Roman world fragmented after 476 CE.
- Byzantine scholars and monastics preserved priceless classical Greek and Roman literature, philosophy, and legal traditions, including Justinian's monumental legal code.
- A unique synthesis of Greek language, imperial authority, and Christian theology fostered the development of Eastern Orthodoxy, which eventually split from Western Catholicism in 1054 CE.
- Byzantine missionaries and diplomats spread literacy and religion northward into Eastern Europe, supporting the development of the Cyrillic alphabet and successfully converting Kievan Rus to Orthodox Christianity in 988 CE.
