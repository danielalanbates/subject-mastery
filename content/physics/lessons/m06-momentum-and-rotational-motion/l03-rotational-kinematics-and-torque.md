# Rotational Kinematics and Torque

Imagine a figure skater spinning on the tip of a skate. She begins in a wide, slow blur and then pulls her arms tight to her chest, instantly rocketing into a dizzying blur of motion. In our last lesson, we explored how objects travel through space and collide, keeping the total linear momentum conserved. But linear motion along a straight line cannot fully describe things that spin in place. To understand the physics of a spinning world, we need to translate our familiar concepts of distance, speed, and force into a new language designed specifically for rotation.

Let us start by looking at how we describe position when a wheel turns or a planet rotates. Instead of measuring linear distance in meters along a straight path, we measure angle, often using radians, where one full circle equals two times pi radians. When a rigid object rotates, every point on that object sweeps through the exact same angle in the exact same amount of time, regardless of how far that point sits from the center.

Building on angular position, we arrive at angular velocity, represented by the Greek letter omega. Angular velocity tells us how fast an angle changes over time, measured in radians per second. If angular velocity changes, the object experiences angular acceleration, represented by the Greek letter alpha, measured in radians per second squared. These three rotational quantities match the linear quantities of displacement, velocity, and acceleration we studied earlier. If an object has a constant angular acceleration, we can calculate its motion using rotational kinematic equations that mirror our linear formulas. The final angular velocity equals the initial angular velocity plus the angular acceleration multiplied by time. The angular displacement equals the initial angular velocity multiplied by time plus one half times the angular acceleration multiplied by the square of time.

While every point on a rotating wheel shares the same angular speed, points farther from the center travel faster along their circular paths than points near the middle. We connect this linear movement along the edge to our rotational measures using the radius of the circular path. The linear tangential speed of any point equals the radius multiplied by the angular velocity. Similarly, the tangential acceleration of that point equals the radius multiplied by the angular acceleration.

Yet, just as linear acceleration does not happen without a linear force, angular acceleration does not happen without something causing a twist. That twisting agent is torque. If you want to open a heavy door, you do not push near the hinge; you push near the doorknob, as far from the hinge as possible. You are applying torque. Torque depends on three things: the magnitude of the force applied, the distance from the pivot point to the spot where the force is applied, and the angle at which you push.

To maximize torque, you apply your force perpendicular to the lever arm. Imagine tightening a stubborn bolt with a wrench. If you push straight down at the very end of the wrench, you create a large torque because the distance from the pivot is large and your push is perpendicular. If you push halfway up the wrench, you cut your torque in half. If you push directly toward or away from the bolt, your torque drops to zero, no matter how hard you push. Torque is measured in newton meters.

Let us walk through a quick example. Suppose you are using a standard wrench that is zero point five meters long to loosen a rusted bolt. You apply a force of twenty newtons straight down at the very end of the wrench, making a right angle with the handle. To find the resulting torque, you multiply the force of twenty newtons by the distance of zero point five meters. That gives you a torque of ten newton meters. A torque of ten newton meters is enough to start turning many household fasteners, providing a clear, satisfying sense check for our calculation.

Notice that torque is not a force itself, but rather a measure of how effectively a force causes an object to rotate around a pivot. When you apply unbalanced torques to an object, you change its rotational state, speeding up or slowing down its spin. As we move forward, we will see how this rotational counterpart to force combines with mass to govern the total angular momentum of spinning systems.

## Key ideas
- Rotational kinematics describes spinning motion using angular displacement, angular velocity, and angular acceleration governed by equations analogous to linear motion.
- Tangential speed and tangential acceleration directly link linear motion along a circular path to angular velocity and angular acceleration through the radius.
- Every point on a rigidly rotating object shares the same angular velocity and angular acceleration regardless of its distance from the axis of rotation.
- Torque is the rotational equivalent of force, measuring the effectiveness of a push or pull in causing an object to rotate around a pivot.
- The magnitude of torque depends directly on the applied force, the length of the lever arm from the pivot, and the sine of the angle of the applied force.
