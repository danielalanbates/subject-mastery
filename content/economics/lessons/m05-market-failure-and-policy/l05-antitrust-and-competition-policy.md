# Antitrust and Competition Policy

Imagine walking into your local grocery store to buy cereal, only to discover that twenty different colorful boxes are all owned by the exact same parent corporation. On the surface, you see variety. Beneath the surface, you face a single, unified pricing strategy. Left entirely to themselves, successful market economies have a natural gravitational pull toward consolidation. Companies merge, acquire rivals, and build fortresses of market power to protect their profits. While our previous lesson explored how hidden information distorts markets, today we examine how governments step in when private businesses grow so large or collude so closely that they crush competition altogether.

In the United States, primary responsibility for enforcing antitrust policy rests with two federal agencies: the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission. These agencies monitor commercial behavior to prevent firms from artificially restricting output or inflating prices. In doing so, regulators distinguish between conduct that is inherently harmful and conduct that requires context.

Certain actions are classified as per se illegal, meaning they are prohibited by their very nature without any need to prove specific economic harm. Agreements among direct competitors to fix prices, rig bids, or allocate sales territories fall into this category. Because these practices lack any genuine economic justification, courts declare them illegal on their face. By contrast, most other commercial practices are evaluated under the rule-of-reason standard. Under a rule-of-reason analysis, regulators and courts weigh the potential anti-competitive harms of a business practice against its potential pro-competitive efficiencies, such as cost savings or improved product quality. Arrangements like exclusive dealing agreements or tying products together are allowed if their economic benefits outweigh their restrictive effects.

Regulatory agencies also evaluate proposed corporate mergers, dividing them into horizontal and vertical combinations. A vertical merger joins companies operating at different stages of the supply chain, such as an automobile manufacturer buying a tire producer. A horizontal merger combines direct rivals selling competing products in the same market, such as two major airlines merging into one. Because horizontal mergers directly eliminate a competitor and reduce consumer choice, they generally raise far greater competitive concerns than vertical mergers.

To assess horizontal mergers objectively, regulators analyze market concentration using a tool called the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index. To calculate this index, economists square the market share percentage of every firm in an industry and sum the results. For example, in a market shared equally by ten firms, each firm holds a ten percent share. Squaring ten gives one hundred, and adding those ten squared values yields an index of one thousand. If two of those firms merge, market concentration rises. Under the 2023 United States merger guidelines, a market with an index above eighteen hundred is highly concentrated, and a merger that raises the index by more than one hundred points in such a market is presumed to harm competition. Earlier guidelines used thresholds of twenty-five hundred and two hundred. In these cases, the government can challenge the deal in court or require structural remedies, like forcing the merging companies to sell off specific divisions to an independent competitor.

Economists do not always agree on how aggressively antitrust laws should be enforced. Economists and lawyers have debated the proper goal of competition policy for decades. Older approaches focused on maintaining decentralized market structures to protect smaller firms and preserve economic democracy. The Chicago School, however, argued that antitrust policy should focus strictly on consumer welfare, measured by lower prices and expanded output. Under this view, if a massive horizontal merger allows a firm to reduce unit costs through economies of scale, consumers might benefit even if fewer independent sellers remain in the market. More recently, a group often called the neo-Brandeisians, after the early twentieth-century judge Louis Brandeis, has argued for stronger enforcement against today's largest technology firms, a debate that remains open.

Beyond merger review, competition policy prohibits exclusionary tactics like predatory pricing, where a dominant firm temporarily prices products below cost to drive smaller competitors into bankruptcy before raising prices back up. By enforcing clear rules, competition policy aims to keep markets dynamic, fair, and open to innovative newcomers.

## Key ideas
- Antitrust policy in the United States is enforced by the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission to protect competitive markets.
- Certain practices like price fixing and bid rigging are per se illegal, whereas other practices are evaluated under the rule of reason by balancing competitive harms against economic efficiencies.
- Horizontal mergers combine direct competitors and receive intense regulatory scrutiny, often evaluated using the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index where a post-merger score above twenty-five hundred indicates high concentration.
- Economists debate whether antitrust policy should preserve decentralized market structures or focus exclusively on consumer welfare measured by prices and output.
- In addition to reviewing mergers, regulators prohibit exclusionary practices like predatory pricing to maintain open and contestable markets.
