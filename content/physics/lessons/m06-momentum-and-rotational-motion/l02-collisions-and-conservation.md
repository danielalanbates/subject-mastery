# Collisions and Conservation

Imagine watching two billiard balls glide across a smooth green felt table. One ball travels straight ahead and strikes a resting ball head-on. The moving ball stops dead, and the resting ball instantly takes off with the exact same speed. In our last lesson, we saw how an impulse, which is a force applied over a time interval, changes an object's momentum. Now we shift our focus to what happens when two or more objects interact with each other during a brief impact. The total momentum of a closed system before a collision always equals the total momentum of that same system right after the collision. This powerful principle is known as the law of conservation of momentum.

To understand why this conservation law holds true, think back to Newton's third law of motion. When the two billiard balls collide, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other for the exact same fraction of a second. According to the definition of impulse, each ball experiences an equal and opposite change in momentum. The momentum lost by the first ball is precisely gained by the second ball. Because these internal forces cancel out when we look at the system as a whole, the overall momentum remains entirely unchanged. Nature keeps a strict ledger of motion, and nothing is ever lost in the exchange.

Collisions generally fall into two broad categories depending on how the objects behave after they meet. The first category is an elastic collision. In an ideal elastic collision, objects bounce off each other so perfectly that no mechanical energy is lost to heat, sound, or permanent deformation. Our billiard ball example comes very close to this ideal. The second category is an inelastic collision. In an inelastic collision, some of the initial kinetic energy transforms into other forms of energy, such as thermal energy or the sound of a crunch. If two objects smash together and stick permanently to each other, we call that a completely inelastic collision.

Let us walk through a concrete example of a completely inelastic collision using round numbers. Imagine a freight car with a mass of ten thousand kilograms rolling along a flat track at a speed of four meters per second. It couples automatically with a stationary freight car that also has a mass of ten thousand kilograms. Before the coupling, the total momentum of the system is the mass of the first car multiplied by its speed, giving forty thousand kilogram meters per second. The second car contributes zero momentum because its speed is zero.

After the coupling, the two cars lock together and move as a single combined mass of twenty thousand kilograms. Because the law of conservation of momentum requires the total momentum after the collision to match the total momentum before, the new combined mass must still hold forty thousand kilogram meters per second of momentum. To find the final speed, we divide that total momentum by the new total mass. Forty thousand divided by twenty thousand gives us a final speed of two meters per second. A quick sense check confirms our result makes physical sense. By doubling the moving mass while keeping the total momentum constant, the speed must drop by half, down to two meters per second.

Now let us examine an elastic collision where objects bounce apart, which requires us to use both the conservation of momentum and the conservation of mechanical energy. Imagine a moving ball with a mass of two kilograms traveling at a speed of three meters per second, striking a stationary ball with a mass of one kilogram head-on. Because momentum is conserved, the total momentum before the collision is six kilogram meters per second, which must equal the combined momentum of both balls after they bounce. At the same time, because the collision is perfectly elastic, the total kinetic energy before the collision must equal the total kinetic energy after the collision. By setting up these two simultaneous equations, we can solve for the unknown final speeds of both objects. For our example, the first ball slows down to one meter per second, and the second ball springs forward at a speed of four meters per second. A sense check shows that the total momentum of six is preserved, and the total kinetic energy is also conserved, confirming our mathematical model.

Real-world impacts often involve partial energy loss rather than perfect elasticity. In a car crash investigation, experts measure skid marks and vehicle damage to work backward using these conservation laws. By knowing the masses of the vehicles and their post-collision velocities, forensic scientists can calculate the speeds of the cars right before the impact occurred. These calculations help determine fault and improve safety barrier designs on highways. As we look ahead to our next lesson, we will transition from linear motion to rotational kinematics and torque, exploring how spinning objects also obey strict conservation laws.

## Key ideas
- The law of conservation of momentum states that the total momentum of a closed system remains constant before and after any collision.
- Internal forces during a collision are equal and opposite, meaning any momentum lost by one object is completely gained by another.
- In elastic collisions, objects bounce apart without any loss of total mechanical energy, and predicting their outcomes requires applying both momentum and kinetic energy conservation.
- In inelastic collisions, kinetic energy is partially transformed into other forms of energy such as heat or sound, sometimes resulting in objects sticking together.
