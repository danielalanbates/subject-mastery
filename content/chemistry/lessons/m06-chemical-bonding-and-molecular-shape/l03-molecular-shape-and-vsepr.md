# Molecular Shape and VSEPR Theory

Imagine you are holding a handful of balloons. If you tie four of them together at their necks, they will not clump into a flat square. Instead, they will naturally push against each other until they form a three-dimensional shape that looks like a tripod with a fourth balloon reaching toward the sky. This simple physical arrangement is the secret to understanding how molecules behave. In our last lesson, we used Lewis structures to map out which atoms are connected to which. But those flat diagrams on a page are just a starting point. To truly understand chemistry, we must step into the third dimension.

The tool we use to predict these shapes is called the valence shell electron pair repulsion theory, or VSEPR. The core idea is elegant and intuitive. Electrons are negatively charged, and like charges repel each other. Whether those electrons are busy forming a chemical bond or just sitting as a lone pair on a central atom, they want as much personal space as possible. They will position themselves around the central atom to get as far away from one another as they can.

To predict these shapes systematically, we use the steric number, which is the sum of the number of atoms bonded to the central atom and the number of lone pairs on that central atom. We also use AXE notation, where A represents the central atom, X represents a bonded atom, and E represents a lone pair.

When a central atom has a steric number of two, the electron groups arrange themselves linearly with a bond angle of one hundred eighty degrees, such as in carbon dioxide. With a steric number of three, the groups form a trigonal planar geometry with bond angles of one hundred twenty degrees, seen in boron trifluoride. A steric number of four results in a tetrahedral electron geometry with bond angles of one hundred nine point five degrees.

We must distinguish between electron-pair geometry and molecular shape. Electron-pair geometry refers to the arrangement of all electron groups. Molecular shape describes only the positions of the atoms. If there are no lone pairs, the two concepts are identical. However, lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs, which forces the atoms into different arrangements and slightly shrinks the bond angles.

For example, in methane, which is C H four, the central carbon has four bonding pairs and zero lone pairs. It is a perfect tetrahedron. In ammonia, which is N H three, the central nitrogen has three bonding pairs and one lone pair. The electron geometry is tetrahedral, but the molecular shape is a trigonal pyramid with bond angles of about one hundred seven degrees. In water, or H two O, the oxygen atom has two bonding pairs and two lone pairs. This results in a bent molecular shape with bond angles of roughly one hundred four point five degrees.

Nature also allows for higher coordination numbers. A steric number of five creates a trigonal bipyramidal electron geometry. If lone pairs are present, we get shapes like the seesaw or T-shaped molecule. A steric number of six leads to an octahedral electron geometry. If lone pairs replace bonding positions here, we can see shapes such as square pyramidal or square planar.

When you analyze a molecule, start by drawing the Lewis structure. Count the electron groups around the central atom to find the steric number. Remember that a double or triple bond counts as only one group because the electrons are held in the same region of space. Once you have that count, you can determine the underlying geometry. From there, account for the lone pairs to identify the actual shape of the atoms. Mastering this spatial thinking allows you to predict how molecules interact with light and react with other substances. These specific shapes are the primary reason why some molecules are polar and others are not.

## Key ideas

- The valence shell electron pair repulsion theory explains that electron groups arrange themselves to minimize electrostatic repulsion, resulting in predictable geometric patterns.
- The steric number, calculated as the sum of bonded atoms and lone pairs, determines the base electron-pair geometry of a molecule.
- Molecular shape describes the arrangement of atoms, which may differ from the electron-pair geometry when lone pairs are present because lone pairs exert greater repulsive force.
- Systematic geometries range from linear and trigonal planar to tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, and octahedral, with specific bond angles defined by the number of electron groups.
