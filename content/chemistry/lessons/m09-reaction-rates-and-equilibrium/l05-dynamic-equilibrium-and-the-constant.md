# Dynamic Equilibrium and the Equilibrium Constant

Imagine you are standing in a busy airport terminal. People are moving through the sliding glass doors in both directions. Passengers are walking into the terminal to catch flights, while an equal number of people are walking out to meet their rides. From a distance, the total number of people inside the terminal stays exactly the same, even though individual people are constantly entering and leaving. This is a perfect analogy for dynamic equilibrium in chemistry. In our previous look at reaction mechanisms, we saw how molecules collide to form products. Many chemical reactions, however, do not simply race to completion. Instead, they reach a state where the forward reaction and the reverse reaction happen at the same time and at the same speed.

When a chemical reaction is reversible, the products can react with each other to turn back into the original reactants. We represent this with a double arrow, indicating that the process flows in both directions. Dynamic equilibrium occurs when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. The system is dynamic because the reactions never actually stop; they continue to churn away at the molecular level. It is balanced because the concentrations of the reactants and products remain constant over time. It is a state of perpetual motion that results in a stable, unchanging outcome.

To describe this balance mathematically, chemists use the equilibrium constant. You can think of this constant as a fingerprint for a specific reaction at a specific temperature. To write the expression for this constant, we take the concentrations of the products and multiply them together, then divide that result by the multiplied concentrations of the reactants. If any substance has a coefficient in the balanced chemical equation, that number becomes an exponent in our expression. For example, if two moles of a substance react, we square its concentration in our calculation.

Chemists use different units depending on the state of the substances involved. For reactions in a liquid solution, we express these concentrations in units of moles per liter, and we call the resulting constant the equilibrium constant in terms of concentration. For gas-phase reactions, we often express the constant in terms of partial pressures, and we call this the equilibrium constant in terms of pressure. These two constants are related, but they are not identical.

The numerical value of the equilibrium constant tells us a great deal about the position of the equilibrium. If the constant is very large, much greater than one, it tells us that the products dominate the mixture. The reaction has essentially shifted toward the right, favoring the formation of products. Conversely, if the constant is very small, much less than one, the reactants are favored. The system remains mostly composed of the starting materials. A value near one suggests that both reactants and products are present in significant, comparable amounts when the system settles into its stable state.

Sometimes, we need to know if a reaction is currently at equilibrium or if it is still drifting toward that state. We use the reaction quotient for this purpose. The reaction quotient is calculated exactly like the equilibrium constant, using the current concentrations or partial pressures of the substances at any given moment. By comparing the reaction quotient to the known equilibrium constant, we can predict the direction of the net reaction. If the quotient is smaller than the constant, the reaction must proceed in the forward direction to generate more products. If the quotient is larger than the constant, the reaction must reverse to consume excess products and form more reactants.

Let us walk through a simple calculation using concentrations. Consider a reaction where one molecule of nitrogen gas reacts with three molecules of hydrogen gas to produce two molecules of ammonia. Suppose we find that at equilibrium, the concentration of nitrogen is one mole per liter, hydrogen is two moles per liter, and ammonia is four moles per liter. To find the equilibrium constant, we place the product, ammonia, on top. We square its concentration because of the two in the balanced equation, giving us four times four, which is sixteen. On the bottom, we place the nitrogen concentration of one, multiplied by the hydrogen concentration of two cubed. Since two cubed is eight, our denominator is one times eight, which equals eight. Dividing sixteen by eight gives us an equilibrium constant of two.

This value of two indicates that the products are favored at this temperature. By mastering how to write these expressions and interpret these values, you gain the ability to predict the behavior of chemical systems before they ever reach the lab bench.

## Key ideas

- Dynamic equilibrium occurs in reversible reactions when the forward and reverse reaction rates are equal, resulting in constant concentrations or partial pressures for all substances.
- The equilibrium constant is calculated by multiplying product values and dividing by reactant values, with each value raised to the power of its stoichiometric coefficient.
- Chemists use the equilibrium constant in terms of concentration for solutions and the equilibrium constant in terms of pressure for gas-phase reactions.
- A very large equilibrium constant indicates that the reaction favors products, while a very small constant indicates that the reaction favors reactants.
- The reaction quotient allows us to compare the current state of a reaction mixture to its equilibrium state to predict whether the forward or reverse reaction will proceed.
