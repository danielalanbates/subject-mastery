# Latin American Wars of Independence

Imagine standing in a grand cathedral in Lima or Mexico City at the turn of the nineteenth century. The incense rises toward gilded ceilings, and the congregation kneels beneath the watchful eyes of Spanish bishops and royal officials. For three centuries, this transatlantic empire seemed permanent, anchored by absolute monarchs and an unyielding caste system. Yet within two decades, this entire structure shattered. Armies marched across the Andes, royal fleets retreated across the Atlantic, and a constellation of new republics emerged from the ruins of Iberian rule.

To understand how this collapse happened, we must look at the rigid social hierarchy that Spain and Portugal had constructed in the Americas. At the summit stood the peninsulares, officials born in Europe who monopolized the highest offices of church and state. Just below them were the criollos, people of Spanish descent born in the Americas. Criollos often owned vast plantations and mines, yet they were systematically excluded from governing their own lands. Beneath them laboured a vast majority of indigenous peoples, enslaved Africans, and castas, individuals of mixed racial ancestry. As Enlightenment ideas trickled across the ocean, and as enslaved revolutionaries in Saint-Domingue proved that colonial masters could be overthrown, criollo elites began to question whether Madrid had any legitimate right to rule them.

The catalyst for revolution did not come from an uprising in the colonies, but from a crisis in Europe. In 1808, Napoleon Bonaparte invaded the Iberian Peninsula, imprisoned the Spanish king Ferdinand VII, and placed his own brother on the throne. This sparked a profound constitutional crisis. If the legitimate monarch was gone, where did sovereignty reside? In Spain, resistance committees formed to govern in the king's name. In the Americas, criollo leaders argued that if the king was deposed, sovereignty reverted to the people, creating local municipal councils, known as juntas, to govern autonomously. When the Spanish monarchy was restored in 1814, King Ferdinand attempted to turn back the clock and reassert absolute control. It was too late. The colonies had tasted self-government, and compromise was no longer possible.

From this crisis emerged two of the most celebrated military figures in modern history: Simón Bolívar in the north and José de San Martín in the south. Bolívar, an aristocrat from Caracas steeped in Enlightenment philosophy, envisioned a united South American republic. His campaigns were breathtaking in their audacity, most famously crossing the freezing peaks of the Andes in 1819 to surprise royalist forces in New Granada and liberate Colombia. Meanwhile, San Martín built an army in Argentina, crossed the southern Andes with artillery and supplies, and liberated Chile before sailing north to strike at the heart of Spanish power in Peru.

While the northern and southern campaigns captured the imagination, events in Mexico followed a different and far more radical path. In 1810, a parish priest named Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla rang his church bell in the small town of Dolores and issued a passionate cry for social and economic justice. Unlike the elite-led movements in South America, Hidalgo's rebellion instantly mobilized tens of thousands of indigenous peasants and mestizos who demanded the return of stolen lands and an end to caste oppression. Alarmed by this social upheaval, criollo elites joined royalist forces to crush the peasant revolt and execute Hidalgo. Mexico would eventually achieve independence in 1821 under a conservative criollo general, Agustín de Iturbide, who preserved the old social hierarchy while cutting ties with Spain.

Portuguese Brazil experienced a remarkably peaceful transition. When Napoleon invaded Portugal, the royal family fled across the Atlantic and established Rio de Janeiro as the heart of the Portuguese Empire. When the king returned to Lisbon in 1821, he left his son Pedro behind as regent. Recognizing the unstoppable tide of independence, Prince Pedro declared Brazil an independent empire in 1822, crowning himself emperor. By avoiding a protracted war, Brazil retained its monarchy and its plantation economy, complete with the brutal institution of chattel slavery.

Nation-building across the rest of Latin America proved extraordinarily difficult. The wars had devastated agricultural lands, destroyed silver mines, and depleted the treasury. Bolívar watched his grand dream of a unified northern republic disintegrate into regional factionalism, famously lamenting that trying to unite Latin America was like ploughing the sea. Political power fell largely to regional military strongmen known as caudillos, who ruled through personal charisma and force. Yet despite decades of instability, these newly independent nations had permanently shattered the Iberian colonial order. As these societies wrestled with the painful legacies of colonialism and the search for economic survival, their gaze soon shifted across the Atlantic, where a different kind of revolution, driven by coal, steam, and factories, was transforming the global economy.

## Key ideas
- The Latin American wars of independence dismantled three centuries of Spanish and Portuguese colonial rule between 1810 and 1825.
- Napoleon's invasion of the Iberian Peninsula in 1808 created a constitutional crisis that triggered the formation of autonomous local governing councils in the Americas.
- Military leaders like Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín liberated vast regions through daring cross-Andean military campaigns.
- Social divisions ran deep, as demonstrated when Mexican peasant uprisings frightened criollo elites into pursuing conservative paths to independence.
- Newly formed nations struggled with post-war devastation, political instability under regional strongmen, and the lasting economic legacies of colonialism.
