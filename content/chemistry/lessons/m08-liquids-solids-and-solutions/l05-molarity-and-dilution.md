# Molarity and Dilution

Imagine you are in a kitchen, preparing a pitcher of iced tea. If you add one spoonful of sugar to a gallon of water, the tea tastes barely sweet. If you add ten spoonfuls, the flavor is intense. In chemistry, we need a way to describe this intensity—the relative amount of a substance dissolved in a solvent—with exact numerical precision. While our previous lesson explored the general concept of solubility and the limits of how much solute can dissolve in a solvent, today we focus on quantifying that relationship. This is essential because, in a laboratory or industrial setting, the effectiveness of a chemical reaction often depends entirely on the specific concentration of the reactants.

The most common way chemists express concentration is through molarity. Molarity is defined as the number of moles of solute dissolved in one liter of total solution. Notice that the volume here refers to the total volume of the solution, not just the volume of the solvent you started with. To calculate molarity, you divide the moles of your solute by the total liters of the solution. For instance, if you dissolve zero point five moles of sodium chloride in enough water to create exactly two liters of liquid, you calculate the molarity by dividing zero point five moles by two liters. This gives you a concentration of zero point two five moles per liter.

Preparing a solution of a specific molarity requires careful technique. You would not simply add a set amount of solute to a liter of water, because the solute itself takes up space and changes the final volume. Instead, you weigh out the solid solute, place it in a specialized glass container called a volumetric flask, and add enough solvent to dissolve the solid. Only after the solid is dissolved do you add more solvent until the liquid reaches the precise calibration mark on the neck of the flask. This ensures that the final total volume is exactly the volume marked on the flask.

There is one important caveat to keep in mind regarding molarity: it is temperature dependent. Because liquids expand or contract as their temperature changes, the total volume of a solution will shift if the temperature rises or falls. Since the number of moles of solute remains constant, the molarity will change as the volume changes. For high-precision work, chemists must account for these temperature fluctuations.

Sometimes, you need a solution that is less concentrated than your stock supply. This is where the process of dilution comes in. Dilution involves adding more solvent to a concentrated solution to lower its molarity. Because you are only adding solvent, the total number of moles of solute remains constant. This leads us to a very useful mathematical relationship: the initial molarity multiplied by the initial volume is equal to the final molarity multiplied by the final volume.

If you have one hundred milliliters of a two moles per liter solution of hydrochloric acid and you want to dilute it to a one mole per liter concentration, you can use this formula to find the required final volume. First, multiply the initial concentration of two moles per liter by the initial volume of one hundred milliliters to get two hundred millimoles. Next, set this equal to the final concentration of one mole per liter multiplied by the unknown final volume. Dividing two hundred millimoles by one mole per liter gives you a final volume of two hundred milliliters. You would simply add solvent until the total volume reaches that two hundred milliliter mark.

While molarity is the gold standard for laboratory chemistry because it links directly to the mole, other units are useful in different contexts. Mass percent is a simple ratio of the mass of the solute divided by the total mass of the solution, multiplied by one hundred to get a percentage. This is often used for commercial products like household bleach or saline solutions. For extremely dilute substances, such as trace contaminants in drinking water or pollutants in the air, we use parts per million. A part per million represents one gram of solute for every one million grams of solution, which, for a dilute solution in water, is about one milligram per liter. These units allow us to describe concentrations that are far too small to be expressed conveniently as molarity.

Whether we are measuring the salinity of the ocean in parts per million or preparing a precise reagent for a titration, these concentration units give us the tools to communicate how much matter is present in a given space. Understanding how concentration affects the behavior of liquids and solids provides the foundation for predicting how these substances will interact when they are mixed.

## Key ideas

- Molarity is the standard unit of concentration in chemistry, defined as the number of moles of solute per liter of total solution.
- Because the volume of a liquid changes with temperature, the molarity of a solution is also temperature dependent.
- To prepare a solution of a specific molarity, a chemist must ensure the final total volume of the mixture reaches the desired mark in a volumetric flask.
- Dilution is the process of adding solvent to a solution to decrease its concentration while keeping the total number of moles of solute constant.
- Alternative units like mass percent and parts per million provide convenient ways to express concentration for commercial products or extremely dilute environmental samples.
