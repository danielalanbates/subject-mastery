# The World Between the Testaments

Imagine walking into a room where the furniture has been completely rearranged while you were away. That is what a reader experiences stepping from the last pages of the Old Testament into the opening chapters of the New Testament. In our last lesson, we traced how the Hebrew prophets nurtured deep longings for a divine king and a restored Israel. Yet when the curtain rises on the Gospels, the political map, the religious vocabulary, and the daily language of the Jewish people have undergone a radical transformation. Four centuries separate the closing of the prophetic books from the birth of Jesus, a period rich in political upheaval, literary activity, and intense religious creativity that we call the Second Temple period.

To understand the world of Jesus, we must first look at the shifting empires that ruled the Judean landscape. Following the Persian period, which fostered the return from exile, Alexander the Great swept across the ancient world in the fourth century BCE, bringing Greek culture, language, and philosophy in his wake. This process of Hellenization created deep tensions within Jewish society. Some embraced Greek education and city life, while others fiercely resisted any threat to the Torah. After Alexander's death, his generals divided his empire. Judea eventually fell under the control of the Seleucid dynasty, based in Syria.

The boiling point arrived in the second century BCE under the Seleucid ruler Antiochus Epiphanes. Seeking to unify his realm, Antiochus outlawed traditional Jewish practices, desecrated the temple in Jerusalem, and demanded worship of Greek deities. This tyranny sparked the Maccabean Revolt, a guerrilla war led by a priestly family known as the Hasmoneans. Remarkably, the rebels won, cleansing the temple and restoring an independent Jewish kingdom for the first time in centuries. That historic victory is still remembered today in the Jewish festival of Hanukkah.

Yet independence brought its own internal corruption, and by 63 BCE, Roman legions under Pompey marched into Jerusalem, turning Judea into a client kingdom. Rome soon installed local rulers, most notably Herod the Great, a master builder and ruthless politician who governed Judea as a Roman client king at the time of Jesus' birth. Herod rebuilt the Jerusalem temple on a magnificent scale, but his paranoia and cruelty left a dark mark on the populace. Roman occupation cast a long shadow, burdening the people with heavy taxes and stationing pagan soldiers in the holy land. For a devout Jew, daily life under Caesar and the Herodian dynasty was a constant reminder that the promises of political freedom had not yet materialized.

This pressurized political environment fueled a vibrant and diverse religious landscape. Far from being a monolith, Second Temple Judaism housed several distinct movements. The Pharisees emerged as a movement dedicated to rigorous obedience to the law of Moses, extending ritual purity rules beyond the temple into everyday domestic life. They believed in the resurrection of the dead and valued both the written scriptures and oral tradition. In sharp contrast stood the Sadducees, who were closely tied to the Jerusalem temple establishment and aristocratic priestly families. The Sadducees accepted only the written Torah as authoritative, rejected the resurrection of the dead, and pursued a pragmatic relationship with Rome. Meanwhile, the Essenes retreated to desert communities, convinced that the temple priesthood was corrupt and awaiting an apocalyptic intervention from God. Revolutionary factions also grew increasingly restless, believing that armed rebellion was the only faithful response to Rome.

Amid these social divisions, crucial institutions and texts shaped everyday Jewish life. The synagogue, whose exact origins are debated but whose earliest clear evidence comes from Egypt in the third century BCE, became a central institution for local Jewish prayer, community gathering, and the teaching of the Torah, ensuring that faith could thrive far beyond the Jerusalem altar. At the same time, the scattering of the Jewish people across the Mediterranean led to the creation of the Septuagint, a Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible made in Alexandria. The Septuagint became the primary Bible for Greek-speaking Jews and later served as the scriptural foundation for the expanding early Christian communities.

Beneath all these developments lay a shared, burning hope for deliverance, often stoked by apocalyptic literature such as the biblical book of Daniel, alongside non-canonical writings like First Enoch and, after the temple’s fall in 70 CE, Fourth Ezra. These apocalyptic texts used vivid, visionary language to assure suffering believers that God would soon judge wicked empires and establish His eternal kingdom. Whether looking for a priestly messiah, a royal warrior like King David, or a heavenly figure like the Son of Man, the people of Judea were watching and waiting. The stage was set for a dramatic intervention through the entry of the divine Word into human history.

## Key ideas
- The Second Temple period spans the centuries between the Old and New Testaments, marked by profound political shifts under Greek and Roman imperial rule, including the reign of Herodian client kings like Herod the Great.
- The development of the synagogue as a local center for worship and Torah study, alongside the creation of the Septuagint Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, profoundly shaped both Jewish and early Christian communities.
- Apocalyptic literature, including books like Daniel, First Enoch, and Fourth Ezra, flourished during this era and powerfully shaped contemporary messianic expectations.
- Diverse religious movements, including Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, and revolutionary groups, developed distinct strategies for maintaining faithfulness to God under foreign occupation.
