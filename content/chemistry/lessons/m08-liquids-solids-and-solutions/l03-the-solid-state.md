# The Solid State

Pick up a common diamond ring and then a piece of candle wax. Both are solids, yet they behave in drastically different ways. The diamond is famously hard and refuses to melt until it reaches temperatures hot enough to destroy most lab equipment. The wax, by contrast, is soft and begins to slump into a liquid puddle on a warm summer day. Why do these materials occupy the same state of matter while possessing such opposite physical traits? The answer lies in the microscopic architecture of the solid state.

In our last lesson, we explored how substances transition between phases based on pressure and temperature. Now, we zoom in to see how particles are organized within those phases. Solids fall into two broad categories: crystalline and amorphous. In a crystalline solid, the atoms or ions or molecules are arranged in a highly ordered, repeating pattern that extends in all three dimensions. Think of this like a perfectly organized brick wall. Amorphous solids, however, lack this long-range order. Their particles are jumbled together, more like a pile of disorganized gravel. Glass and many plastics are classic examples of amorphous solids. Because they lack a rigid, repeating lattice, they do not have a sharp melting point; instead, they gradually soften as they are heated.

When we look at crystalline solids, we can classify them further based on the particles that make up the structure and the forces holding them together. The first type is the ionic solid. These consist of positive ions and negative ions held together by strong electrostatic attractions. Common table salt, or sodium chloride, is the quintessential example. Because these electrostatic forces are powerful and extend in every direction, ionic solids are hard, brittle, and have very high melting points. They do not conduct electricity in their solid form because the ions are locked in place, but they become excellent conductors once melted.

Next, we have molecular solids. These are made of individual molecules, such as sucrose or water in the form of ice, held together by intermolecular forces like hydrogen bonding or London dispersion forces. Because these intermolecular forces are much weaker than the ionic bonds we just discussed, molecular solids are generally soft and have low melting points. They are also poor conductors of electricity, as there are no free-moving charged particles.

Then, there are the covalent network solids. These are essentially one giant molecule held together by a continuous network of covalent bonds. Diamond is the perfect example here. Every carbon atom is covalently bonded to four other carbon atoms in a rigid, repeating tetrahedral structure. Because breaking the entire crystal requires snapping countless covalent bonds, these materials are incredibly hard and have exceptionally high melting points. They are typically insulators, though there are exceptions like graphite, which conducts electricity because of its unique layered structure.

Finally, we encounter metallic solids. These consist of metal atoms packed tightly together, where the valence electrons are not bound to any single atom. Instead, these electrons form a sea of negative charge that flows freely throughout the entire structure. This electron sea explains why metals are excellent conductors of electricity and heat. It also accounts for their malleability and ductility, as the metal atoms can slide past one another without breaking the structure, cushioned by the surrounding electrons.

To put this into practice, consider the difference between a block of copper and a block of dry ice. Copper is a metallic solid, meaning it conducts electricity well and can be hammered into a wire. Dry ice, or solid carbon dioxide, is a molecular solid. It is held together by weak dispersion forces, making it brittle and causing it to sublime at low temperatures. If you were to test their melting points, you would find that copper requires over 1,085 degrees Celsius, while dry ice disappears into a gas at roughly negative 78 degrees Celsius.

Understanding these structural differences allows us to predict how materials will interact with their environment. Whether a substance stays solid or changes phase depends heavily on how these internal forces respond to energy, which is exactly where we will head in our next discussion on how substances interact to form mixtures.

## Key ideas

- Crystalline solids possess a highly ordered, repeating internal structure, whereas amorphous solids lack long-range order and soften gradually rather than having a sharp melting point.
- Ionic solids are held by strong electrostatic attractions, resulting in high melting points and brittleness, while molecular solids are held by weaker intermolecular forces and typically have lower melting points.
- Covalent network solids consist of atoms linked by a continuous network of covalent bonds, making them extremely hard and heat resistant.
- Metallic solids are characterized by a sea of delocalized electrons that move freely, which accounts for their high electrical conductivity and their ability to be deformed without shattering.
