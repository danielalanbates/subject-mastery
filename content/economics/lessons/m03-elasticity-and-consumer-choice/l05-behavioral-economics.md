# Behavioral Economics and Choice

Imagine standing in front of a coffee shop menu offering a small coffee for two dollars, a medium for four dollars, and a large for four dollars and fifty cents. Traditional economic theory suggests that you carefully weigh the marginal utility of each ounce against its price to find your optimal consumption bundle. Yet, almost instantly, most people choose the large. Why? Because the medium coffee exists primarily as a decoy, making the large look like an incredible bargain. In our last lesson, we explored how consumers maximize utility under budget constraints using cold, calculating logic. Today, we turn to behavioral economics, a field that asks a radically different question: what happens when real human beings make economic choices using messy, emotional, and shortcut-driven brains?

Traditional economic models rest on the assumption of what scholars call rational actor theory. This framework assumes that people possess stable preferences, unlimited cognitive computing power, and an uncanny ability to optimize every purchasing decision. Behavioral economics, pioneered by psychologists and economists like Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, challenges these assumptions head-on. Empirical evidence reveals that human decision making is systematically flawed in predictable ways. We do not always maximize utility in the textbook sense. Instead, we rely on mental shortcuts, or heuristics, which help us navigate a complex world but often lead us into predictable cognitive biases.

One of the most powerful challenges to traditional theory is prospect theory, which demonstrates that people evaluate gains and losses differently. Classical theory assumes that a loss of one hundred dollars hurts just as much as a gain of one hundred dollars brings joy. Behavioral economists find that losses sting roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good. This asymmetry is known as loss aversion. If a grocery store raises the price of your favorite cereal by fifty cents, you might angrily switch brands out of proportion to the actual hit to your budget. Conversely, framing matters immensely. A merchant offering a fifty cent discount for cash versus a fifty cent surcharge for credit is describing the exact same transaction, yet consumers react with significantly more hostility to the surcharge because it feels like a painful loss.

Another major departure from rational choice is bounded rationality. Herbert Simon argued that because human beings have limited time, information, and brainpower, we do not optimize. Instead, we satisfice. We set a threshold of satisfaction and choose the first option that meets it, rather than searching exhaustively for the absolute best choice. Think about buying a tube of toothpaste. You do not analyze the chemical composition, manufacturing ethics, and price-per-millimeter of all thirty options on the shelf. You grab a familiar brand that is good enough. Traditional models struggle to account for satisficing because they treat search costs and cognitive limits as zero, when in reality, our mental bandwidth is one of our scarcest resources.

Behavioral insights also expose deep flaws in our self-control, captured by the concept of present bias. Traditional models assume time consistency, meaning your preferences today regarding next year will match your actual preferences when next year arrives. In practice, humans suffer from hyperbolic discounting. We heavily overweight immediate gratification and underweight future consequences. This explains why we eagerly subscribe to a gym membership in January with grand intentions of daily workouts, only to let it sit unused by March. Our present self hijacks the plans of our future self, leading to economic outcomes that pure rationality cannot easily explain.

Understanding these quirks is not just an academic exercise; it transforms how we design public policy and market environments. Governments and corporations now use choice architecture, subtly altering the environment in which decisions are made without restricting freedom of choice. A classic example is setting default options. When employees are automatically enrolled in a retirement savings plan unless they actively opt out, participation rates skyrocket compared to systems where employees must actively opt in. The underlying economic incentives have not changed a bit, but the psychological friction has. As we move from consumer choice into our next module, we will shift our focus from the consumer to the firm, examining how businesses manage their costs of production to supply the very goods we struggle so strangely to buy.

## Key ideas
- Traditional economic models assume consumers are fully rational optimizers with stable preferences and unlimited cognitive capacity.
- Behavioral economics uses psychological insights to show that human choices are driven by mental shortcuts, heuristics, and predictable biases.
- Loss aversion demonstrates that individuals feel the pain of a loss much more acutely than the joy of an equivalent gain.
- Concepts like bounded rationality and present bias explain why people satisfice with good enough choices and overvalue immediate gratification.
