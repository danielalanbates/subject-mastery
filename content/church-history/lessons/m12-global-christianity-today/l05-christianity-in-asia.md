# Christianity in Asia

Imagine walking through the bustling streets of Seoul, South Korea, on a Sunday morning. You might notice the skyline dominated not just by modern skyscrapers, but by the glowing neon crosses of countless church buildings. In a nation where Christianity was a tiny minority a century ago, it has become a profound social and cultural force. This rapid growth is the most striking example of the faith’s expansion in modern Asia. The Asian experience presents a narrative of resilience, adaptation, and sudden growth that differs from the story of Latin America.

In South Korea, the church’s rise is often tied to a unique blend of national identity and intense prayer movements. Following the trauma of the Korean War in the early 1950s, many citizens sought hope and community in the message of the gospel. The church became a place of education, social welfare, and spiritual renewal. Today, South Korean churches are not just local centers of worship. They are among the most active missionary-sending bodies in the world, carrying their vision of faith to distant continents. Furthermore, the rapid growth of Pentecostal and Charismatic movements has become a defining feature of the religious landscape in South Korea.

Moving to China, the story is complex and marked by the shifting tides of the 20th century. After the revolution of 1949, foreign missionaries were expelled, and the church faced severe restrictions. Yet, the faith did not vanish. It exists in two main forms. First, there is the state-sanctioned Three-Self Patriotic Movement, which operates within the legal framework established by the government. Simultaneously, a vast network of unregistered house churches has grown, where believers meet in homes to pray and study scripture. Scholars often debate the exact size of this movement, as secrecy makes accurate counting impossible, but it is clear that millions of Chinese citizens have found a home in these fellowships. Pentecostal and Charismatic expressions have also seen rapid growth within these Chinese communities.

India offers yet another perspective, as it is home to one of the oldest Christian traditions on the planet. According to long-standing local memory, the apostle Thomas arrived in India in the 1st century CE, establishing communities that have endured for nearly two thousand years. These Saint Thomas Christians, or the Nasrani, have historically lived in the southern state of Kerala. They maintain a rich, ancient liturgy and a distinct cultural identity. Alongside these ancient roots, modern Christianity in India has seen significant growth among Dalit communities, who have often found in the gospel a message of human dignity and equality that challenges traditional social hierarchies.

Across the sea in the Philippines, the story of Christianity is one of deep, centuries-long integration. Introduced by Spanish explorers and missionaries beginning in the 16th century, Roman Catholicism became the primary religious identity of the archipelago. Today, the Philippines stands out as a predominantly Catholic nation. Catholicism here is woven into the fabric of daily life, influencing national holidays, festivals, and political discourse. The Catholic Church also plays a vital role in the Philippines through its extensive network of schools, hospitals, and social service agencies. While Catholicism is the majority faith, the Philippines also hosts a notable minority of Protestant and independent churches that contribute to the nation's religious diversity.

Despite these diverse expressions, Christianity in Asia faces a common set of challenges. In many nations, Christians remain a small minority, navigating complex relationships with governments and dominant local cultures. The tension between maintaining traditional beliefs and engaging with the rapid modernization of Asian societies remains a central theme. Whether through the high-energy worship of a Seoul megachurch, the quiet devotion of a house church in a Chinese province, or the historic processions of a parish in Manila, Asian Christianity is no longer a peripheral movement. It is a vital, indigenous reality that is actively shaping the global conversation about what it means to follow Christ in the 21st century.

## Key ideas

- The rise of Christianity in South Korea is marked by rapid expansion since the mid-20th century and a strong commitment to global missionary work, alongside the growth of Pentecostal movements.
- In China, the church has persisted through a dual structure of government-registered congregations and unregistered house churches, with significant growth in Pentecostal and Charismatic movements.
- India represents a unique blend of ancient traditions, dating back to the arrival of the apostle Thomas in the 1st century CE, and modern movements that emphasize social equality.
- The Philippines maintains a distinct identity as a predominantly Catholic nation, where the church provides essential education and social services and exists alongside a notable minority of Protestant and independent churches.
