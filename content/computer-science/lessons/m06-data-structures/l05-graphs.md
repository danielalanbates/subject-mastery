# Graphs

Imagine you are standing in a large city, looking at a map of the subway system. You see dots representing stations and lines connecting those stations. This simple visual is the perfect way to understand a graph. While the trees we explored in our last lesson are structured with a clear hierarchy—a single root branching out into children—a graph is far more flexible. In a graph, any point can connect to any other point. It is the fundamental data structure for modeling complex relationships where there is no top or bottom, just a web of interconnected entities.

In computer science, we call the dots nodes and the lines edges. If you are modeling a social network, each person is a node, and an edge between two people represents a friendship. If you are modeling a road map, the intersections are nodes, and the roads are edges. Edges can be undirected, meaning the relationship goes both ways, like a two-way street or a mutual friendship. They can also be directed, meaning the connection only works in one direction, like a one-way street or a follower on a social media platform where the relationship is not necessarily reciprocated.

Because graphs are so interconnected, we need specific strategies to explore them. Imagine you are trying to find a specific person in a vast social network, or perhaps you are looking for a path through a labyrinth of hallways. One common approach is called breadth-first search. Think of this like a ripple in a pond. You start at your chosen node, then visit every immediate neighbor connected to it. Once you have seen all those immediate friends, you move to their friends, then to their friends' friends, and so on, level by level. It is systematic and ensures you cover everything close to your starting point before venturing further out.

Another strategy is the depth-first search. This approach is more like exploring a maze by keeping your hand on one wall and following it as deep as you can go. You pick a neighbor, move to it, and then immediately look for a neighbor of that new node. You keep diving deeper into the graph until you hit a dead end or a node you have already visited. Only then do you backtrack to find a different path. While breadth-first search is excellent for finding the shortest path in terms of the number of steps, depth-first search is often more memory-efficient when you are searching for a specific connection hidden deep within a massive, sprawling structure.

One of the most practical applications of graphs is finding the shortest route between two points. Imagine you are using a mapping application to drive across the country. Each city is a node, and each highway segment is an edge. Crucially, these edges have weights, which represent the time or distance it takes to travel between cities. A simple breadth-first search will not work here because it only counts the number of edges, not the cost of those edges. Instead, we use algorithms that prioritize the path with the smallest total weight.

The algorithm begins by labeling your starting city with a cost of zero and every other city with an infinite cost, signifying that we have not yet found a way to reach them. The algorithm then looks at all the neighbors of the current city, calculates the cost to reach them, and updates those values if the new path is shorter than any previously known route. It then picks the unvisited city with the lowest known cost and repeats the process. By always choosing the most promising next step, the algorithm eventually finds the most efficient path to every other point in the graph. It is a beautiful example of how we can turn a complex, messy web of connections into a precise, logical map.

Graphs allow us to represent almost any system where things interact, from the way electricity flows through a circuit to how information spreads across the internet. However, as these networks grow to include millions or billions of nodes, finding the perfect solution becomes incredibly difficult. Sometimes, we have to settle for good enough rather than perfect. As we move forward, we will see how the sheer complexity of these massive, interconnected problems pushes us to the very limits of what computers can solve in a reasonable amount of time.

## Key ideas

- A graph consists of nodes representing entities and edges representing the connections between them, allowing for non-hierarchical, complex relationships.
- Breadth-first search explores a graph level by level, making it ideal for finding the shortest path in terms of the number of connections.
- Depth-first search travels as deep as possible along a single path before backtracking, which is often more memory-efficient for specific types of searches.
- Shortest-path algorithms use weighted edges to calculate the most efficient route between nodes, accounting for factors like distance or time.
- Graphs provide the mathematical foundation for modeling real-world systems like social networks, global transportation grids, and the architecture of the internet.
