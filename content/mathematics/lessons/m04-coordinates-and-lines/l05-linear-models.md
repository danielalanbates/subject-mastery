# Linear Models in Context

Imagine you rent a moving truck that charges a flat fee of fifty dollars, plus an additional thirty dollars for every hour you keep it. If you keep the truck for three hours, how do you figure out the total cost without guessing? In our last lesson, we built equations of lines from slopes and points. Today, we put that machinery to work, turning real-world situations into linear models that let us interpret data, make predictions, and understand the limits of our math.

To build a linear model from scratch, you need two ingredients: a starting value and a constant rate. Think back to slope-intercept form, where a line is described by a slope and a y-intercept. In a real-world setting, the y-intercept is your starting value, which is what happens when your independent variable is zero. The slope is your constant rate of change, telling you how much the dependent variable increases or decreases for every single step forward in the independent variable.

Let us return to the moving truck. Let the independent variable, which we will call h, represent the number of hours. Let the dependent variable, which we will call c, represent the total cost in dollars. The company charges a flat fee of fifty dollars, which is our starting value, so fifty is the vertical intercept. Then they charge thirty dollars for every hour, which is our constant rate of change, so thirty is our slope. Putting these pieces together in an equation, we write c equals thirty times h plus fifty. This linear model tells the whole story in a single algebraic sentence.

Once you have your model, you can interpret its parts and make predictions with appropriate units. The slope of thirty has units of dollars per hour, telling us that each additional hour adds thirty dollars to the bill. The vertical intercept of fifty has units of dollars, representing the upfront cost before any hours tick by. If you need the truck for four hours, substitute four for h in the expression thirty times four plus fifty. Thirty times four is one hundred twenty, and adding fifty gives one hundred seventy dollars.

When we make predictions using our model, we must distinguish between interpolation and extrapolation. Interpolation means making a prediction within the range of data we have already observed or tested, which is generally quite reliable. Extrapolation means making a prediction beyond that known data range, which can be risky because conditions might change outside our tested window. Furthermore, every model has its limits. Can you keep the truck for negative five hours? Of course not, so the domain of our model is restricted to non-negative numbers. Can you keep it for a thousand hours? Practically speaking, the rental company would require the truck back or force a new contract, meaning our linear model stops working beyond a certain reasonable threshold.

Sometimes, two quantities are proportional, meaning one variable is a constant multiple of another. We call this direct variation. If a car travels at a steady speed of sixty miles per hour, the distance traveled is sixty times the number of hours. There is no starting value other than zero, so the line passes straight through the origin. We say that distance varies directly with time, and the constant multiplier is our slope.

Real data collected from experiments or observations rarely falls on a single straight line. Instead, points scatter across the coordinate plane like stars in a constellation. When an analyst looks at this scatter, they often seek a line of best fit, which is a single straight line that comes as close as possible to all the data points at once. Even though the individual points do not fit the rule perfectly, the line of best fit gives us a general trend, letting us summarize a messy reality with a clean, predictable rate of change and starting value.

Linear models give us a powerful lens for viewing change, but change itself takes many forms in mathematics. As we move from straight lines into our next module, we will explore the broader concept of a function, discovering how inputs and outputs relate when rules become more flexible than a simple constant slope.

## Key ideas
- A linear model requires a starting value acting as a vertical intercept and a constant rate acting as a slope, with each parameter carrying specific real-world units.
- Direct variation occurs when two variables are proportional, creating a linear model with no starting value that passes directly through the origin.
- Predictions made within the range of known data are called interpolation, while predictions made beyond that range are called extrapolation, which can be unreliable.
- Every real-world linear model has practical limits on its domain where the assumptions of constant change stop holding true.
- A line of best fit approximates scattered real-world data, revealing an underlying trend even when individual points do not fall on a straight path.
