# The Attributes and Nature of God

When historic Christian theologians began to map out the doctrine of God, they faced a profound challenge. How do we speak about a reality that exceeds human language? In our previous lesson, we looked at how Reformation-era confessions summarized the Christian faith. Now, we examine the core of that faith: who God is in Himself.

To answer this, theologians traditionally divide God's attributes into two distinct categories. First, we have the incommunicable attributes. These are the characteristics that belong to God alone, qualities that He does not share with creation. Think first of His eternity, meaning He exists outside the bounds of time. Think also of His immutability, which simply means that God does not change. In the book of Malachi chapter three, verse six, in the King James Version, God declares, "For I am the Lord, I change not," and the verse continues, "therefore ye sons of Jacob are not consumed." While human beings grow, learn, and decay over time, God remains constant in His perfection.

Classical theology adds further depth to these incommunicable traits through the doctrines of divine aseity, simplicity, omniscience, and omnipotence. Aseity means God is entirely self-existent and depends on nothing outside Himself for His being. Divine simplicity teaches that God is not a collection of separate parts. His attributes are identical with His very essence. God is not sometimes loving and sometimes just; His love and justice are His very nature. Furthermore, His omniscience means He possesses exhaustive knowledge of all things, past, present, and future, while His omnipotence affirms that He possesses supreme, unlimited power.

Alongside the incommunicable attributes stand the communicable attributes. These are the traits that God shares analogously with His human creatures, because He made us in His image. When we speak of God's love, justice, mercy, and wisdom, we are using communicable attributes. Of course, there is a catch. When God is loving or just, He possesses these qualities infinitely and essentially. When we are loving or just, we reflect them imperfectly as finite creatures. A single drop of water shares the chemical properties of the ocean, yet it lacks the ocean's vastness. In the same way, human goodness is a tiny splash reflecting the infinite ocean of divine goodness.

Within this framework, classical theology highlights three pillars of God's character: holiness, love, and sovereignty. Holiness is perhaps the most fundamental description of God's otherness. In scripture, to be holy is to be set apart, distinct from all creation, and entirely free from moral evil. When the prophet Isaiah encounters the divine presence in chapter six, the seraphim cry out, in the King James Version, "Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory." This signals a purity and majesty so intense that it humbles everything created. God's holiness means He is the absolute standard of righteousness, uncompromised by the corruption of the world.

Yet this same holy God is defined by love. In Christian theology, love is not merely a mood or an impulse; it is the very essence of the divine nature. The apostle John writes in his first letter, chapter four, verse eight, in the King James Version, that "God is love." This love is self-giving and relational. God does not need creation to be complete, yet He freely chooses to pour out His love in creation and redemption.

Tying holiness and love together is the doctrine of sovereignty. Sovereignty means that God rules over the cosmos with supreme authority and freedom. Nothing happens outside of His ultimate care or permission. Throughout church history, theologians have wrestled with how this absolute sovereignty interacts with human responsibility. Reformed theologians emphasize God's meticulous decree over all things, while other traditions, such as Arminianism, stress that God sovereignly chooses to limit the exercise of His power to preserve genuine human free will. Despite these internal family debates, all orthodox traditions agree that God is neither a distant clockmaker nor a helpless spectator, but the active, reigning Lord of history.

As we conclude, we are left with a portrait of a God who is both utterly transcendent and intimately near. He is unchangeable yet responsive, infinitely holy yet deeply loving. Eastern Orthodox theology adds a caution here: God's essence remains beyond all knowing, and we know him through his energies, his real presence and action in the world. Grasping these characteristics prepares us to look closer at the inner life of the divine in our next study.

## Key ideas
- Classical theology categorizes God's traits into incommunicable attributes, which belong to Him alone, and communicable attributes, which He shares analogously with humanity.
- The doctrine of divine simplicity affirms that God is not composed of parts, meaning His attributes are identical with His very essence.
- Aseity, omniscience, and omnipotence stand as classic incommunicable attributes describing God's self-existence, total knowledge, and unlimited power.
- Holiness signifies God's absolute moral purity and His transcendent otherness as the ultimate standard of righteousness.
