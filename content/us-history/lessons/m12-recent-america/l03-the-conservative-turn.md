# The Conservative Turn

Imagine a political map of the United States in the late nineteen seventies. After the disillusionment of the Vietnam War and the shock of the Watergate scandal, the nation felt adrift. Economic stagnation, characterized by high inflation and high unemployment—a combination economists called stagflation—had eroded public faith in the government’s ability to manage the country. In our last lesson, we saw how the seventies were defined by a sense of limits and cynicism. Out of this malaise emerged a new, powerful political coalition that sought to redefine the role of government in American life.

This coalition, often called the New Right, was a diverse alliance. It brought together fiscal conservatives who wanted lower taxes and less regulation, social conservatives concerned about changing cultural norms, and religious groups who felt that the nation had strayed from traditional values. A significant force within this movement was the mobilization of women, led by figures such as Phyllis Schlafly. Through the STOP ERA campaign, Schlafly and her supporters successfully organized social conservatives to oppose the Equal Rights Amendment, arguing that it would threaten traditional family structures and women's protections.

The man who became the face of this movement was Ronald Reagan, a former actor and governor of California. During the nineteen eighty presidential election, Reagan tapped into the public’s frustration with the status quo. In a televised debate, he asked voters to consider whether they were better off than they had been four years prior. For many, the answer was no. Reagan promised a renewal of American patriotism, a robust military buildup to challenge the Soviet Union, and a fundamental shift in how the economy functioned. His landslide victory in 1980 signaled the beginning of what historians often call the Reagan Revolution.

The economic reality Reagan inherited was complex. Before his tax cuts took effect, the Federal Reserve, under the leadership of Paul Volcker, had already begun raising interest rates significantly to break the back of inflation. While this move was painful for many Americans, it was a critical factor in stabilizing the economy, a reality that complicates the narrative that tax cuts alone were responsible for the subsequent recovery. Reagan’s own economic philosophy, known as supply-side economics, argued that the best way to stimulate growth was to cut taxes, particularly for corporations and the wealthy. The theory was that these tax breaks would encourage investment and create new jobs.

The Reagan administration also took a firm stance against organized labor. In 1981, when the Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization, or PATCO, went on strike, Reagan fired the striking workers and permanently replaced them. This was a pivotal moment for labor relations, signaling a new era of hostility toward unions and setting a standard for how the federal government would interact with organized labor for years to come.

The debate over these economic and labor policies remains a central point of historical discussion. Proponents highlight the period of economic growth that occurred during the nineteen eighties and argue that deregulation and tax cuts liberated the American spirit of entrepreneurship. Conversely, opponents emphasize that the national debt ballooned during this decade. They argue that the benefits of the economic expansion were not distributed equally, pointing to the decline of manufacturing jobs and the stagnation of real wages for many middle-class families as evidence that the policy failed to lift all boats.

Beyond the economy, the Reagan era was marked by a shift in the federal judiciary and social policy. Reagan appointed conservative judges to federal courts, including the Supreme Court, with the goal of interpreting the Constitution through an originalist lens, which focuses on the intent of the authors at the time of writing. Meanwhile, social conservatives pushed for policies that limited abortion access and promoted prayer in schools. These debates energized the conservative coalition, creating a sharp polarization in American political life that continued to intensify.

The impact of this era was not limited to domestic policy. Reagan’s rhetoric regarding the Soviet Union, which he famously labeled an evil empire, marked a departure from the detente that had characterized the previous decade. He advocated for a massive increase in military spending to pressure the Soviet Union into economic exhaustion. This strategy, along with changing internal dynamics within the Soviet bloc, set the stage for the dramatic geopolitical shifts that would define the end of the century.

By the time Reagan left office in 1989, the political landscape of the United States had been transformed. The conservative movement had successfully shifted the national conversation toward smaller government, lower taxes, and a stronger military presence. However, the nation remained deeply divided over the long-term consequences of these changes.

## Key ideas

- The New Right coalition emerged in the late nineteen seventies, fueled by economic stagnation and the successful mobilization of social conservatives, including the STOP ERA movement led by Phyllis Schlafly.
- Ronald Reagan’s 1980 election victory signaled a shift toward conservative policies, though the economic recovery was significantly aided by Paul Volcker’s interest rate hikes at the Federal Reserve.
- Reagan’s 1981 decision to fire striking air-traffic controllers marked a major shift in federal labor policy and signaled a decline in union influence.
- Supply-side economics remains a subject of intense debate, with critics pointing to rising national debt and economic inequality, while supporters credit the policies for fostering long-term growth.
- The Reagan era transformed the federal judiciary through the appointment of originalist judges and deepened partisan polarization on social and cultural issues.
