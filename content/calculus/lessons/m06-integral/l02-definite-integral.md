# The Definite Integral

Imagine driving a car whose speedometer is broken, but you have a clever passenger who notes down your velocity every ten seconds. At the end of a minute, you have six numbers. You know that distance equals velocity multiplied by time, but your velocity kept changing. How do you find the exact total distance traveled? In our last lesson, we looked at how splitting an area under a curve into thin rectangles gives an approximation of accumulated change, using what we call Riemann sums. Today, we turn that approximation into a precise mathematical tool by taking the limit as those rectangles become infinitely narrow, arriving at the definition of the definite integral.

To see how this works by ear, picture the graph of a continuous function plotted on a coordinate plane, rising and falling above the horizontal axis. Suppose we want to find the net accumulated area bounded by this curve between a starting point on the left and an ending point on the right. We partition that interval into a number of subintervals. Let us call the number of subintervals n. For simplicity, imagine each subinterval has an equal width, which we call delta x. We find delta x by taking the right endpoint, subtracting the left endpoint, and dividing that difference by n.

Within each subinterval, we pick a sample point to determine the height of a rectangle. We might choose the right endpoint, the left endpoint, or the midpoint. Let us denote the chosen sample point in the first subinterval as c sub one, in the second as c sub two, and so on up to c sub n. The area of any single rectangle is the height of the function at that sample point multiplied by the width delta x. If we sum up the areas of all n rectangles from the first to the nth, we get a Riemann sum. This sum approximates the total area under the curve.

Now, what happens if we make our approximation better and better? We can do this by letting the number of subintervals, n, grow larger and larger without bound. As n approaches infinity, the width delta x shrinks toward zero. If the limit of these Riemann sums exists as n grows infinitely large, regardless of how we choose our sample points inside each tiny slice, we call that limiting value the definite integral of the function from the left endpoint to the right endpoint.

We write this in words as the integral from the left endpoint to the right endpoint of f of x, d x. The history of this notation involves one of the most famous and bitter disputes in the history of mathematics. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently developed calculus, yet they engaged in a lifelong quarrel over who deserved priority. Newton introduced a notation using dots placed above variables to denote rates of change, while Leibniz introduced the integral sign we use today, which is a tall, elongated s shape representing a continuous sum, paired with a d notation for differentials like d x to indicate the variable of integration. When this limit exists, we say the function is integrable on that interval. Every function that is continuous on a closed interval is integrable there.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Suppose we want to find the definite integral of the constant function two from an x value of zero to an x value of three. Because the function is a horizontal line at a height of two, the region under the curve is simply a rectangle with a base of three and a height of two. If we set up a Riemann sum with n subintervals, each width delta x is three divided by n. If we evaluate our function at the right endpoint of each subinterval, the height is always two. Multiplying the height two by the width three divided by n gives six divided by n for each rectangle. Adding up n of these identical rectangles gives n multiplied by the quantity six divided by n, which simplifies to six. Taking the limit as n approaches infinity of the constant six leaves us with six. The area is indeed three multiplied by two, which is six.

This limiting process of Riemann sums gives us the exact net area, accounting for any regions where the curve dips below the horizontal axis as negative area. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will discover the stunning shortcut that connects this heavy limit machinery directly to antiderivatives through the fundamental theorem of calculus.

## Key ideas
- The definite integral formalizes the accumulation of change by taking the limit of a Riemann sum as the number of subintervals approaches infinity and their width approaches zero.
- A Riemann sum approximates the net area under a curve by adding the areas of thin rectangles whose heights are determined by sample points within each subinterval.
- Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz introduced the tall, elongated s integral sign and the differential notation, while Isaac Newton used dot notation, and the two inventors engaged in a bitter priority dispute.
- A function is integrable on an interval if the limit of its Riemann sums exists and yields a unique value regardless of how sample points are chosen.
