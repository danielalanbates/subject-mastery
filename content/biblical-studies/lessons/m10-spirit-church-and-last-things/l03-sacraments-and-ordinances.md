# Sacraments and Ordinances in Diverse Traditions

Imagine walking into a quiet cathedral where candles flicker softly, incense rises toward the vaulted ceiling, and believers kneel to receive bread and wine at an altar. Now, imagine walking into a brightly lit auditorium where a modest wooden table sits at the front, and a pastor breaks a single loaf of bread and passes small plates down the row. Both groups are practicing the same foundational Christian rite, yet their understandings of what is actually happening could hardly be more different. In our last lesson, we explored the nature and mission of the church as the body of Christ. Today, we turn to the visible, tangible acts that shape and sustain that body: baptism and the Lord's Supper.

Across Christian history, these sacred practices have been called by different names, reflecting deep theological divides. Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and some Protestant traditions often use the word sacrament, deriving from a Latin term for a military oath. They view these rites as visible signs of an invisible grace, instituted by Christ, through which God actively communicates spiritual life to the believer. Other Protestant traditions, particularly those arising from the Radical Reformation and later evangelical movements, prefer the word ordinance. This term emphasizes that these practices are sacred duties or memorials commanded by Christ, carried out in obedience and faith rather than acting as channels of automatic grace.

Traditions even count them differently: Catholic and Orthodox churches recognize seven sacraments, while most Protestant churches recognize two, baptism and the Lord's Supper, which this lesson focuses on. When it comes to baptism, the differences involve who should receive the water and what the water accomplishes. In the Roman Catholic tradition, baptism is viewed as a necessary sacrament of initiation that washes away original sin, incorporates the person into the body of Christ, and imparts new life. Eastern Orthodox theology similarly views baptism as the gateway to spiritual life and incorporation into the church, framing it as dying and rising with Christ while emphasizing healing from the effects of ancestral sin rather than inherited guilt. Because of this, both traditions practice infant baptism, trusting that God's grace initiates the relationship before the child can articulate personal faith. Mainline Protestants, such as Lutherans, Anglicans, and Presbyterians, also practice infant baptism, though they articulate its meaning slightly differently, often stressing God's covenant promise to families and the community of faith.

On the other side of the spectrum, Baptist and Pentecostal traditions practice believer's baptism, restricting the rite to those who have made a conscious profession of faith. For these groups, baptism by full immersion is a powerful symbolic portrait of dying and rising with Christ. They argue that the New Testament pattern requires personal faith to precede the sign. While these positions appear irreconcilable, all sides agree that baptism marks entry into the Christian community and names the believer with the triune name of God.

The Lord's Supper, also known as Holy Communion, the Eucharist, or the breaking of bread, reveals an even greater variety of theological interpretation. In Catholic theology, the bread and wine undergo transubstantiation during the mass, meaning their underlying substance changes into the actual body and blood of Christ, even though their outward appearances remain unchanged. Eastern Orthodox believers also affirm a profound, mysterious real presence of Christ in the elements, though they typically avoid strict philosophical terms like transubstantiation, preferring to treat it as a holy mystery.

Lutheran and Reformed traditions hold distinct views of the real presence as well. Martin Luther taught that Christ's true body and blood are genuinely present in, with, and under the bread and wine, an understanding that Lutherans distinguish from the term consubstantiation. John Calvin proposed a spiritual presence, teaching that while the elements remain physically unchanged, the Holy Spirit truly feeds the believer upon Christ across the distance of heaven and earth. Conversely, many Baptist and non-denominational churches follow a memorial view, associated with the Swiss reformer Huldrych Zwingli, which sees the bread and wine as sacred symbols that stir remembrance of Christ's sacrificial death. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will examine what happens to the human person at the end of earthly life, exploring death and the intermediate state.

## Key ideas
- Sacraments are understood by Catholic, Orthodox, and mainline Protestant traditions as visible signs that actually convey divine grace.
- Ordinances are viewed by many evangelical and Baptist groups as sacred memorials and acts of obedience commanded by Christ.
- Christian traditions divide over infant baptism as a sign of covenant or grace versus believer's baptism as a response to personal faith.
- Interpretations of the Lord's Supper range from the physical real presence in Catholic and Lutheran theology to the spiritual presence and the memorial view.
