# Arc Length and Physical Applications

Imagine driving along a winding mountain road. The odometer clicks upward, measuring the actual distance traveled along every curve and switchback, not just the straight-line distance from your start to your finish. How do we calculate that exact length mathematically for a curve given by an equation? In our recent work, we used integration to find the area between curves and the volumes of complex solids by slicing them into disks, washers, or cylindrical shells. Today, we extend that exact same philosophy of slicing and summing to measure the physical world, finding the precise length of a curved path, the total work required to stretch a spring or pump liquid, and the mass of a non-uniform rod.

Let us begin with the length of a curve. Suppose you have a smooth curve described by a function on an interval from one endpoint to another. If we zoom in extremely close to any small piece of that curve, the arc looks almost like a straight line. We can approximate the total length of the curve by dividing it into many tiny sub-intervals, drawing a straight line segment across each one, and adding up all their lengths. Each tiny segment forms the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the horizontal change in x and the vertical change in y. By the Pythagorean theorem, the length of that little hypotenuse is the square root of the sum of the square of the change in x plus the square of the change in y.

To turn this geometric approximation into an exact integral, we factor out the squared change in x from inside the square root. What remains inside is one plus the square of the change in y divided by the change in x. As we take the limit where the number of sub-intervals goes to infinity and their widths shrink to zero, that ratio of changes turns into a derivative. The total arc length is given by the definite integral from the starting point to the ending point of the square root of the quantity one plus the square of the derivative of the function, all with respect to x.

Let us calculate a concrete example. Suppose our function is the square root of the cube of x, and we want to find its arc length from x equals zero to x equals four. First, we find the derivative of our function, which is three halves times the square root of x. Next, we square this derivative to get nine quarters times x. We add one to this squared derivative, giving us the quantity one plus nine quarters times x under our square root sign. To evaluate the integral of this square root from zero to four, we use a simple substitution. Let u equal the quantity one plus nine quarters times x, meaning that d u is nine quarters d x. After adjusting our limits and constants, we integrate and find that the exact arc length of this curve is eight twenty-sevenths times the quantity ten times the square root of ten, minus one, which is about nine point zero seven units. As a check, the straight segment from the origin to the endpoint four, eight is about eight point nine four units long, a little shorter than the curve, as it must be.

Beyond geometry, integration lets us solve fundamental physical problems, such as finding the work done by a variable force. In physics, work is defined as force multiplied by distance when the force is constant. But what if the force changes continuously, like a spring stretching further and further from its natural resting position? According to Hooke's law, the force required to hold a spring stretched to a certain distance is proportional to that distance. To find the total work required to stretch a spring from an initial length to a final length, we slice the distance into tiny increments, multiply each tiny displacement by the variable force acting at that point, and integrate. The total work is the definite integral from the initial displacement to the final displacement of the force function with respect to displacement.

Similarly, we can calculate the mass of a one-dimensional object like a metal rod whose density varies from point to point rather than staying uniform. If the linear density is given as a function of position, we slice the rod into tiny pieces of length d x, multiply the density at each point by the length of the slice to get the mass of that tiny piece, and integrate across the entire length of the rod. Both work and mass rely on the exact same intellectual move pioneered by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, whose bitter priority dispute over calculus involved notation we still use today, with Leibniz introducing the integral sign and Newton employing fluxions and dots. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will see how these powerful differential and integral tools allow us to model dynamic processes like population growth and radioactive decay.

## Key ideas
- The arc length of a smooth curve is calculated by integrating the square root of the quantity one plus the square of the derivative of the function over the given interval.
- Geometric approximations using tiny right triangles allow us to turn discrete Pythagorean hypotenuse lengths into a continuous definite integral.
- Work is found by integrating a variable force function with respect to displacement over a specified distance interval.
- The mass of a non-uniform object is determined by integrating a variable density function across its entire spatial domain.
