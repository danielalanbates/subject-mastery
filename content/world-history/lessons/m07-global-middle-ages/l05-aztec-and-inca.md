# The Aztec and Inca Empires

Imagine standing in a bustling market square where merchants sell fresh avocados, sharp obsidian blades, and vibrant textiles dyed with deep reds and purples. Everything around you has arrived by canoe or on the backs of human porters because these societies do not use draft animals or wheeled vehicles. Above this market rises the double stairway of a massive stone pyramid, while fresh water flows into the city through a stone aqueduct that spans a vast lake. This is not a scene from Renaissance Europe or the trade hubs of Eurasia. This is Tenochtitlan, the magnificent capital of the Aztec Empire, thriving in the fifteenth century CE. While European states were recovering from various local crises and exploring new political paths, two massive, highly sophisticated civilizations were engineering sprawling empires across the varied landscapes of the Americas.

To understand these civilizations, we must look past the old colonial myth that the Americas were a quiet wilderness before European contact. In truth, the Americas were home to densely populated, highly organized states. The Aztecs, or the Mexica people, built their empire in the Valley of Mexico starting in the early fourteenth century CE. The traditional date for the founding of their capital, Tenochtitlan, is about 1325 CE on a marshy island in the middle of Lake Texcoco. Rather than seeing the swamp as a drawback, the Mexica turned it into an engineering triumph. They constructed chinampas, which are often called floating gardens. These were rectangular plots of fertile mud built up from the lakebed, secured by willow trees, and watered constantly from below. These raised fields yielded multiple harvests a year, easily feeding hundreds of thousands of urban residents.

Local governance and everyday life in Aztec communities were anchored by the calpulli, a kinship and territorial system that organized citizens into distinct social groups. The calpulli managed local land allocation, organized community labor, and handled basic tax collection for the state. The Aztec Empire as a whole was less a unified nation-state and more a vast tributary network. The Mexica formed a triple alliance in 1428 CE with two neighboring city-states, asserting military dominance over hundreds of smaller altepetl, or local ethnic states. Instead of replacing local rulers, Aztec administrators demanded regular payments of staple goods like corn, cotton, cocoa beans, and colorful bird feathers. They maintained control through a mix of psychological intimidation, military might, and a complex network of causeways and canals that allowed swift troop movements across the lake. Tribute flowed into Tenochtitlan in staggering quantities, fueling an elite class of priests and warriors who maintained a cosmology centered on keeping the sun in motion through ritual sacrifice and devotion.

Far to the south, along the towering spine of the Andes Mountains, the Inca built an even larger territorial empire known as Tawantinsuyu, meaning the four regions together. Beginning their rapid expansion in the mid-fifteenth century CE under leaders like Pachacuti, the Inca conquered diverse ecological zones, from arid coastal deserts to freezing mountain peaks and humid Amazonian foothills. To govern this vertical landscape, the Inca relied on a brilliant administrative system that did not use a written alphabet. Instead, they recorded census data, crop yields, and tribute accounts using the khipu, a clever device of knotted strings of various colors and lengths. Trained administrators, known as khipukamayuq, could read and transmit complex quantitative information across thousands of miles of rugged terrain.

Some historians argue that Inca expansion was driven by a custom known as split inheritance, in which a dead ruler kept ownership of his personal wealth, lands, and palaces to support his royal mummy cult. This meant every new emperor had to conquer his own new lands and build his own power base from scratch, which drove rapid imperial expansion. The state also maintained massive stone storehouses, called qollqas, scattered across the empire. These storehouses held vast reserves of food, textiles, and weapons, allowing the Inca to sustain a redistributive economy and supply mandatory laborers.

The Inca agricultural and infrastructural achievements were equally remarkable. They carved vast agricultural terraces into steep mountain slopes, trapping heat in the stone walls and preventing erosion while growing hundreds of varieties of potatoes and maize. To link their mountainous empire, they constructed an extensive road system featuring stone-paved highways, woven-grass suspension bridges, and relay stations where runners called chasquis sprinted messages across the empire in days. Labor was organized through the mita system, a mandatory public service model where households contributed labor for state projects like roads, mines, and fortresses in exchange for imperial protection and food security during famines.

Both the Aztec and Inca empires demonstrated breathtaking engineering, sophisticated bureaucracy, and deep mastery of their unique environments. Their administrative prowess allowed them to mobilize vast human resources and reshape their worlds long before the first sails of European maritime explorers appeared on the horizon.

## Key ideas
- The Aztec Empire, centered on the island capital of Tenochtitlan, built a vast tributary network supported by ingenious chinampa agriculture and local calpulli governance systems.
- The Inca Empire mastered the challenging Andean landscape by constructing extensive stone roads, suspension bridges, and steep agricultural terraces across multiple ecological zones.
- Inca statecraft relied on imperial storehouses called qollqas for economic redistribution, split inheritance to drive continuous expansion, and knotted-string khipu devices for administrative record-keeping.
- State-managed labor systems, such as the Incan mita, allowed these American empires to mobilize massive workforces for monumental architecture and infrastructure without the use of draft animals or wheeled vehicles.
