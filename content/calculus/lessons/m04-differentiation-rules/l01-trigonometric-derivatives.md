# Trigonometric Derivatives

Imagine watching a child on a playground swing, soaring back and forth in a smooth, rhythmic arc. If you wanted to track not just where the swing is at any moment, but how fast its speed changes and in which direction, you would need more than simple straight-line motion. You would need the mathematics of waves and cycles. In our last lesson, we explored velocity and acceleration to understand motion along a straight path using power and product rules. But much of the physical world moves in repeating patterns, governed by angles and periodic curves. To find the rates of change for these circular and wave-like phenomena, we must learn how to differentiate trigonometric functions.

The discovery of calculus in the late seventeenth century famously gave rise to a bitter priority dispute between Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. Newton developed his version of calculus, which he called the method of fluxions, using dot notation to represent rates of change. Leibniz developed his own independent calculus using differential notation, introducing symbols like d x that we still use today. While their personal rivalry grew fierce over who came first, both men provided the foundation we use to find slopes of curves.

Before we jump into the rules, let us remember why the derivative represents an instantaneous rate of change, much like the speedometer reading in a car. When we look at a smooth curve representing a wave, the derivative at any point gives us the slope of the tangent line at that exact location. For the fundamental wave of the sine function, which starts at zero and climbs smoothly upward, the steepness of the curve changes constantly. At the very peak of the wave, the curve flattens out completely for a brief moment, meaning the tangent line is horizontal and its slope is zero. Just before that peak, the curve was rising, and just after, it falls.

Let us state the core rule for our first trigonometric function. The derivative of the sine of x with respect to x is the cosine of x. Think about how this matches our mental picture of the wave. Where the sine curve starts at zero and rises steeply, its slope is positive one, and indeed the cosine of zero is one. When the sine curve reaches its maximum height at a value of pi divided by two, the curve is momentarily flat, so its slope is zero, and the cosine of pi divided by two is indeed zero. The sine wave and the cosine wave are intimately connected, each acting as the derivative of the other with a few sign adjustments.

Moving on to the cosine function itself, its derivative introduces a negative sign. The derivative of the cosine of x with respect to x is negative the sine of x. This makes intuitive sense if you visualize a cosine wave, which starts at its highest point when x is zero. From that peak, the cosine curve immediately turns downward and heads into a valley, meaning its initial slope is pointing downward into negative territory. The negative sign in the derivative captures that downward plunge.

With the rules for sine and cosine established, we can build the rules for other trigonometric expressions by treating them as quotients. Recall from our earlier work that the tangent of x is defined as the sine of x divided by the cosine of x. By applying the quotient rule, which handles fractions of functions, we can find how tangent changes. The derivative of the tangent of x with respect to x turns out to be the square of the secant of x, written as the quantity secant of x, all squared. Similarly, because the secant of x is defined as one divided by the cosine of x, another application of our quotient rules reveals its rate of change. The derivative of the secant of x with respect to x is the product of the secant of x and the tangent of x.

Let us walk through a quick example to see these rules in action. Suppose we want to find the derivative of a function made by adding the sine of x to the product of two and the cosine of x. Using the sum and constant multiple rules alongside our new trigonometric derivatives, we handle each term separately. The derivative of the sine of x is the cosine of x. For the second term, the constant two remains, and the derivative of the cosine of x is negative the sine of x. Putting those pieces together, our resulting derivative is the cosine of x minus two times the sine of x. This tells us the exact instantaneous rate of change for our combined wave at any input value. As we move forward into our next lesson, we will discover how to handle even more complex combinations of these functions by peeling back layers like an onion using the chain rule.

## Key ideas
- Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently developed calculus, sparking a bitter priority dispute while introducing dot notation and differential notation respectively.
- The derivative of the sine of x is the cosine of x, reflecting how the slope of a sine wave starts positive and flattens at its peak.
- The derivative of the cosine of x is negative the sine of x, accounting for the downward slope of a cosine wave as it leaves its maximum point.
- The derivative of the tangent of x is the square of the secant of x, derived by treating tangent as a quotient of sine and cosine.
- The derivative of the secant of x is the product of the secant of x and the tangent of x, found by applying fraction rules to the reciprocal of cosine.
