# Units and the International System

In September 1999, NASA lost the 125,000,000 dollar Mars Climate Orbiter as it entered the Martian atmosphere. The spacecraft burned up because one engineering team calculated thruster force in pounds, while another team expected the data in newtons. A simple mix-up of units destroyed years of work. In our previous lesson, we saw how physical models help us describe nature. But a physical model is only as good as the numbers we put into it. To share information accurately across the world, scientists rely on a universal language of measurement known as the International System of Units, abbreviated as SI.

The International System rests on seven base units, from which all other scientific measurements are derived. In mechanics, we rely primarily on three: the meter for distance, the kilogram for mass, and the second for time. The remaining four base units are the ampere for electric current, the kelvin for thermodynamic temperature, the mole for amount of substance, and the candela for luminous intensity. Historically, base units were defined by physical artifacts, like a standard platinum-iridium cylinder kept in France for the kilogram. Today, all seven SI base units are defined by fixing the exact numerical values of fundamental physical constants. For example, the meter is defined using the speed of light in a vacuum, and the kilogram is defined using the Planck constant.

Unlike traditional systems that use inconsistent conversion factors like 12 or 5,280, the metric system is built entirely on powers of 10. Prefix names tell you the exact scale of a quantity. Beyond kilo for 1,000, centi for one hundredth, and milli for one thousandth, physics frequently uses larger and smaller prefixes. Mega means one million, and giga means one billion. Micro means one millionth, and nano means one billionth. For instance, a gigawatt is 1,000,000,000 watts, while a nanometer is one billionth of a meter.

When we combine base units, we get derived units. Speed, for instance, is distance divided by time, measured in meters per second. Energy is measured in joules, which breaks down into kilograms times meters squared per second squared. A golden rule of physics is dimensional consistency. You can only add or subtract quantities that have the exact same units. You cannot add 3 meters to 4 seconds, just as you cannot add apples to space shuttles. If an equation attempts to sum two different units, that physical equation is fundamentally wrong.

To convert between units safely, we use conversion factors. A conversion factor is a ratio equal to the number 1. For example, since 1,000 meters equals 1 kilometer, the ratio of 1,000 meters to 1 kilometer equals 1. Because multiplying any quantity by 1 does not change its actual physical magnitude, we can multiply by these fractions to swap unit labels without altering the underlying physical reality. The trick is to arrange the fraction so that the unit you want to eliminate cancels out, leaving only the desired unit behind.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Suppose a car travels on a highway at 72 kilometers per hour, and we want to know its speed in meters per second. First, we convert kilometers to meters. Since there are 1,000 meters in 1 kilometer, we multiply 72 kilometers per hour by the ratio of 1,000 meters to 1 kilometer. The kilometer units cancel out, leaving 72,000 meters per hour. Next, we convert hours to seconds. There are 3,600 seconds in 1 hour. Because hours are currently in the denominator, we place hours in the numerator of our conversion factor, multiplying by 1 hour divided by 3,600 seconds.

Dividing 72,000 by 3,600 yields 20 meters per second. Let us do a quick sense check. A speed of 20 meters per second means covering the length of an Olympic swimming pool, fifty meters, in about two and a half seconds. That feels completely reasonable for a car moving at moderate highway speed. By applying conversion factors step by step, we avoided confusion and arrived at a precise, consistent value ready for calculation.

Unit conversions require extra care when dealing with squared or cubed units, like area or volume. If you want to convert square meters into square centimeters, you cannot simply multiply by 100. Since 1 meter equals 100 centimeters, 1 square meter represents a square that is 100 centimeters wide and 100 centimeters long. Multiplying those dimensions together shows that 1 square meter equals 10,000 square centimeters. Always square or cube the entire conversion factor, including both the number and the unit, when converting multi-dimensional quantities.

Mastering standard units and conversion factors ensures that our physical equations remain rigorous and comparable across the globe. However, every measurement we take in the real world carries a degree of uncertainty. In our next lesson, we will explore how to report measurements accurately using significant figures and how to make quick, sensible estimates about physical quantities.

## Key ideas

- The International System of Units establishes seven base units defined by universal physical constants to maintain consistency across scientific measurement.
- Metric prefixes modify base units by powers of 10, ranging from tiny scales like nano and micro to very large scales like mega and giga.
- Conversion factors are ratios equal to one that allow us to change unit labels without altering the physical quantity being measured.
- Dimensional consistency requires that all terms added or subtracted in a valid physical equation must have identical units.
