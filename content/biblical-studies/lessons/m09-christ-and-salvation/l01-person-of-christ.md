# The Person of Christ

Imagine holding a simple glass of water to the light. You can see right through it, and yet if you try to separate its wetness from its chemistry, you find an inseparable reality. When Christians throughout the centuries have looked at Jesus of Nazareth, they have wrestled with a vastly deeper mystery. In our last lesson, we examined how human beings fell into sin and fractured their relationship with God. Today, we turn to the answer to that fracture: the person of Jesus Christ. Specifically, we are exploring the doctrine of the incarnation, which teaches that the eternal Son of God took on human flesh to dwell among us.

The word incarnation comes from Latin roots meaning into flesh. It is the core confession of Christian faith that Jesus is not merely a wise teacher, a moral prophet, or a spiritual master, but God incarnate. The New Testament writers labor to express this reality using striking language. The Gospel of John opens, in the King James Version of John chapter one, verse one: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” A few verses later, in verse fourteen, it says: “And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us.” This is not a metaphor for divine inspiration. It is an assertion that the infinite, uncreated Creator entered human history as a vulnerable infant in Bethlehem.

Scriptural evidence for this dual identity, both fully divine and fully human, runs through the entire New Testament. Jesus exhibits divine prerogatives: he forgives sins, claims authority over the Sabbath, receives worship, and shares a unique oneness with the Father. Yet the Gospels are equally insistent on His authentic humanity. Jesus grows tired, experiences hunger, weeps in grief, and faces genuine temptation. He bleeds, suffers, and dies on a Roman cross. To lose either side of this equation is to lose the Christian message of redemption. If He is not fully God, His death has no cosmic power to save. If He is not fully human, He cannot truly represent humanity in our rebellion and need.

As the church grew, early theologians faced the immense challenge of explaining how divinity and humanity could coexist in one person without mixing, changing, dividing, or separating. This struggle culminated at the Council of Chalcedon in 451. The Chalcedonian Definition remains the standard baseline for historic Christian orthodoxy across Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Protestant traditions. It confesses that Jesus Christ is one single person subsisting in two distinct natures, the divine and the human, without confusion and without change.

To picture this, we must avoid ancient heresies that continuously threaten to distort our understanding. Some early thinkers, like Apollinaris, argued that the divine Word replaced the human mind of Jesus, making Him less than fully human. Others, like Nestorius, were accused of splitting Christ into two separate persons, one divine and one human sharing a moral bond. The church rejected both extremes. Chalcedon insisted that the divine nature did not diminish the human nature, nor did the human nature swallow up the divine. Jesus acts and speaks as a single, unified subject, yet He does so with all the attributes proper to both God and man.

This doctrine has profound implications for how we understand God. Instead of a distant deity sitting in remote isolation, the incarnation reveals a God who plunges directly into human suffering. It tells us that our physical bodies, our daily labor, and our historical existence matter deeply to the Creator. In Jesus, God takes our very nature upon Himself to redeem it from the inside out.

As we move forward, we must ask how this remarkable person accomplishes our rescue. Next, we will explore the various models of the atonement, examining how the life, death, and resurrection of the incarnate Son bridge the chasm between a holy God and fallen humanity.

## Key ideas
- The incarnation teaches that the eternal Son of God took on real human flesh, becoming fully human while remaining fully divine.
- Biblical texts such as the Gospel of John present Jesus as the divine Word who entered history to dwell among humanity.
- The Chalcedonian Definition established that Jesus is one person with two distinct natures, neither confused nor separated.
- Historic Christian orthodoxy insists that Jesus must be both fully God and fully human to accomplish human redemption.
