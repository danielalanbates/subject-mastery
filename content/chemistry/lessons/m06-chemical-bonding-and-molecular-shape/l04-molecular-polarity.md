# Molecular Polarity

Imagine you are holding a small, clear glass of water. If you were to bring a charged plastic comb near a thin, steady stream of water flowing from a faucet, you would see the water bend toward the comb. This happens because water is a polar molecule. It has two electrically different ends, with one end holding a partial negative charge and the other holding a partial positive charge. To understand why this happens, we must look at both the nature of the chemical bonds inside a molecule and the physical shape that those atoms take in space.

In our previous lesson, we used the valence shell electron pair repulsion theory to determine the three-dimensional shapes of molecules. We saw that electrons arrange themselves to stay as far apart as possible. Now, we must combine that knowledge with the concept of bond polarity. A bond is polar when two atoms share electrons unequally because one atom is more electronegative than the other.

Electronegativity is a measure of how strongly an atom pulls shared electrons toward itself. This property follows clear patterns on the periodic table. Generally, electronegativity increases as you move from left to right across a period and decreases as you move down a group. These trends are driven by the effective nuclear charge and the distance of the valence electrons from the nucleus. When atoms with different electronegativities bond, the electron cloud shifts toward the more greedy atom, creating a dipole. A dipole is a separation of charge within a bond.

However, having polar bonds does not automatically make a molecule polar. Think of a game of tug of war. If two people pull on a rope with equal strength in exactly opposite directions, the rope does not move. The net force is zero. The same principle applies to molecules. We must look at the overall symmetry of the structure. If the polar bonds are arranged in a perfectly symmetrical way, they can cancel each other out. If the molecule is asymmetrical, the dipoles do not cancel, and the entire molecule becomes polar.

Let us look at carbon dioxide, which has a formula of one carbon and two oxygen atoms, as our first example. The carbon atom sits in the center with an oxygen atom on either side. This creates a linear shape. Oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so each bond is polar, with the electron cloud shifting toward the oxygen atoms. Because the molecule is linear, these two bond dipoles point in exactly opposite directions. They pull with equal strength away from the center. Like our tug of war, the effects cancel out perfectly. Therefore, even though carbon dioxide has two polar bonds, the molecule as a whole is nonpolar.

Now, consider water, which has a formula of two hydrogen and one oxygen atoms. Like carbon dioxide, it contains polar bonds because oxygen pulls electrons away from the hydrogen atoms. However, water is not linear. Thanks to the two lone pairs of electrons on the oxygen atom, the shape of water is bent. Because the molecule is bent, the two bond dipoles do not point in opposite directions. Instead, they point at an angle. They reinforce each other rather than canceling out. This makes the oxygen side of the water molecule partially negative and the hydrogen side partially positive. Water is a classic polar molecule, which is exactly why its stream is pulled toward your charged plastic comb.

Ammonia, with a formula of one nitrogen and three hydrogen atoms, is our final example. The nitrogen atom is at the top, and three hydrogen atoms form a pyramid base beneath it. Nitrogen is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the electron density shifts toward the nitrogen atom. Because of the lone pair on the nitrogen, the molecule is not flat. It has a trigonal pyramidal shape. The three bond dipoles all point toward the nitrogen atom. They do not cancel out because they are all angled upward. This makes ammonia a polar molecule, with the nitrogen end carrying a partial negative charge.

Determining polarity is a matter of checking two things. First, identify if the bonds themselves are polar based on the electronegativity trends of the atoms involved. Second, examine the molecular geometry to see if those dipoles cancel out due to symmetry. If the molecule is symmetrical and the outer atoms are identical, the dipoles will likely cancel, leaving you with a nonpolar molecule. If the molecule is asymmetrical, or if the outer atoms are different, the molecule will likely be polar. Understanding this balance is essential for predicting how substances interact, dissolve, and change state.

## Key ideas

- Bond polarity arises from differences in electronegativity, which increases across a period and decreases down a group on the periodic table.
- A molecule is polar if it contains polar bonds arranged in an asymmetrical shape such that the individual bond dipoles do not cancel out.
- Symmetrical molecules with identical outer atoms, such as carbon dioxide, are typically nonpolar because their bond dipoles pull in opposite directions and cancel each other.
- Molecules with bent or pyramidal shapes, such as water and ammonia, are polar because their geometry prevents the bond dipoles from canceling out.
- Molecular polarity is a critical factor in determining physical properties like solubility and the strength of intermolecular forces.
