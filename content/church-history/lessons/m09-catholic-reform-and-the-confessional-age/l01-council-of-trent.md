# The Council of Trent

Imagine you are standing in a grand, drafty cathedral in the Italian city of Trent, nestled in the jagged peaks of the Alps. The year is 1545. For nearly three decades, the Christian West has been splintered. Martin Luther’s challenges in Germany and the subsequent movements in Switzerland, England, and Scotland have fundamentally altered the religious landscape. The Roman Catholic Church is now facing a pivotal moment of reckoning. For eighteen years, off and on, bishops and theologians gathered here to determine the future of their faith. This was the Council of Trent, the engine of what we now call the Catholic Reformation.

When historians discuss this era, they often use two different labels: the Catholic Reformation and the Counter-Reformation. These are not mutually exclusive, but they highlight different motivations. The Catholic Reformation refers to the internal desire to purge corruption, improve education, and deepen piety—a process that actually began before Luther appeared. The Counter-Reformation, by contrast, describes the specific institutional response to Protestantism, focusing on defining Catholic doctrine in direct opposition to the new teachings. Trent was the crucible where these two impulses merged into a single, decisive program.

The council’s first order of business was to address the authority of the Word of God. Protestants had championed the idea of Scripture alone as the final authority for faith. In response, the Council of Trent affirmed that the Bible is indeed the inspired Word of God. However, they also insisted that sacred tradition—the body of teaching handed down from the apostles and interpreted by the Church—carries equal authority. They also confirmed the inclusion of the deuterocanonical books in the Old Testament, which many Protestant reformers had moved to the margins or removed entirely.

On the central question of how a person is saved, the council had to navigate the deep divide opened by the Reformation. Luther had argued that justification is by faith alone, apart from human works. The Council of Trent took a more complex approach. They taught that while grace is a free gift from God that no one can earn, human beings must cooperate with that grace. Justification, they argued, is not merely a legal declaration of righteousness, but a process of inner renewal. Faith is the beginning, but it must be accompanied by hope and charity, and it is expressed through the life of the Church and its sacraments. The council also reaffirmed the traditional doctrine of original sin, teaching that it is transmitted to all human beings and that baptism is the necessary instrument for its remission and for the beginning of the life of grace.

The council firmly upheld the seven sacraments of the Church. They taught that there are seven distinct rites—baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, penance, anointing of the sick, holy orders, and matrimony—and clarified that these are not just symbols. They are effective signs that actually confer the grace they signify. They specifically defended the doctrine of transubstantiation, the belief that the bread and wine truly become the body and blood of Christ. Furthermore, they affirmed that the Mass is a true propitiatory sacrifice, meaning it is an offering made to God to satisfy for sins, rather than a mere memorial service.

Beyond doctrine, the council recognized that the Church’s moral authority had been weakened by the failures of its leaders. Many clergy were poorly trained, absent from their parishes, or living in ways that scandalized the faithful. To fix this, Trent mandated the creation of seminaries in every diocese to ensure that priests were educated, disciplined, and capable of teaching the people. Bishops were commanded to reside in their own dioceses, ending the practice of collecting income from multiple offices while ignoring the spiritual needs of the flock.

The Council of Trent did not end the divisions in Europe. Instead, it drew firm lines in the sand, creating a more standardized, disciplined, and doctrinally clear version of Roman Catholicism that would endure for centuries. It provided the institutional backbone for a Church that was now ready to look outward, not just to fix its own house, but to spread its message across the globe. As the dust settled in the Alps, the Church turned its eyes toward a new generation of spiritual leaders and missionaries who would carry this reformed vision to the corners of the earth.

## Key ideas

- The Council of Trent functioned as both a movement for internal moral renewal and a structured institutional response against Protestant theology.
- The council affirmed that both Scripture and apostolic tradition are essential, equal sources of divine authority for the Church.
- In response to Protestant emphasis on faith alone, the council taught that justification is a process of interior renewal involving the cooperation of human will with divine grace.
- The council reaffirmed the doctrine of original sin and its necessity for baptism, while also defining the Mass as a true propitiatory sacrifice.
- Trent revitalized the Church’s mission by mandating the establishment of formal seminaries to improve the education and moral discipline of the priesthood.
