# Biblical Foundations of Ethics

Imagine you are standing at a busy intersection in a foreign city. You have no map, no GPS, and the street signs are written in a language you cannot read. Every direction looks equally plausible, yet you have a pressing destination to reach. How do you decide which way to turn? This is the fundamental challenge of ethics. It is the study of how we determine what is right, what is wrong, and how we ought to live. In our previous lesson, we examined the historical evidence for the resurrection of Jesus as a cornerstone of the Christian faith. Now, we must ask: if that historical claim is true, how does it reshape the way we navigate the moral landscape of our lives?

For the Christian, the answer to that question is not found in a subjective feeling or a shifting cultural consensus. Instead, it is anchored in the character of God as revealed through Scripture. Biblical ethics does not begin with a list of abstract rules, but with the identity of the Lawgiver. Because God is holy, just, and loving, the moral order is not arbitrary. It is a reflection of who God is. When we speak of biblical foundations, we are pointing to the belief that the Bible provides a cohesive framework for morality. It tells us that human beings are created in the image of God, which gives every person inherent dignity and worth.

This framework operates through what theologians often call the moral law. Throughout the Old Testament, we see this expressed in the Ten Commandments and the broader call to holiness. Jesus then elevates this in the New Testament by focusing on the heart. In the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-two, Jesus summarizes the entire law in two commandments. In the King James Version he says: “Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind,” and, “Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.” He adds that on these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets. This shift from mere external compliance to internal transformation is essential. It moves ethics from a cold legal checklist to a living relationship with the Creator.

Different traditions within the church emphasize different aspects of this foundation. Reformed theology often highlights the use of the law as a guide for the believer, helping us to understand the holiness of God and our own need for grace. Catholic tradition emphasizes the concept of natural law, which suggests that because God created the world, the moral order is woven into the very fabric of human reason and nature, accessible to all people. Meanwhile, many in the Wesleyan and Pentecostal traditions emphasize the role of the Holy Spirit in guiding the believer toward sanctification, enabling us to discern how to act in ways that reflect the fruit of the Spirit in our daily lives.

Despite these nuances, there is a broad, historic consensus. All these traditions treat Scripture as authoritative for moral life, though Catholic and Orthodox Christians read it within the church's living tradition. Consider the example of honesty. Some secular approaches justify honesty because it is socially useful; others, like that of the philosopher Immanuel Kant, because lying could never be a universal rule. A biblical approach, however, grounds honesty in the nature of God, who is Truth. We do not lie, not just because it creates chaos, but because it violates the character of the God in whose image we are made. The Bible provides the "why" behind the "what" of our actions.

Applying this framework requires discernment. We are not just following a static rulebook; we are participating in the story of redemption. This is why we read the Bible as a whole. We look at the trajectory of scripture, from the creation of humanity to the life of Jesus, and finally to the new creation. This overarching narrative gives us a sense of direction. It teaches us that our moral choices have eternal significance because they are part of our response to God’s grace.

As we move forward, we will see that this biblical foundation serves as the bedrock for more specific ethical theories. Understanding these foundations prepares us to explore how these principles translate into our personal habits and character. In our next lesson, we will move from these broad foundations to discuss the specific concept of virtue ethics and how it shapes the development of a godly Christian life.

## Key ideas

- Biblical ethics is rooted in the character of God rather than human opinion or shifting cultural trends.
- The command to love God and love one's neighbor serves as the summary of the moral requirements found throughout Scripture.
- Human dignity is derived from the doctrine that all people are created in the image of God, which dictates how we must treat one another.
- While different Christian traditions emphasize varying roles for the law, reason, and the Holy Spirit, they all treat Scripture as authoritative for moral guidance.
