# Kinetic-Molecular Theory and Real Gases

Imagine you are standing in a crowded room where everyone is moving in random directions, constantly bumping into each other and the walls. This chaotic scene is exactly what happens inside a balloon filled with helium. While the gas laws we studied previously allow us to calculate changes in pressure, volume, and temperature, they do not explain why gases behave this way on a microscopic level. To understand the "why," we look to the kinetic-molecular theory. This model suggests that gases consist of tiny particles in constant, rapid, and random motion. These particles are so small compared to the distance between them that we consider their individual volume to be essentially zero. Furthermore, we assume that these particles do not attract or repel each other, and that their collisions are perfectly elastic, meaning no kinetic energy is lost when they strike the walls of a container.

Pressure, in this view, is simply the result of these countless collisions against the container walls. When you increase the temperature of a gas, you are increasing the average kinetic energy of its particles. They move faster and strike the walls more frequently and with greater force, which is why pressure rises as temperature goes up. This movement also explains how gases spread out. Diffusion is the process by which gas molecules mix to fill a space, while effusion is the specific process where gas molecules escape through a tiny opening into a vacuum.

Thomas Graham, a nineteenth-century chemist, observed that lighter gases escape faster than heavier ones. Graham’s law states that the rate of effusion of a gas is inversely proportional to the square root of its molar mass. Think of it like a race. If you have a light molecule like hydrogen gas, with a molar mass of about two grams per mole, and a heavy molecule like sulfur hexafluoride, with a molar mass of about one hundred forty-six grams per mole, the hydrogen will zip through a pinhole much faster. To find the ratio of their rates, you take the square root of the molar mass of the heavy gas divided by the molar mass of the light gas. If we divide one hundred forty-six by two, we get seventy-three. The square root of seventy-three is approximately eight point five. This means hydrogen gas effuses about eight point five times faster than sulfur hexafluoride under the same conditions.

However, the kinetic-molecular theory is an idealization. Real gases do not always follow these rules perfectly. The theory assumes that gas particles have no volume and no intermolecular attractions, but in the real world, molecules do take up space, and they do exert forces on one another. These departures from ideal behavior become significant under two specific conditions: high pressure and low temperature.

At very high pressures, gas molecules are squeezed so close together that the volume of the particles themselves becomes a significant portion of the total volume of the gas. The space they occupy is no longer negligible. At low temperatures, the particles move much more slowly. Because they are moving slowly, the weak attractive forces between them—which we usually ignore—start to matter, since slow molecules stay near each other long enough to be pulled together. These attractions cause the gas to hit the container walls with less force than the ideal gas law would predict, resulting in a lower pressure than expected.

Scientists account for these deviations using the van der Waals equation, which adds correction factors to the ideal gas law. One factor adjusts for the volume occupied by the particles, while the other adjusts for the attractive forces between them. By recognizing these limitations, we gain a much deeper understanding of how gases transition into states where molecules are packed closely together. Understanding these attractive forces is the perfect bridge to our next topic, where we will explore how these same forces hold molecules together in the liquid phase.

## Key ideas

- The kinetic-molecular theory explains gas pressure as the sum of collisions between particles and container walls, with temperature representing the average kinetic energy of those particles.
- Graham's law demonstrates that gas effusion rates are inversely proportional to the square root of the molar mass, meaning lighter gases travel through small openings faster than heavier ones.
- Real gases deviate from ideal behavior because individual molecules occupy a finite volume and exert attractive forces on one another.
- These deviations are most pronounced at high pressures, where particle volume becomes significant, and at low temperatures, where attractive forces cause molecules to clump together.
