# Solving Separable Equations

In our previous lesson, we learned how to visualize families of solutions using slope fields and how to step forward along tangent lines with Euler’s method. Those tools provide indispensable insight, but they give us approximations rather than exact formulas. If you want to know the precise concentration of a chemical in a reaction over time, an approximation might leave you unsatisfied. Fortunately, for an important class of first order differential equations, we do not have to guess or approximate. We can find the exact formula through an algebraic technique known as separation of variables.

A first order differential equation is called separable if we can rewrite it so that every expression involving the dependent variable y sits on one side of the equation, and every expression involving the independent variable x sits on the other side. In other words, the derivative d y by d x must be expressible as a product of a function of x alone and a function of y alone.

To solve such an equation, we rely heavily on the notation for derivatives introduced by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz and the earlier fluxions notation introduced by Isaac Newton. Newton and Leibniz engaged in a bitter priority dispute over who founded calculus, but mathematicians across the globe ultimately adopted Leibniz’s notation because of how naturally it handles changes of variables. Leibniz wrote the derivative as the ratio of two differentials: d y over d x. In separation of variables, we treat that ratio as if it were a genuine fraction. We multiply both sides by d x and divide or multiply by functions of y, cleanly segregating the variables.

Treating d y and d x as independent quantities might feel like a questionable algebraic trick. However, the procedure is fully justified by the chain rule and integration by substitution. If we write a differential equation in the form g of y times d y by d x equals h of x, integrating both sides with respect to x gives the integral of g of y times d y by d x, d x, on the left, and the integral of h of x, d x, on the right. By the substitution rule for integrals, the left side is equivalent to the integral of g of y, d y. Moving the differential d x across the equals sign is simply an efficient shorthand for that exact substitution step.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Suppose we want to solve the differential equation stating that d y by d x equals two x times y, where y is strictly positive. That assumption makes it safe to divide by y; the constant function y equals zero also solves the equation, an equilibrium solution that dividing by y would lose. Our first task is to separate the variables. We divide both sides by y and multiply both sides by d x. This leaves one over y, d y, on the left side, and two x, d x, on the right side. Every trace of y is gathered on the left, and every trace of x is gathered on the right.

Next, we integrate both sides. On the left, the antiderivative of one over y with respect to y is the natural logarithm of the absolute value of y. For our currently positive range of y, this simplifies to the natural logarithm of y. On the right, the antiderivative of two x with respect to x is x squared. Each side produces an arbitrary constant of integration, but we can combine them into a single constant, which we will call c, placed on the right side. This gives us the equation: the natural logarithm of y equals x squared plus c.

To solve for y explicitly, we exponentiate both sides using the base e. The left side becomes y. The right side becomes e raised to the power of the quantity x squared plus c. Using the laws of exponents, e raised to the sum x squared plus c can be rewritten as e to the power c times e to the power x squared. Because c is an arbitrary real constant, e to the power c is an arbitrary positive constant. Let us rename that positive constant capital k. Our general solution is therefore y equals capital k times e raised to the power of x squared.

Sometimes, after integrating both sides of a separable equation, the resulting algebraic relationship cannot be solved explicitly for y in terms of elementary functions. In such cases, we obtain an implicit solution, which relates x and y through an equation where y is not isolated on one side. Even without an explicit formula, an implicit solution defines y as a function of x implicitly and allows us to study the behavior of the system.

We can easily verify our explicit result by differentiating our candidate function. The derivative of y with respect to x, by the chain rule, is capital k times e to the power of x squared, multiplied by the derivative of the exponent, which is two x. Since capital k times e to the power of x squared is simply y itself, the derivative simplifies to two x times y. This matches our original differential equation word for word. If we are handed an initial condition, such as y of zero equals three, we substitute zero for x and three for y. Because e to the power zero is one, capital k must equal three, giving the unique particular solution: y equals three times e raised to the power of x squared.

## Key ideas

- A first order differential equation is separable if it can be factored into a function of x alone multiplied by a function of y alone.
- Separation of variables isolates all terms involving y with the differential d y on one side, and all terms involving x with the differential d x on the other side.
- Integrating both sides transforms the differential equation into an algebraic equation that yields explicit solutions or, when algebra prevents isolation, valid implicit solutions for y.
- A single arbitrary constant of integration on one side captures the full family of general solutions.
- Dividing by expressions of y during separation requires checking whether setting those expressions to zero yields lost equilibrium solutions.
