# Hydrocarbons

If you have ever flicked a lighter, turned on a gas stove, or filled your car at the pump, you have interacted with the backbone of modern chemistry. Everything from the fuel in your engine to the plastic in your phone case relies on a vast, diverse class of molecules called hydrocarbons. These compounds consist entirely of carbon and hydrogen atoms, held together by covalent bonds. While our previous lessons focused on the energy of electrons in cells and batteries, organic chemistry shifts our attention to the incredible structural versatility of carbon. Carbon is unique because it can form stable, long chains and rings, acting as the structural scaffolding for almost all the molecules of life.

The simplest family of hydrocarbons is the alkanes. You can think of alkanes as saturated compounds, meaning every carbon atom is bonded to the maximum possible number of hydrogen atoms. In an alkane, every carbon atom uses single bonds to connect to its neighbors. Because these bonds are single, the atoms can rotate freely around them, giving the molecules a flexible, chain-like structure. Beyond these open chains, we also find cycloalkanes, where the carbon atoms connect to form a closed ring. These are also saturated, as they contain only single bonds.

When we name these, we use a system based on the number of carbon atoms. A chain of one carbon is methane, two is ethane, three is propane, and four is butane. As the chain grows, we add the suffix ane. For longer chains, we use International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry rules to identify the longest continuous carbon chain. If a branch exists, we number the chain starting from the end that gives the branch the lowest possible number.

When we move to alkenes and alkynes, we encounter unsaturation. An alkene contains at least one carbon to carbon double bond. Because of this double bond, the carbon atoms involved cannot rotate, which locks the molecule into a specific shape. This restriction leads to geometric isomerism. In a cis isomer, the two groups attached to the double-bonded carbons are on the same side of the molecule. In a trans isomer, they are on opposite sides. We name these by changing the suffix to ene and using a number to indicate the location of the double bond. For instance, but-one-ene has the double bond starting at the first carbon, while but-two-ene has it starting at the second. Alkynes go a step further, containing a carbon to carbon triple bond, and they carry the suffix yne.

These molecules show clear trends in physical properties. As the molecular weight of a hydrocarbon increases, the boiling point rises because the molecules have more surface area to interact with one another. However, branching lowers the boiling point. A straight chain like pentane has a higher boiling point than its branched isomer, isopentane, because the straight chain can pack more tightly, allowing for stronger intermolecular attractions.

Beyond these chains, we find the aromatic compounds. The most famous example is benzene, which consists of six carbon atoms arranged in a flat, hexagonal ring. The electrons in benzene are delocalized across the entire ring structure, which gives these molecules a special kind of stability. You might recognize the scent of aromatic compounds in spices like cinnamon or vanilla. They are called aromatic because of this unique ring-based electronic arrangement that behaves very differently from simple chains.

A fascinating feature of these molecules is the existence of structural isomers. Imagine you have four carbon atoms and ten hydrogen atoms. You could string them together in a straight line to make butane. However, you could also arrange those same atoms so that three carbons form a main chain with the fourth carbon branching off the side. This is isobutane. Even though both molecules have the same formula, four carbons and ten hydrogens, their different shapes give them different physical properties. Isomers are simply molecules that share the same chemical formula but have different arrangements of atoms. The more carbon atoms you add to a chain, the more ways you can branch them, leading to an almost infinite variety of possible structures. Mastering this nomenclature allows chemists to communicate the exact structure of a molecule just by speaking its name.

As we move forward, we will see that these hydrocarbons are rarely found in nature by themselves. They are often modified by the addition of oxygen, nitrogen, or other elements. By introducing these new atoms, we create functional groups, which act like chemical handles that give molecules specific personalities and reactive behaviors. In our next lesson, we will explore these oxygen-containing functional groups, which transform simple hydrocarbons into the alcohols, ethers, and acids that define so much of our biological and industrial world.

## Key ideas

- Hydrocarbons are organic molecules composed entirely of carbon and hydrogen, serving as the fundamental building blocks for complex chemistry.
- Alkanes and cycloalkanes contain only single bonds and are considered saturated, while alkenes and alkynes contain double or triple bonds and are considered unsaturated.
- Geometric isomerism, specifically cis and trans configurations, arises in alkenes because the double bond prevents free rotation of the carbon atoms.
- Physical properties like boiling points are influenced by molecular weight, which increases the boiling point, and branching, which decreases it.
- IUPAC nomenclature provides a systematic way to name hydrocarbons by identifying the longest chain, numbering it to locate bonds and branches, and using specific suffixes.
