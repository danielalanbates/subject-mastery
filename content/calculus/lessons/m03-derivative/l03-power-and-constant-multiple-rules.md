# Power and Constant Multiple Rules

Imagine standing in a physics laboratory, tracking a heavy steel ball dropped from a high balcony. In our last lesson, we learned how to find the velocity of that ball at any exact fraction of a second by taking the limit of the average velocity over shrinking time intervals. But calculating that limit using the formal definition of the derivative for every single step of an experiment would take a lifetime of algebraic grinding. Fortunately, mathematicians discovered shortcuts that let us bypass the limit definition entirely for common classes of functions. Today, we unlock the most powerful of these shortcuts for polynomials, transforming what used to be a tedious algebraic marathon into a straightforward calculation.

Let us look at the simplest polynomial terms first. If we take the function where f of x equals x squared, its derivative, f prime of x, measures the slope of the tangent line at any point x. If you plot that curve and sketch the slopes, a remarkable pattern emerges. The slope at x is always two times x. If you try the same experiment with x cubed, the derivative turns out to be three times x squared. For x to the fourth power, the derivative is four times x cubed. A universal rule hides beneath these examples. To find the derivative of any variable raised to a constant power, you bring that exponent down to the front as a coefficient, and then subtract one from the exponent in the power position.

This master pattern is called the power rule. Formally, for any real number power, the derivative of x raised to the power n is equal to n times x raised to the quantity n minus one. Why does this work? The reason is easiest to see when n is a positive whole number, where it flows directly from expanding binomial expressions using algebra. When you apply the limit definition to x raised to the power n, you expand the numerator using the binomial theorem. Every term after the first two contains a factor of the step size squared or higher. When you divide by the step size and take the limit as that step size approaches zero, all those higher order terms vanish into thin air, leaving behind exactly n times x raised to the power n minus one.

Next, we often encounter terms multiplied by constant numbers, such as five times x cubed. We need a rule for handling coefficients without having to restart our logic from scratch. This brings us to the constant multiple rule. It states simply that the derivative of a constant multiplied by a function is equal to that same constant multiplied by the derivative of the function. In spoken terms, you can factor the constant right out front, take the derivative of the function using whatever rule applies, and then multiply the result back by your constant. It tells us that scaling a function vertically scales its rate of change by the exact same factor.

Let us walk through a complete worked example step by step to see these ideas in action. Suppose we want to find the derivative of the polynomial function where f of x equals four times x cubed minus five times x squared plus seven times x minus three. We can tackle this term by term because the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives. For our first term, four times x cubed, we use the constant multiple rule to keep the four, and we apply the power rule to x cubed, which gives three times x squared. Multiplying four by three times x squared yields twelve times x squared for our first piece.

Moving to our second term, minus five times x squared, we keep the minus five and apply the power rule to x squared, which brings down a two and leaves x to the first power. Multiplying minus five by two times x gives minus ten times x. For our third term, seven times x, the variable x is raised to the first power. Applying the power rule brings down the one, and x to the zero power becomes one, leaving just the constant seven. Finally, our last term is minus three, which is a constant value. A constant number represents a completely flat horizontal line whose slope is always zero, so its derivative vanishes completely.

Putting all these pieces together, the complete derivative f prime of x for our polynomial is twelve times x squared minus ten times x plus seven. We can spot-check our work against the limit definition: in our last lesson, the limit process showed that the derivative of x squared is two x, and the power rule gives exactly the same answer. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will build on these algebraic foundations to discover how to handle products and quotients of functions when simple term by term differentiation is no longer enough.

## Key ideas
- The power rule states that the derivative of x raised to the power n is n times x raised to the quantity n minus one.
- The constant multiple rule allows us to factor a constant out of a derivative calculation and multiply it back at the end.
- Polynomials can be differentiated term by term because the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives of its parts.
- The derivative of any constant number is always zero because a horizontal line has a slope of zero.
