# Prosperity and Conflict in the 1920s

Imagine a world where the hum of the assembly line replaced the slow pace of the artisan’s workshop, and the air crackled with the sounds of a new invention called the radio. After the trauma and sacrifice of the First World War, the United States entered the 1920s with a desperate desire to modernize. This was an era defined by a soaring consumer economy. Mass production, which Henry Ford famously adapted for automobile manufacturing in 1913, made cars and household appliances affordable for the average family. While installment buying had existed for decades to help people purchase items like sewing machines or furniture, the 1920s saw a massive expansion of credit. This fueled a cycle of consumption that seemed to promise endless prosperity.

This economic shift transformed the American landscape. Cities grew taller and more crowded, and a new mass culture emerged. Across the country, people listened to the same music, watched the same Hollywood films, and cheered for the same sports heroes. Young people, particularly in urban centers, began to challenge traditional social norms. This led to the cultural phenomenon of the flapper, a woman who bobbed her hair, wore shorter skirts, and asserted a new sense of personal and social independence.

At the heart of this cultural explosion was the Harlem Renaissance. In the vibrant neighborhood of Harlem in New York City, Black writers, artists, and musicians created a flourishing movement that celebrated African American identity. Figures like Langston Hughes and Zora Neale Hurston used poetry and prose to explore the complexities of Black life. Meanwhile, jazz musicians like Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington redefined American music. This movement was a powerful assertion of dignity and creativity, even as it operated within a nation still deeply scarred by systemic racism and the legacy of the Jim Crow South.

Yet, this decade was not a smooth march toward progress. It was a time of deep anxiety and fierce reaction. The Russian Revolution of 1917 had sparked an intense fear of communism in the United States, known as the First Red Scare. This fear led to the targeting of immigrants and labor activists, resulting in mass arrests and deportations. The government passed strict immigration quotas, effectively closing the door on many people from Southern and Eastern Europe, and excluding Asians almost entirely.

These tensions extended into the moral and religious lives of Americans. The ratification of the Eighteenth Amendment in 1919 ushered in Prohibition, banning the manufacture and sale of alcohol. Instead of creating a more virtuous society, the law fueled the rise of organized crime and illegal speakeasies. Simultaneously, a conflict erupted between modernists and traditionalists over the role of religion in public life. This reached a national breaking point during the 1925 Scopes trial in Tennessee, where a high school teacher was prosecuted for teaching evolution. Although the teacher, John Scopes, was convicted and fined, the trial highlighted a profound divide between scientific modernism and fundamentalist Christianity.

The era’s conflicts were not just political or religious; they were fundamentally rooted in racial and ethnic hostility. The Ku Klux Klan experienced a massive revival, not just in the South, but across the Midwest and the West. Unlike its earlier incarnation, this version of the Klan positioned itself as a defender of what it called one hundred percent Americanism. Its members targeted not only Black Americans but also Catholics, Jews, and immigrants. By the mid-1920s, the organization claimed millions of members. This reflected a dark undercurrent of nativism that sought to preserve a vision of the nation that excluded anyone who did not fit their narrow definition of belonging.

The decade was a paradox of dizzying speed and fearful stagnation. Americans were embracing a future of global commerce and technological convenience while simultaneously clinging to older, more exclusionary visions of their country. The stock market boom of the late 1920s seemed to suggest that the good times would last forever, masking the fragility of the economic system. The dream of a permanent, prosperous consumer society, however, would soon face a catastrophic challenge that would force the nation to rethink its entire economic foundation.

## Key ideas
- The 1920s witnessed the rise of a modern consumer economy characterized by mass production, the expanded use of credit for durable goods, and a shared national culture shaped by radio and film.
- The Harlem Renaissance served as a vital intellectual and artistic movement that asserted Black identity and influence in American society despite persistent racial inequality.
- Social tensions manifested in the First Red Scare, the failure of Prohibition, and the intense cultural battles between modernists and fundamentalists, most notably in the Scopes trial.
- Nativist fears and racial prejudice fueled a resurgence of the Ku Klux Klan and the passage of restrictive immigration laws, highlighting the decade's struggle with defining who truly belonged in the American identity.
