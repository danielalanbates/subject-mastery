# Solving Exponential and Logarithmic Equations

Suppose you know that two to the power of a certain unknown quantity equals seven. How do you find that quantity when your variable is trapped high up in an exponent? In our last lesson, we explored the laws of logarithms, seeing how they allow us to turn multiplication into addition and exponents into coefficients. Today, we put those laws to work by solving equations where the unknown lurks either inside an exponent or tucked safely behind a logarithm. These techniques let us dismantle complex expressions and isolate our variable, taking us from a tangled puzzle to a precise, verifiable number.

When an equation features our variable stuck in the exponent, such as three to the power of the quantity x plus one equals ten, we cannot simply use division or roots to clear the way. Instead, we take a logarithm of both sides. Because logarithms and exponential functions are inverses, applying a logarithm to both sides unlocks the exponent and brings the variable down to ground level. We can use any logarithm base we like, though the natural logarithm, written with the base e, is usually the most efficient choice.

Let us walk through an example step by step. Suppose three to the power of the quantity x plus one equals ten. We take the natural logarithm of both sides. By the power property of logarithms, the exponent quantity x plus one moves from its elevated position to become a coefficient sitting in front of the natural logarithm of three. So we have the quantity x plus one times the natural logarithm of three equals the natural logarithm of ten. Now, divide both sides by the natural logarithm of three. This gives us the quantity x plus one equals the natural logarithm of ten divided by the natural logarithm of three. Finally, subtract one from both sides. Our exact answer is the quantity the natural logarithm of ten divided by the natural logarithm of three, minus one.

Logarithmic equations, where the variable sits inside the logarithm symbol rather than the exponent, require the opposite strategy. Here, we must rewrite the logarithmic statement in its equivalent exponential form to free the variable. Imagine we have the natural logarithm of the quantity x minus three equals two. By definition, a logarithmic statement tells us the exponent to which our base must be raised to produce a given result. Because the natural logarithm has a base of e, we rewrite this equation by raising e to the power of both sides, or equivalently, by writing e squared equals the quantity x minus three.

Once the logarithm is gone, solving for the variable is straightforward. Add three to both sides of e squared equals the quantity x minus three, and we find that x equals e squared plus three. However, working with logarithms introduces a vital danger that does not appear in linear or quadratic equations. Logarithms possess a strict domain restriction. You can never take the logarithm of zero or a negative number. This means every potential solution must be checked against the original equation to ensure it does not produce an illegal input inside any logarithm.

Let us look at what happens when an extraneous solution slips through our algebraic steps. Suppose we need to solve the natural logarithm of x plus the natural logarithm of the quantity x minus two equals zero. Using our logarithm laws, we combine the left side into a single logarithm, yielding the natural logarithm of the product x times the quantity x minus two equals zero. Rewriting this in exponential form, e to the power of zero equals x squared minus two x. Since any nonzero number to the power of zero is one, our equation becomes one equals x squared minus two x, or x squared minus two x minus one equals zero.

To solve x squared minus two x minus one equals zero using the quadratic formula, we identify our coefficients as one for the squared term, negative two for the linear term, and negative one for the constant term. Substituting these into the quadratic formula, we have the negative of negative two, which is two, plus or minus the square root of the quantity negative two squared minus four times one times negative one, all divided by two times one. Simplifying under the square root, negative two squared is four, and negative four times one times negative one is positive four, so four plus four gives us eight. This leaves us with two plus or minus the square root of eight, all divided by two. We can simplify the square root of eight as two times the square root of two, making our expression two plus or minus two times the square root of two, all divided by two, which reduces to one plus or minus the square root of two.

One of those values is one plus the square root of two, and the other is one minus the square root of two. The square root of two is roughly one point four one four, meaning one plus the square root of two is about two point four one four, which works nicely when plugged back into our original logarithmic expressions. But what about the second candidate, one minus the square root of two? That calculation gives us approximately negative zero point four one four. If we try to substitute that negative number back into the original term involving the natural logarithm of x, we are asked to find the natural logarithm of a negative number, which is undefined in the real number system. Therefore, that second candidate is an extraneous solution and must be discarded. Recognizing the boundaries of our functions ensures our final answers are mathematically valid, setting the stage for when we use these very equations to track real-world phenomena like population growth and radioactive decay.

## Key ideas
- When an equation has a variable in an exponent, taking a logarithm of both sides brings the exponent down into a manageable position.
- Logarithmic equations are solved by rewriting them in their equivalent exponential form to isolate the variable.
- Every potential solution to a logarithmic equation must be substituted back into the original statement to check whether it violates domain restrictions.
- Solutions that produce a zero or a negative number inside any logarithm are extraneous and must be discarded.
