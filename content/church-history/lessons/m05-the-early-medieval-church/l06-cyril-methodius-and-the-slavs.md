# Cyril, Methodius and the Slavs

Imagine trying to explain the most profound truths of your faith to someone, but you are forbidden from using their native language. You are forced to speak in a tongue they do not understand, making the message feel like a foreign imposition rather than a gift of grace. This was the challenge faced by many missionaries in the ninth century. As the Byzantine Empire sought to expand its influence among the Slavic peoples, the church faced a pivotal question: should the gospel be locked within the prestige languages of Latin and Greek, or could it be translated into the living languages of the people?

In our previous lesson, we discussed the intense struggle over icons within Byzantium. While the imperial capital was preoccupied with these theological debates, the brothers Cyril and Methodius—two scholars from Thessaloniki, in modern-day Greece—were preparing for a different kind of mission. In the year 863 CE, Prince Rastislav of Great Moravia, located in what is now the Czech Republic and Slovakia, requested teachers who could explain the Christian faith to his people in their own language. His request was partly motivated by a desire to resist Frankish ecclesiastical influence and maintain Moravian political independence. The Byzantine Emperor chose these two brothers for the task.

Cyril and Methodius were uniquely prepared for this work. They were linguists who understood that for the Slavic people to truly grasp the liturgy and the scriptures, they needed a written alphabet. Cyril developed a script, known as Glagolitic, which was designed specifically to represent the unique sounds of the Slavic tongue. By translating the Gospels and the liturgical texts into Old Church Slavonic, they essentially gave the Slavic people a literary culture. This was a radical act. It suggested that no single language held a monopoly on holiness and that the Word of God could be expressed through the unique rhythm and structure of any culture.

Their mission, however, was not without controversy. Many church leaders in the West, particularly those influenced by the Frankish church, insisted that only the three languages used on the inscription above the cross of Christ—Hebrew, Greek, and Latin—were appropriate for worship. They viewed the use of Slavonic as a suspicious innovation. Despite this resistance, the brothers traveled to Rome to defend their work. They eventually secured the approval of the Pope, who recognized the pastoral necessity of their mission. This support allowed the Slavonic liturgy to take root, though the brothers faced constant political maneuvering between the influence of the Frankish West and the Byzantine East.

After the brothers died, their disciples, including Clement of Ohrid and Naum, carried the mission to Bulgaria. There, they developed the Cyrillic script, which was derived from Glagolitic and Greek. This script eventually superseded Glagolitic for writing most Slavic languages, especially in Russia and Serbia, and Old Church Slavonic became the established liturgical language of the Bulgarian Orthodox Church.

The legacy of these brothers reached a turning point in the year 988 CE. Vladimir the Great, the ruler of the Kievan Rus—a state centered in what is now Ukraine—was searching for a religion that would unite his people. According to tradition, Vladimir sent envoys to Constantinople. They described their experience in the Hagia Sophia as so transcendent that they did not know whether they were in heaven or on earth. Vladimir chose to align his kingdom with the Byzantine tradition. He was baptized, and his people followed suit in a mass conversion. While this initial baptism employed Greek clergy and the Greek liturgy, the foundation laid by Cyril and Methodius allowed the Slavonic liturgy to be introduced shortly thereafter. This ensured that the church became deeply embedded in the cultural identity of the Slavic people.

Today, Cyril and Methodius are venerated as saints in both the Catholic and Orthodox Churches and are celebrated as co-patrons of Europe. Their work transformed the landscape of the continent. By validating the use of local languages in worship, they created a model for missionary work that respected the dignity of the recipient culture. Yet, this expansion also deepened the cultural and ecclesiastical divide between the Latin-speaking West and the Greek and Slavonic-speaking East. These tensions would eventually lead to a clearer definition of authority, setting the stage for the reforms that would define the High Middle Ages.

## Key ideas

- Cyril and Methodius were Byzantine scholars who invented the Glagolitic alphabet to translate the Bible and liturgy into Old Church Slavonic, making Christianity accessible to Slavic peoples.
- The brothers’ disciples, such as Clement of Ohrid, developed the Cyrillic script and established Old Church Slavonic as the liturgical language in Bulgaria.
- The baptism of Vladimir the Great in 988 CE marked the formal conversion of the Kievan Rus, permanently aligning that region with the Byzantine Orthodox tradition.
- Cyril and Methodius are venerated as saints in both the Catholic and Orthodox Churches and are honored as co-patrons of Europe for their role in shaping Slavic culture and identity.
