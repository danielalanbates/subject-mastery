# Bacteria and Archaea

Imagine dipping a single drop of water from a quiet pond, placing it under a microscope, and discovering a bustling metropolis. In our last lesson, we looked at viruses, those obligate cellular hijackers that sit on the fuzzy border of living and nonliving things. Today, we cross firmly into the domain of true cellular life, exploring the two great groups of prokaryotes: bacteria and archaea. While viruses require a living host cell to replicate, prokaryotes are self-contained biochemical powerhouses. They lack a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, yet they run the oldest, most diverse metabolic show on Earth, sustaining nearly every ecosystem we know.

To appreciate these microscopic architects, we must first look at how they harvest energy and process matter. Unlike animals and plants, which rely primarily on organic carbon consumption and aerobic respiration or oxygenic photosynthesis, prokaryotes display astonishing metabolic flexibility. Some are phototrophs, capturing sunlight through unique pigments without necessarily producing oxygen as a byproduct. Others are chemoautotrophs, pulling energy straight from inorganic compounds like hydrogen sulfide, iron, or ammonia. Deep in the pitch-black ocean trenches, chemoautotrophic bacteria fuel entire ecosystems around hydrothermal vents, completely independent of solar energy. Furthermore, their respiratory strategies span a vast spectrum. They range from strict aerobes that need oxygen to survive, to obligate anaerobes for which oxygen is a lethal poison, to facultative anaerobes that can switch back and forth depending on environmental conditions.

Archaea take this metabolic ingenuity to legendary extremes, earning the classic label of extremophiles, though modern genetic studies show many species also live quietly in ordinary soils and surface ocean waters. Structurally, archaea look much like bacteria under a microscope, but their internal molecular machinery tells a radically different evolutionary story. Their cell membranes are built from unique lipids linked together by ether bonds rather than the ester bonds found in bacteria and eukaryotes, granting them supreme structural stability in hostile environments. Some archaea, known as halophiles, thrive in water saturated with salt, such as the Dead Sea. Others, the thermophiles, flourish in boiling hot springs, possessing specialized enzymes that refuse to denature at temperatures that would cook a typical protein. In oxygen-depleted mud and animal digestive tracts, methanogenic archaea produce methane gas as a byproduct of their unique energy metabolism.

Beyond their metabolic wizardry, bacteria and archaea play indispensable ecological roles that keep the biosphere running. They are nature's ultimate recyclers. When plants and animals die, heterotrophic bacteria break down complex organic molecules, returning vital carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus to the soil and water. Without this decomposition, nutrient cycles would grind to a halt, and life would quickly suffocate under its own accumulated waste. In the soil and in root nodules, specialized nitrogen-fixing bacteria pull inert nitrogen gas straight from the air and convert it into forms that plants can readily absorb, driving global agriculture and wild plant growth alike.

The sheer scale of these populations is difficult to grasp. A single gram of fertile soil can contain billions of individual bacterial cells. These organisms have been evolving for over three billion years, adapting to every conceivable niche on the planet. They exist in the clouds, in deep crustal rocks miles beneath the surface, and in the frozen ice of the poles. This ubiquity is not accidental. It is the result of rapid reproduction and horizontal gene transfer, a process where they swap genetic material directly with their neighbors. This allows them to spread beneficial traits, such as antibiotic resistance or new metabolic pathways, rapidly across populations, sometimes even between different species.

Within our own bodies, complex communities of prokaryotes form vital microbiomes that aid digestion, train our immune systems, and ward off pathogenic invaders. Of course, a small fraction of bacteria are pathogenic, meaning they cause disease by producing toxins or invading tissues. Yet even here, evolutionary studies remind us that disease is just one minor facet of a vast, ancient domain. We are essentially walking ecosystems, hosting trillions of microscopic residents that influence our health in ways we are only beginning to understand. As we trace how these single-celled pioneers mastered every corner of our planet, we set the stage for our next step, where we will examine how more complex eukaryotic cells finally emerged from ancestral lineages. By understanding the prokaryotic foundation, we gain a clearer view of the entire tree of life.

## Key ideas
- Bacteria and archaea are ancient prokaryotic domains that lack nuclei and membrane-bound organelles while exhibiting immense metabolic diversity.
- Prokaryotes utilize diverse energy strategies, including phototrophy, organic heterotrophy, and chemoautotrophy fueled by inorganic compounds.
- Archaea feature distinct membrane lipid chemistry that allows many of them to thrive in extreme environments like boiling hot springs and hypersaline lakes.
- Prokaryotes drive global nutrient cycles by decomposing organic matter and fixing atmospheric nitrogen into biologically usable forms.
- The success of bacteria and archaea is bolstered by rapid reproduction and horizontal gene transfer, allowing them to adapt to nearly every environment on Earth.
