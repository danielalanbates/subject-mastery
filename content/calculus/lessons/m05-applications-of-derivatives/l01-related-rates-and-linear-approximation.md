# Related Rates and Linear Approximation

Imagine holding a ladder that slides down a slick stone wall. The bottom of the ladder races away from the building across the pavement while the top drops downward. These two motions are locked in a frantic race, and if you know how fast the bottom is sliding, you can figure out the speed of the descending top. This is the heart of related rates, a method that translates geometric relationships into moving velocities. In our last lesson, we learned how to find the derivative of an inverse function. Now, we use those derivatives to watch how changing quantities chase one another through time.

The development of calculus rests on the work of Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. They unlocked these methods centuries ago, though their historic priority dispute was bitter and deeply personal. Newton introduced notation using dots over variables to represent rates of change with respect to time, while Leibniz introduced the fractional notation of differentials, such as d y over d x, which remains standard today.

To understand related rates, we rely on the chain rule, which lets us differentiate equations where variables depend on an unspoken time variable, usually called t. Picture a circular ripple spreading across a still pond. As time passes, the radius increases, and the area increases right along with it. We know that the area of a circle equals pi times the radius squared. If we want to know how fast the area is growing when the radius is three feet, we do not just plug in three. Instead, we take the derivative of both sides of the area equation with respect to time.

Let us walk through this circular ripple step by step. We have the equation where the area equals pi times the radius squared. We differentiate both sides with respect to time. On the left side, the derivative of the area with respect to time is the rate of change of the area. On the right side, we apply the power rule and the chain rule to the radius squared. The derivative of the radius squared is two times the radius, multiplied by the derivative of the radius with respect to time. So, the rate of change of the area equals two times pi times the radius, all multiplied by the rate of change of the radius.

If the radius is expanding at a constant rate of two feet per second, and we want to find the speed of the area growth when the radius is three feet, we simply substitute those numbers into our derivative equation. The radius is three, and the rate of change of the radius is two. Multiplying two times pi times three times two gives twelve times pi square feet per second. Notice that we substituted our specific measurements only after taking the derivative. If you plug in a changing measurement too early, you freeze a moving picture into a still photograph and lose the velocity entirely.

Related rates problems always follow this reliable rhythm. First, imagine the physical layout and assign letters to the quantities that change. Second, write an equation that ties those variables together, often using the Pythagorean theorem or trigonometric ratios. Third, differentiate every term with respect to time using the chain rule. Finally, substitute your known values and rates to solve for the missing speed. The beauty of this approach is that it handles everything from draining conical water tanks to airplanes flying overhead.

Derivatives also let us zoom in on complicated curves and pretend they are straight lines. This technique is called linear approximation, or using differentials to approximate values. If you stand on the surface of the earth, it feels flat because you are zoomed in so close. In the same way, if you zoom in on a smooth curve near a specific point, it looks almost identical to its tangent line. We can use the equation of that tangent line to estimate difficult function values without a calculator.

To formalize this method, we introduce differentials, written as d y and d x when spoken. The differential d y equals f prime of x times d x, with the derivative taken at our known point, which expresses the linear approximation formula stating that the change in y is approximately the derivative at that point times the change in x. The error in this approximation is the difference between the actual change in the function and this linear estimate, which shrinks much faster than the change in x as you get closer to the point of tangency.

Suppose we need to estimate the square root of four point one. We know the square root of four is exactly two, and four point one is very close to four. Let us use the function where f of x equals the square root of x. The derivative of f of x, or f prime of x, equals one divided by the quantity two times the square root of x. At our starting point of four, the derivative is one divided by four, or zero point two five. The tangent line equation tells us that the value at four point one is approximately the value at four plus the derivative at four multiplied by the tiny change in x, which is zero point one, written here as d x.

Putting those pieces together, we start with two, and we add zero point two five multiplied by zero point one. This gives us two plus zero point zero two five, resulting in an approximation of two point zero two five. The true square root of four point one is remarkably close to this estimate, showing how wonderfully a simple straight line can approximate a curving reality. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will use these very same derivative tools to hunt down the highest peaks and lowest valleys of functions.

## Key ideas
- Related rates problems use the chain rule to connect the speeds at which different physical quantities change over time, following a historical framework shaped by the independent discoveries of Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz.
- Always differentiate your geometric equations with respect to time before substituting numerical values for changing variables.
- Linear approximation uses the tangent line of a function near a known point to estimate difficult values nearby.
- Differentials, written as d y and d x, allow us to state that d y equals f prime of x times d x, providing a precise way to estimate function changes with a small error term.
