# Factoring Trinomials and Special Forms

Imagine you are standing before a rectangular lawn whose total area is given by the expression x squared plus seven x plus twelve, and you need to cut it into two rectangular strips whose widths correspond to the factors of that area. In our last lesson, we pulled out greatest common factors and grouped terms to break polynomials down. But what happens when a polynomial does not have a single term common to every piece, yet still splits neatly into the product of two binomials? Factoring trinomials is the art of running multiplication backward, turning a sum of three terms into a product of simpler polynomials, unlocking equations that would otherwise remain stubbornly locked.

Let us begin with trinomials where the leading coefficient, the number multiplying x squared, is simply one. Consider the expression x squared plus seven x plus twelve. We want to find two binomials, the quantity x plus p and the quantity x plus q, whose product equals this trinomial. When we multiply those two binomials using the distributive property, the outer and inner terms combine to give the sum of p and q times x, while the last terms multiply to give p times q. This means our constants p and q must do a double job: their product must equal the constant term twelve, and their sum must equal the middle coefficient seven.

We search through the factor pairs of twelve. One and twelve give a sum of thirteen. Two and six give a sum of eight. Three and four give a sum of seven. We have found our numbers. Therefore, x squared plus seven x plus twelve factors completely into the product of the quantity x plus three and the quantity x plus four. You can always verify this by multiplying the binomials back together; x times x is x squared, the outer and inner products give four x and three x which add to seven x, and three times four gives twelve.

When the leading coefficient is larger than one, the search becomes slightly more nuanced. Suppose we want to factor two x squared plus seven x plus three. The leading coefficient is now two, and the constant term is three. We look for two binomials whose first terms multiply to two x squared, which means one must start with two x and the other with x. Their outer and inner products must combine to produce the middle term, seven x, while their last terms must multiply to three. Because three is prime, its factors are only one and three. We test the possible combinations. If we place three with two x and one with x, the outer product is six x and the inner is one x, which add up to seven x. Thus, two x squared plus seven x plus three factors into the quantity two x plus one times the quantity x plus three.

Beyond general trinomials, certain algebraic structures appear so frequently that mathematicians gave them special names and shortcut rules. A perfect square trinomial arises when a binomial is multiplied by itself. For example, squaring the quantity x plus five gives x squared plus ten x plus twenty-five. Notice the pattern: the first term is squared, the last term is the square of five, and the middle term is twice the product of x and five. If the middle term is negative, such as in the expression x squared minus ten x plus twenty-five, the binomial squares as the quantity x minus five times the quantity x minus five. Whenever you spot a trinomial where the first and last terms are positive squares and the middle term is twice the product of their square roots, you can factor it instantly as a squared binomial.

Another vital special form is the difference of two squares, such as x squared minus sixteen. Here, the middle term has vanished because we are multiplying the sum and difference of the same two values, the quantity x plus four and the quantity x minus four. The outer and inner products cancel each other out completely, leaving just the square of the first term minus the square of the second.

Cubic expressions also follow precise structural rules. The sum of cubes formula states that x cubed plus eight factors into the quantity x plus two times the trinomial x squared minus two x plus four. Conversely, the difference of cubes formula states that x cubed minus eight factors into the quantity x minus two times the trinomial x squared plus two x plus four. Each of these templates combines a binomial and a specific trinomial to break down terms that ordinary factoring cannot touch. As we move next into rational expressions, you will see how these fully factored forms allow us to cancel common factors across fractions just as we do with ordinary numbers.

## Key ideas
- Factoring a trinomial with a leading coefficient of one relies on finding two numbers whose product equals the constant term and whose sum equals the middle coefficient.
- When the leading coefficient is greater than one, we test combinations of binomial factors whose first and last terms produce the correct outer and inner products when multiplied.
- Perfect square trinomials feature first and last terms that are squares, with a middle term equal to twice the product of their square roots, using either a positive or a negative sign.
- The difference of two squares splits into the product of the sum and difference of those two squared values, with the middle term canceling out.
- The sum and difference of cubes formulas break cubic binomials down into the product of a binomial and a specific trinomial.
