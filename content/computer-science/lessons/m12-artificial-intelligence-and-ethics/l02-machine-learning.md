# Machine Learning

Imagine you want to teach a computer to identify a picture of a cat. In our previous lesson, we looked at how computers solve problems through search, essentially exploring a map of possibilities to find a goal. But how would you write a search algorithm to identify a cat? You might try to list rules: ears are triangular, whiskers are long, and eyes have a certain shape. Yet, as soon as you define those rules, you encounter a problem. What about a cat curled into a ball? What about a cat seen from behind? The variations are endless, and writing hand-written rules for every possible feline posture is impossible. This is where machine learning changes the game. Instead of telling the computer what a cat looks like, we show it thousands of examples and let it figure out the patterns itself.

Machine learning is a shift from explicit programming to statistical inference. We provide the machine with a massive collection of data, and it builds a mathematical model that represents the underlying patterns. This process is generally categorized into three main approaches. The first is supervised learning. In this setup, we provide the computer with labeled data. For our cat example, we feed it thousands of photos, each tagged with the word cat or not a cat. The computer analyzes the pixels, identifies features that correlate with those labels, and learns to predict the label for new, unseen photos. It is like a student learning from a textbook that includes an answer key at the back.

The second approach is unsupervised learning. Here, we give the computer data without any labels or predefined answers. The goal is not to predict a specific outcome but to find hidden structures or groupings within the information. For instance, if you gave a computer a database of customer purchasing habits, it might notice that one group of people consistently buys gardening supplies while another group buys specialized electronic parts. The computer does not know what those groups are called, but it discovers that they exist based on the similarity of the data points. It is like a researcher looking at a pile of raw, disorganized field notes and finding natural clusters of information.

The third approach is reinforcement learning, which is modeled on the way we might train a pet or learn a game. The computer, often called an agent, interacts with an environment. It performs actions and receives feedback in the form of rewards or penalties. If the agent makes a good move, it gets a positive score; if it makes a mistake, it gets a negative one. Over time, the agent learns a strategy to maximize its total reward. This is how many systems have learned to play complex strategy games, by playing millions of rounds against themselves and refining their tactics based on the results.

Regardless of the approach, the quality of the model depends entirely on the data. We typically split our data into two sets. The first is the training data, which the computer uses to build its model. The second is the test data, which we hide from the computer during the training phase. Once the model is built, we show it the test data to see how well it performs on information it has never encountered before. This is a crucial step to ensure the model has actually learned the general concept rather than just memorizing the specific examples it saw during training.

However, we must balance the complexity of our models. If a model is too simple to capture the underlying pattern, we call this underfitting. An underfit model performs poorly on both the training data and the test data because it fails to grasp the fundamental logic of the problem. Conversely, we face the trap of overfitting. Imagine a student who memorizes every single practice question in a textbook but fails the actual exam because the questions are phrased slightly differently. That student has overfit their knowledge. In computing, overfitting happens when a model learns the training data too perfectly, including the random noise or irrelevant details. Because it has memorized the specific training examples rather than the underlying pattern, it fails to generalize to new data. As of the mid-2020s, balancing the complexity of a model to ensure it learns the signal without falling for underfitting or overfitting remains a central challenge for engineers.

By shifting from rigid, hand-written instructions to these pattern-finding techniques, we have enabled computers to perform tasks that were once thought to be exclusively human, such as recognizing faces or translating languages. However, as we rely more on these statistical models, the way they perceive and interpret the world becomes a vital area of study. To understand how these models can be made even more powerful and capable of handling complex, non-linear relationships, we must next examine the architecture of neural networks and how they are loosely inspired by the structure of the human brain.

## Key ideas

- Machine learning allows computers to learn patterns from data rather than relying on hand-written rules that are often too rigid for complex, real-world tasks.
- Supervised learning uses labeled examples to train a model, while unsupervised learning looks for hidden structures in unlabeled data, and reinforcement learning uses rewards to guide behavior.
- Data is divided into training sets for building the model and test sets for evaluating how well the model generalizes to new, unseen information.
- Underfitting occurs when a model is too simple to capture the underlying pattern, while overfitting happens when a model memorizes random noise in the training data, both of which prevent accurate predictions on new data.
