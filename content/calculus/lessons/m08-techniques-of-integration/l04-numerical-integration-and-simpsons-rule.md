# Numerical Integration and Simpson's Rule

Imagine you are sailing a boat along a winding river, and you need to estimate the cross-sectional area of the water to know how much cargo you can carry. You take depth measurements at regular intervals across the width. But the bottom of the river is irregular, and when you try to write down a formula for the curve, you find it has no elementary antiderivative. Even with advanced techniques like partial fractions, you are completely stuck. What do you do when the fundamental theorem of calculus fails you because the antiderivative simply cannot be expressed in terms of basic functions? You turn to numerical integration.

In our previous work, we learned how to break down complex algebraic fractions to find exact antiderivatives. But many definite integrals, such as the integral from zero to one of the quantity e raised to the power of negative x squared, d x, have no closed-form antiderivative at all. To solve these, we must approximate the area under the curve using geometric shapes, much like the Riemann sums we first encountered when defining the definite integral. Instead of arbitrary rectangles, however, we use structured methods that achieve high precision with very little computational effort.

The simplest approach is the midpoint rule. Imagine dividing the interval from our lower bound, call it the lower limit, to our upper bound, call it the upper limit, into a number of equal subintervals. Within each subinterval, we construct a rectangle whose height is determined by the function value at the exact midpoint of that interval. To calculate this, we take the width of each subinterval, multiply it by the sum of all the function values evaluated at the respective midpoints, and that gives us our total estimated area. The midpoint rule tends to be quite efficient because evaluating the function at the center of each slice balances out the overestimates and underestimates.

Another classic technique is the trapezoidal rule. Rather than using flat-topped rectangles, we draw straight line segments connecting the function values at the endpoints of each subinterval, creating a series of trapezoids. The area of a trapezoid is the width of the subinterval divided by two, multiplied by the sum of the left and right function values. When we add these trapezoidal areas together across the entire interval from the lower limit to the upper limit, every interior function value is counted twice because it serves as the right endpoint for one trapezoid and the left endpoint for the next. Thus, the trapezoidal rule sums the first function value, two times every intermediate function value, and the final function value, all multiplied by the width of the subinterval divided by two.

While rectangles and trapezoids use straight lines, Simpson's Rule achieves a dramatic leap in accuracy by using parabolas. Named after the eighteenth century mathematician Thomas Simpson, this method fits a quadratic curve through sets of three consecutive points along our function. Because Simpson's Rule relies on fitting these parabolas to pairs of subintervals at a time, we must divide our total interval into an even number of subintervals. The formula looks remarkably regular. We multiply the subinterval width divided by three by a weighted sum of the function values. The weights start with a one, then alternate four and two for the intermediate values, and end with a one for the final value, forming the pattern one, four, two, four, two, all the way to four and one.

Let us walk through a concrete example using Simpson's Rule to evaluate the integral from zero to two of x squared, d x, using four subintervals. First, our total interval length is two, and dividing by four subintervals gives a width of one-half for each slice. Our partition points are zero, one-half, one, three-halves, and two. The function values at these points are zero, one-quarter, one, nine-quarters, and four. Now, we apply the weights. We take the width one-half, divide it by three to get one-sixth, and multiply by the sum of the weighted function values. That gives us one times zero, plus four times one-quarter, plus two times one, plus four times nine-quarters, plus one times four. Summing those terms inside yields sixteen, and multiplying by one-sixth gives sixteen-sixths, which simplifies to eight-thirds. Because x squared has a simple antiderivative, we know the exact answer is indeed eight-thirds, showing how powerfully parabolic arcs capture curved areas.

As we push these numerical approximations further, we often want to know how much error our estimate carries, allowing us to choose a precise number of subintervals to guarantee accuracy within a desired tolerance. When dealing with functions that stretch toward infinity or over unbounded intervals, however, numerical grids alone are not enough, leading us to investigate the behavior of limits at the outer edges of our domain.

## Key ideas
- Numerical integration allows us to approximate definite integrals when an antiderivative cannot be found using standard analytical techniques.
- The midpoint rule estimates the area under a curve by constructing rectangles whose heights match the function value at the center of each subinterval.
- The trapezoidal rule uses linear segments to form trapezoids over each subinterval, weighting interior function values by two.
- Simpson's Rule requires an even number of subintervals because it fits parabolic arcs across pairs of slices, using an alternating weight pattern that begins and ends with one and cycles through four and two.
