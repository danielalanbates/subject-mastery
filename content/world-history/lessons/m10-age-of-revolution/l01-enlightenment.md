# The Enlightenment and Political Philosophy

Imagine walking through a bustling eighteenth-century salon in Paris, London, or Edinburgh. The air is thick with tobacco smoke, the smell of roasted coffee, and the sharp electricity of argument. Noblemen, merchants, scientists, and writers are gathered around a drawing-room table to debate a radical proposition. What if kings do not rule by divine right? What if political power does not flow downward from a heavenly mandate, but upward from the consent of ordinary people? This intellectual ferment was not confined to polite drawing rooms. It was the beating heart of the Enlightenment, an intellectual movement that systematically dismantled centuries of entrenched political and religious authority across Europe and the broader Atlantic world.

In our last lesson, we watched Tokugawa Japan tightly control social order and foreign contact to maintain absolute internal stability through rigid hierarchy and tradition. Across the globe by the mid-eighteenth century, however, a very different intellectual current began to swell. Thinkers across the Atlantic world started asking dangerous questions. They took the empirical methods of the Scientific Revolution—which had recently unlocked the physical laws governing stars and gravity—and applied them directly to human society, government, and morality. If the physical universe operated by rational, discoverable laws, they reasoned, human societies must operate under natural laws as well.

At the core of this new philosophy was a sweeping re-imagining of human nature and political legitimacy. John Locke argued in his Essay Concerning Human Understanding, published in 1689, that the mind begins as a blank slate shaped by experience, and in his Two Treatises of Government, from the same year, that political power rests on natural rights rather than inherited station. Locke and others formulated the concept of natural rights. These were fundamental entitlements—specifically to life, liberty, and property—that belonged to every human being simply by virtue of existing. Governments, in this view, were not masters placed over subjects, but trustees hired to protect those rights. If a government failed in that trust, the social contract was broken, and the people held the moral right to alter or abolish it.

The movement quickly expanded beyond political theory into a fierce critique of established religion and traditional institutions. Voltaire used biting satire to attack clerical power, religious intolerance, and superstition, championing freedom of speech and reason. At the same time, Denis Diderot spearheaded the monumental Encyclopédie, published beginning in 1751, which sought to compile and spread all human knowledge and promote secular, rational thought. These texts did not stay confined to European capitals. Enlightenment books, newspapers, and pamphlets traveled across maritime trade routes, circulating through port cities in colonial America and the Caribbean, shaping a vibrant transatlantic conversation among merchants, enslaved intellectuals, and planters alike.

As these writings circulated, they transformed abstract philosophical debates into practical blueprints for political action. Baron de Montesquieu proposed in The Spirit of the Laws, published in 1748, dividing governmental power into separate executive, legislative, and judicial branches to prevent tyranny and safeguard liberty. Jean-Jacques Rousseau pushed these concepts further in The Social Contract, published in 1762, by arguing that legitimate sovereignty resided entirely within the general will of the people, rather than in the person of a monarch.

These were not merely academic exercises. They became the intellectual ammunition that would soon shatter the old political order. When colonists in North America rose up against British rule in the American Declaration of Independence in 1776, they explicitly justified their rebellion using Lockean natural rights and the right of revolution. A few years later, across the Atlantic, the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen in 1789 codified these philosophical tenets into revolutionary law. When citizens in France and the wider Atlantic world took up arms against royal absolutism and colonial subjugation, they did not just fight over taxes or bread; they spoke the fluent, revolutionary language of natural rights, social contracts, and popular sovereignty that would reshape the modern world.

## Key ideas

- The Enlightenment applied the rational and empirical methods of the Scientific Revolution to human society, government, and morality.
- Thinkers argued that human beings possess natural rights to life, liberty, and property that exist prior to any government, as famously outlined in John Locke’s Two Treatises of Government in 1689.
- Figures like Voltaire championed religious tolerance and freedom of speech, while Denis Diderot’s Encyclopédie cataloged secular knowledge to challenge traditional authority.
- Seminal texts such as Montesquieu’s The Spirit of the Laws in 1748 and Rousseau’s The Social Contract in 1762 introduced the separation of powers and popular sovereignty.
- These radical ideas diffused across the Atlantic world, directly inspiring the political rhetoric of the American Declaration of Independence in 1776 and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen in 1789.
