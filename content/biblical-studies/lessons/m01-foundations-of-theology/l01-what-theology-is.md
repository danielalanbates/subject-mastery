# What Theology Is

Imagine you are sitting at a kitchen table, a cup of coffee steaming in front of you, and a friend asks, “What does God want me to know about life?” The question is simple, but the answer can be as deep as the ocean. In the Christian tradition, we call the disciplined attempt to answer that question “theology.” The Greek word theologia, from theos meaning God and logos meaning word or study, literally means the study of God. Theology is not just a collection of private opinions; it is a careful, reflective study of God’s self-revelation—what God has chosen to make known about Himself—and of what that revelation means for human beings. In this lesson, we will explore what it means to treat theology as a disciplined reflection, why we focus on God’s self-revelation, and how that shapes the way Christians live.

When scholars speak of disciplined reflection, they mean a methodical, communal, and critical approach. This task takes shape in several distinct arenas. Academic theology happens in universities and seminaries, where scholars use rigorous historical and linguistic tools to study texts and doctrines. Church theology, often called dogmatic or systematic theology, belongs to the life of the Christian community, articulating the core beliefs of the faith. Pastoral and practical theology focuses on how these beliefs guide everyday ministry, preaching, counseling, and ethical living. Across all these settings, theology remains a conversation that stretches across centuries, where each generation listens, asks questions, and adds its voice while staying faithful to the historic message.

The core of Christian theology is God’s self-revelation. In the Bible, we see two main ways God reveals Himself: general revelation, which is visible in the created world, and special revelation, which is communicated through Scripture, the life of Jesus, and the ongoing work of the Holy Spirit. The next lesson in this series will examine those two modes in detail. For now, we can say that general revelation points us to God’s existence and attributes, like power and goodness, while special revelation tells us the specifics of God’s covenant relationship with humanity in Jesus Christ.

Why focus on self-revelation? Because God is not an abstract force that we can infer merely by looking at the universe; He is a personal, relational being who chooses to make Himself known. The Apostle Paul writes in Ephesians chapter three, verse nine, in the King James Version, of “the fellowship of the mystery, which from the beginning of the world hath been hid in God.” This verse shows that theology begins with what God has disclosed, not with what we imagine He might be. The task, then, is to take that disclosure seriously, to examine it with honesty, and to let it shape our worldview.

A disciplined theological reflection proceeds in three broad steps. First, we gather the sources of revelation, which include Scripture, the historic creeds, the teachings of the church fathers, and the lived experience of the faithful. Second, we employ the tools of reason, historical criticism, and philosophical analysis to interpret those sources. Third, we apply the insights to contemporary life. For example, when the Psalmist declares in Psalm twenty-four, verse one, in the King James Version, “The earth is the LORD's, and the fulness thereof,” a theological reflection leads us to a stewardship ethic that respects creation as God’s property.

Theological reflection is also communal. No single person can claim a monopoly on truth about God. The early church gathered in ecumenical councils, such as the Council of Nicaea in 325, to discern the correct understanding of Christ’s nature. Their decisions, recorded in the Nicene Creed, still serve as a benchmark for orthodoxy. Today, theologians from Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions continue to dialogue, each bringing distinct emphases. For instance, Catholics and Orthodox Christians often stress the authority of both Scripture and Sacred Tradition, while many Protestants prioritize the principle that Scripture alone is the final rule of faith. Recognizing these perspectives helps us see theology as a shared, ongoing conversation rather than a solitary speculation.

Another important aspect is the purpose of theology. It is not an academic exercise detached from life; it is intended to transform. The Apostle Peter exhorts believers in the First Epistle of Peter, chapter one, verse fifteen, in the American Standard Version: “be ye yourselves also holy in all manner of living.” Theology therefore aims to shape character, inform worship, guide ethical decisions, and give meaning to suffering and joy. When we understand that God has revealed Himself as a loving Father, a merciful Savior, and a just Judge, we are invited to embody those qualities in our own relationships.

Consider a concrete example: the doctrine of the Trinity. The biblical data, such as Jesus’ baptism and the apostolic prayers, suggest a plurality within God. Early church theologians, using philosophical language, articulated this as one God in three persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. This doctrine is not an abstract puzzle; it informs how Christians pray, how they understand salvation, and how they view community. Thus, a disciplined reflection on God’s self-revelation leads to practical outworkings in worship and ethics.

Finally, theology must remain humble. History shows us that even well-meaning scholars have erred, and that the church has refined its understandings over time. The Reformation, for example, challenged certain medieval teachings while affirming core doctrines like the divinity of Christ. Modern biblical scholarship raises questions about authorship and dating, yet believers across the Christian traditions hold that the core message of God’s self-revelation remains a secure anchor. This humility invites us to keep listening, questioning, and learning, always trusting that God has spoken.

## Key ideas
- Theology is a disciplined, communal reflection on God’s self-revelation and its implications for belief, worship, and practice.
- The theological task spans academic research, church dogmatics, and practical ministry in the life of the faithful.
- God’s self-revelation includes general revelation in creation and special revelation in Scripture and the person of Christ.
- Different Christian traditions emphasize various sources, including Scripture, Tradition, and Reason, while seeking to remain faithful to historic orthodoxy.
- The purpose of theology is transformative, shaping character, ethics, and community in response to God’s disclosed nature.
