# Proteins and Nucleic Acids

Proteins and nucleic acids are the two families of macromolecules that give cells the ability to store information and to perform precise chemical work. Both are polymers, meaning they are built from many repeating units that join together in a chain. In proteins the repeating unit is the amino acid; in nucleic acids the repeating unit is the nucleotide. Understanding how these units connect, how the chains fold, and how they interact with one another is essential for everything from enzyme catalysis to heredity.

Amino acids are small molecules that share a common backbone: a central carbon atom (the alpha carbon) attached to a hydrogen atom, a carboxyl group, and an amino group. Each also carries a distinctive side chain, often called an R group, that gives the amino acid its unique chemical character. When the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of the next, a peptide bond forms and a molecule of water is released. This condensation reaction strings the amino acids together into a polypeptide chain.

The newly synthesized polypeptide emerges from the ribosome as a straight line, but it does not stay that way for long. Interactions among side chains and with the surrounding water cause the chain to fold. Biologists describe four levels of protein structure. The first, the primary structure, is simply the order of amino acids in the chain, and it determines everything that follows. The second level is the secondary structure, created by hydrogen bonds along the backbone. These bonds can produce right-handed alpha helices, where the chain coils like a spring, or beta sheets, where extended strands lie side by side like a folded sheet of paper. Whether a segment becomes a helix or a sheet depends largely on the size, charge, and polarity of its side chains.

Beyond the local helices and sheets, the entire chain collapses into a unique three-dimensional shape called the tertiary structure. Hydrophobic side chains tend to cluster in the interior, away from water, forming a core that stabilizes the protein. Polar and charged side chains remain on the surface, where they can form hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, or even covalent disulfide bonds between two cysteine residues. Many proteins add a fourth level, the quaternary structure, in which several folded chains fit together; hemoglobin, for example, is built from four chains. These shapes create functional sites—for example, an enzyme’s active pocket, whose precise geometry allows it to bind a specific substrate and accelerate a reaction.

Nucleic acids are built from nucleotides, each composed of three parts: a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base. In DNA the sugar is deoxyribose; in RNA it is ribose, which carries an extra oxygen atom. The bases fall into two families: purines (adenine and guanine) and pyrimidines (cytosine, thymine, and uracil). Nucleotides link together through phosphodiester bonds, forming a sugar-phosphate backbone that is chemically robust.

When many nucleotides join, they create long strands that encode genetic information as sequences of bases. In DNA, two strands run in opposite directions and pair through hydrogen bonds: adenine with thymine, and guanine with cytosine. This complementary pairing produces the iconic double helix, described in 1953 by James Watson and Francis Crick, who relied on X-ray images made in the laboratory of Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins. The helix can unwind, allowing enzymes to read the code during replication or transcription. RNA, by contrast, is usually single-stranded and can fold back on itself, forming hairpins and loops that are essential for its roles in protein synthesis and regulation.

The link between nucleic acids and proteins is illustrated by the lactase gene. The DNA segment for lactase contains a specific order of bases. During transcription, an RNA polymerase copies this order into messenger RNA, swapping thymine for uracil. Transfer RNAs then bring the appropriate amino acids to a ribosome, where the messenger RNA’s codons—triplets of bases—determine the sequence of the growing polypeptide. The resulting chain folds into the functional lactase enzyme. A single base change in the DNA can alter one codon, replace an amino acid, and disrupt the enzyme’s shape. That is what happens in a rare inherited condition called congenital lactase deficiency. The far more common loss of lactase in adulthood works differently: the gene itself is fine, but it is switched down after childhood, a story for the lesson on gene regulation.

Both protein folding and nucleic-acid pairing are governed by the same physical forces—hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, and the hydrophobic effect—that we have seen in earlier lessons on water and carbon chemistry. Recognizing these shared principles prepares you for the next topic: how cells organize these macromolecules into compartments, membranes, and cytoskeletal networks that further influence function.

## Key ideas
- Proteins are chains of twenty standard amino acids linked by peptide bonds; their function depends on folding into secondary (alpha helix, beta sheet) and tertiary structures.
- Side-chain interactions—hydrophobic clustering, hydrogen bonds, ionic bridges, and disulfide links—drive protein folding and create active sites.
- Nucleic acids are chains of nucleotides; DNA’s double helix stores genetic information via complementary base pairing, while RNA can both carry messages and fold into functional shapes.
- The central dogma describes the flow of information: DNA is transcribed into messenger RNA, which ribosomes translate into a polypeptide that folds into a functional protein.
- Mutations that alter nucleotide sequences can change amino-acid sequences, potentially disrupting folding and leading to loss of function.
