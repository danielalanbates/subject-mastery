# Transcription and RNA Processing

Imagine a massive, secure library that contains every blueprint needed to build and run an entire city. The master blueprints are far too precious to leave the vault, so when workers need to construct a building, they make a careful, temporary working copy of a single plan. In cellular biology, that vault is the nucleus, the master blueprints are your double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid, and the temporary working copies are messenger ribonucleic acid molecules. Building those working copies is the core business of transcription, the process by which a cell reads a gene and rewrites its instructions into a mobile format.

In our last lesson, we watched how cells replicate their entire genome before dividing. Transcription is very different because it focuses on a single gene at a time, synthesizing a transcript in the five-prime to three-prime direction. The enzyme that does the heavy lifting here is RNA polymerase, specifically RNA polymerase II in eukaryotic cells. Think of RNA polymerase as a molecular reading machine. It slides along the DNA strand, unzips a small section of the double helix, and builds a single-stranded transcript by matching RNA nucleotides to their complementary partners on the DNA template. Where DNA holds an adenine, the polymerase adds a uracil instead of a thymine, matching the rules of nucleic acid chemistry we explored earlier.

To start this process, the polymerase has to land at the right spot. Every gene is preceded by a specific sequence of DNA called a promoter, which acts like a glowing neon sign that reads, start reading here. Often, this promoter includes a conserved sequence known as the TATA box. Specialized proteins known as general transcription factors, including a large multi-protein complex called TFIID, must first gather at this promoter. They act like an air traffic control crew, guiding RNA polymerase to the exact docking site by recognizing the TATA box and helping the enzyme lock onto the DNA. Once the enzyme is properly seated, it moves down the gene, extending the growing RNA chain letter by letter. In eukaryotic cells, transcription termination is coupled to the cleavage of the transcript downstream of the gene, a process driven by specific signals that lead to the release of the RNA polymerase II enzyme from the DNA template.

If you looked at a freshly minted messenger RNA molecule in a human cell right after it is made, you would quickly realize it is not yet ready to leave the nucleus. Eukaryotic transcripts undergo extensive processing that happens co-transcriptionally, meaning the modifications occur even as the RNA chain is still being synthesized. This cleanup job involves three major steps. First, the cell adds a modified guanine cap to the front end of the RNA molecule, commonly referred to as the five-prime cap. This cap acts like a VIP pass, protecting the transcript from cellular degradation and telling the protein-building machinery later on where to attach.

At the other end of the molecule, the cell adds a long tail made up of dozens of adenine nucleotides, known as a poly-A tail. This tail acts like a stability marker, helping determine how long the messenger RNA will survive in the cytoplasm before it gets broken down. Between these two ends lies the actual coding sequence, but even that sequence is interrupted by noncoding stretches called introns. Introns are like commercial breaks spliced right into a movie script. A remarkable molecular machine called the spliceosome swoops in to cut out the introns and glue the remaining coding pieces, known as exons, tightly together. The spliceosome is a complex molecular assembly made of both proteins and small nuclear ribonucleoproteins, known as snRNPs, with specific components designated as U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6.

This splicing process is a marvel of evolutionary efficiency. Through alternative splicing, a single gene can be edited in different ways, allowing one stretch of DNA to yield several distinct protein variants depending on the needs of the cell. This evolutionary trick vastly expands the functional repertoire of our genome without requiring a massive increase in total gene count. Once the five-prime cap is on, the poly-A tail is attached, and the introns are cleanly removed by the snRNP-containing spliceosome, our mature messenger RNA is fully prepped and ready to pass through the nuclear pores into the cytoplasm. There, it will encounter the cellular machinery that translates its coded message into a functional protein.

## Key ideas
- Transcription is the process in which RNA polymerase reads a gene on a DNA template strand to synthesize a complementary messenger RNA transcript in the five-prime to three-prime direction.
- General transcription factors, including TFIID binding to the TATA box at the promoter, guide RNA polymerase to the correct docking site to initiate transcription.
- Eukaryotic RNA processing occurs co-transcriptionally and includes adding a protective five-prime cap and a poly-A tail to the transcript.
- The spliceosome, composed of proteins and small nuclear ribonucleoproteins including U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6, removes noncoding introns and joins exons, enabling alternative splicing and protein diversity.
