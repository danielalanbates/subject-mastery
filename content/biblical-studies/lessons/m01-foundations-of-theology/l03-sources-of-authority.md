# Scripture, Tradition, Reason and Experience

Imagine you are in a quiet library, a single candle flickering beside an ancient manuscript. A scholar beside you asks, “Which voice in this room is most trustworthy when we seek God?” That question frames today’s lesson. We will explore the four primary sources that Christians have historically called “authorities” for understanding God: Scripture, Tradition, Reason, and Experience. Each functions like a lens, shaping how we see the divine story and how we live it out. Grouping exactly these four together is often called the Wesleyan quadrilateral, a name the Methodist scholar Albert Outler gave in the twentieth century to John Wesley’s way of doing theology, but every tradition uses all four in some measure.

First, what do we mean by “authority”? In this context an authority is a source that a community believes has the right to speak for God or to guide belief and practice. The early church fathers used the Greek word exousia for this power, and the Latin auctoritas later carried the same sense. Scripture, the written word of God, holds a special place because it is believed to be directly inspired. The Nicene Creed, for example, in the wording of the old English Book of Common Prayer, confesses that Christ “was incarnate by the Holy Ghost of the Virgin Mary, and was made man,” a truth drawn from the biblical narrative.

Scripture is the collection of texts that record God’s revelation, and Christians of every tradition call it both the Holy Bible and the Word of God. The canon—the books recognized as authoritative—was discerned over several centuries. One early council, the Council of Carthage in the year three hundred and ninety-seven, listed the twenty-seven books of the New Testament that were already widely used in the West. Catholic theology sees the canon as guided by the Holy Spirit and confirmed by the teaching authority of the Church, the Magisterium. Most Protestant traditions, emphasizing sola scriptura or “Scripture alone,” point to the internal witness of the texts and the consensus of the early church as the basis for the canon.

Next comes Tradition, which is not merely old customs but the living transmission of the faith from the apostles onward. In the Eastern Orthodox tradition, Tradition (with a capital “T”) includes the Scriptures, the decisions of the first seven ecumenical councils, the writings of the Church Fathers, the liturgy, and the ongoing life of the Church. The Orthodox often cite early writings such as the Didache, a short church manual probably from around the year one hundred, as witnesses to how the faith was practiced before the canon was fixed. Catholics similarly hold that Tradition, together with Scripture, forms a “deposit of faith” entrusted to the Church. Protestants, while wary of extra-biblical authority, still value historic creeds—such as the Apostles’ Creed—as faithful summaries of biblical teaching, though they regard those creeds as subordinate to Scripture.

Reason is the third lens, the capacity of the human mind to reflect, argue, and discern truth. The medieval scholastic tradition, especially in the work of Thomas Aquinas, showed how reason can be used to explain and defend the mysteries of faith. Aquinas wrote that some truths about God can be known through natural reason—for example, the existence of a first cause—while other truths, such as the Incarnation, require divine revelation. During the Reformation, both Martin Luther and John Calvin affirmed the role of reason, but they cautioned that it must be submitted to Scripture.

Finally, Experience refers to the personal and communal encounter with God in the world. This includes the inner witness of the Holy Spirit, the transformative power of worship, and the way God shapes history. Pentecostal and Charismatic traditions give particular prominence to experiential knowledge, describing “baptism in the Holy Spirit” as an empowering encounter with God, which they test against Scripture. Even in more liturgical traditions, the experience of the sacraments—baptism, the Eucharist, and so on—is understood as a means by which God communicates grace. Experience does not replace Scripture, but it can confirm or challenge our understanding of it.

How do these four sources interact? Consider the doctrine of the Trinity. Scripture contains verses such as Matthew twenty-eight, nineteen, where Jesus commands baptism, in the words of the King James Version, “in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost.”” Tradition preserves the creedal formulations of Nicaea and Constantinople that articulate this mystery. Reason helps us see that the three persons are distinct yet share one divine essence, a concept the early church defended against heresies like Arianism. Experience, for many believers, is felt in the prayer life that invokes the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together. When one source is over-emphasized or neglected, the whole picture becomes distorted.

Different Christian families prioritize these sources in distinctive ways. A Roman Catholic might say that Scripture is the “Word of God written,” but it must be read within the living Tradition of the Church, guided by the Magisterium of the Pope and bishops. An Anglican could stress a “three-legged stool” of Scripture, Tradition, and Reason, insisting that all three are necessary for a balanced theology. A Baptist might hold Scripture as the sole rule of faith, yet still value the historical experience of the church and the reasoned study of the text. An Orthodox Christian would place liturgy and the continuous life of the Church at the center, seeing Scripture as one part of the broader Tradition. Recognizing these patterns opens the door to respectful dialogue and deeper learning.

## Key ideas
- Scripture is the written record of divine revelation, canonized over centuries, and treated as authoritative across Christian traditions.
- Tradition encompasses the teachings, practices, and creeds handed down from the apostles, with varying weight among Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant families.
- Reason is the disciplined use of human intellect to understand and explain faith, employed from Aquinas to contemporary scholars.
- Experience refers to personal and communal encounters with God, especially through worship, sacraments, and the work of the Holy Spirit.
- Different Christian traditions prioritize these four sources in distinct ways, shaping their doctrines and approaches to theological questions.
