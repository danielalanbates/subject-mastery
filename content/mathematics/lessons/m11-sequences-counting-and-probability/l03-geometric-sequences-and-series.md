# Geometric Sequences and Series

Imagine a bouncing ball that climbs back up to half of the height from which it fell on every single bounce. If it drops from a height of eight feet, it reaches four feet on the first rebound, two feet on the second, and one foot on the third. This pattern of multiplying by a fixed factor at each step is entirely different from the steady addition we explored in our last lesson on arithmetic sequences. Instead of adding a common difference, we multiply by a common ratio. In mathematics, an ordered list of numbers where each term after the first is found by multiplying the previous term by a fixed, non-zero number is called a geometric sequence.

Let us look at the numbers themselves. Suppose our sequence begins with three, and our constant multiplier, or common ratio, is two. The first term is three. To find the second term, we multiply three by two, yielding six. For the third term, we multiply six by two, giving twelve. The sequence runs three, six, twelve, twenty-four, and so forth. Notice how the exponent on that multiplier grows with each step. To get from the first term to the second, we multiplied by two once. To get to the third term, we multiplied by two twice, which is multiplying by four.

This leads directly to a general formula for finding any term in a geometric sequence. If we call the first term by the letter b, and the common ratio by the letter r, then the first term is simply b. The second term is b times r. The third term is b times r squared. Following this steady growth, the nth term, which we can call the term at step n, is equal to b times r raised to the power of n minus one. Let us test this rule on our sequence of three, six, twelve, twenty-four. If we want the fourth term, we take our first term of three, and multiply it by the common ratio of two raised to the power of four minus one, which is two cubed. Two cubed is eight, and three times eight is twenty-four, exactly matching our list.

When we add the terms of a sequence together, we form a series. If we add a finite number of terms from a geometric sequence, we get a finite geometric series. Computing this sum by hand for many terms would be tedious, but algebra gives us a wonderfully efficient shortcut. The sum of the first n terms equals the first term b multiplied by the quantity one minus the common ratio raised to the power of n, all divided by the quantity one minus the common ratio, provided that the common ratio is not equal to one. Let us find the sum of the first four terms of our sequence of three, six, twelve, and twenty-four. We take the first term of three, multiply it by the quantity one minus two raised to the fourth power, and divide by the quantity one minus two. Two to the fourth power is sixteen, so the numerator has three times the quantity one minus sixteen, which is three times negative fifteen, or negative forty-five. The denominator is one minus two, which is negative one. Dividing negative forty-five by negative one gives forty-five. If we add three, six, twelve, and twenty-four manually, nine plus twelve is twenty-one, plus twenty-four is forty-five. The formula works beautifully.

What happens if we keep adding terms forever, creating an infinite geometric series? Remarkably, even though we are adding infinitely many numbers, the total sum can sometimes be a finite number. This happens when the absolute value of the common ratio is less than one, meaning the terms are shrinking rapidly toward zero. For instance, if our sequence starts with one and has a common ratio of one-half, our series is one plus one-half plus one-fourth plus one-eighth, and so on. The running totals are one, one and a half, one and three-quarters, one and seven-eighths, each falling short of two by exactly the last term added, so the total creeps closer and closer to two without ever exceeding it. The sum of an infinite geometric series is elegantly given by taking the first term and dividing it by the quantity one minus the common ratio. For our example, one divided by the quantity one minus one-half gives one divided by one-half, which equals two. If the common ratio is one or greater in magnitude, the terms do not shrink toward zero, the running total never settles on a single number, and the infinite series diverges.

Understanding how these terms multiply and accumulate gives us powerful tools for modeling exponential behavior. As we move forward, we will shift our focus from sequences of numbers to the fundamental rules of counting, learning how to systematically arrange and select items without having to list every single possibility by hand.

## Key ideas
- A geometric sequence is a list of numbers where each term after the first is generated by multiplying the preceding term by a constant non-zero common ratio.
- The nth term of a geometric sequence can be found by multiplying the first term by the common ratio raised to the power of n minus one.
- The sum of a finite geometric series is calculated using the first term, the common ratio, and the total number of terms being added.
- An infinite geometric series converges to a finite sum if and only if the absolute value of its common ratio is strictly less than one.
