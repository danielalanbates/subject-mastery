# Churches Under Nazism and Communism

In the quiet of a German study during the mid-nineteen thirties, a pastor might have faced a harrowing choice: swear an oath of allegiance to a dictator who demanded total control, or face the loss of his pulpit and perhaps his life. This was the dark reality for many Christians as the twentieth century descended into the chaos of totalitarianism. In our previous lesson, we explored how the church navigated the tensions between traditional faith and modern cultural movements. Now, we must look at how those same churches faced the brutal reality of states that sought to replace God with the ideology of the party or the race.

When the Nazi party rose to power in Germany in 1933, they did not immediately seek to abolish the church. Instead, they attempted to co-opt it. That same year, the Vatican signed a treaty with the Nazi government known as the Reichskonkordat. The church hoped this agreement would protect its institutional rights and schools. However, the Nazis soon violated the spirit of the treaty, seeking to subordinate all aspects of German life to the state. A movement known as the German Christians sought to align church life with Nazi racial ideology, even suggesting that the Old Testament should be discarded because of its Jewish roots. They viewed the German nation as a divine entity that demanded absolute loyalty. This was a direct challenge to the fundamental Christian belief that our primary allegiance belongs to the kingdom of God, not to any earthly regime.

In response, a group of pastors and theologians formed the Confessing Church. They refused to let the state dictate the terms of their faith. In 1934, they gathered at Barmen to issue a declaration of defiance. The Barmen Declaration asserted that the church belongs only to Jesus Christ and that no other power could claim authority over the soul. Among the leading voices in this movement was Dietrich Bonhoeffer. He argued that true discipleship required standing against the evils of the state, even if it meant participating in secret plots to end the regime. Bonhoeffer eventually paid for his witness with his life, executed in a concentration camp just days before the end of the war.

The role of the Roman Catholic Church during this era remains a subject of intense historical debate. By 1937, Pope Pius the Eleventh realized that the Nazi regime was fundamentally hostile to the faith. He issued the encyclical Mit brennender Sorge, which means With Deep Anxiety. In this document, he condemned Nazi ideology, specifically its worship of race and the state, and denounced the regime’s violations of the concordat. His successor, Pope Pius the Twelfth, who led the church throughout the war, has been criticized by some for not speaking out more forcefully against the Holocaust. Others argue that he chose a path of quiet diplomacy to protect Catholics and to facilitate the rescue of Jewish people through covert networks. This debate highlights the agonizing difficulty of leadership under a regime that responds to public criticism with systematic extermination. Historians continue to sift through the archives, weighing the impact of public statements against the risk of further state-sponsored violence.

While the Nazis sought to control the church, the Soviet Union under communism took a different approach, seeking to eliminate religion entirely. Following the Russian Revolution in 1917, the state launched an aggressive campaign of atheism. Churches were closed, turned into museums, or destroyed. Thousands of priests, bishops, and laypeople were executed or sent to labor camps in Siberia. The Orthodox Church was forced into a precarious existence, often heavily infiltrated by state agents. Despite this, a vibrant, hidden life of faith persisted among the common people, who continued to baptize their children and pray in secret.

In both Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, believers were forced to decide what it meant to be a Christian when the state demanded their total devotion. Some compromised, hoping to save their institutions, while others suffered imprisonment or death to maintain their integrity. These experiences tested the very foundations of Christian ethics, forcing believers to distinguish between the legitimate role of government and the reach of an idolatrous state. It was a period that proved that the church is often most authentic when it is stripped of its worldly power and forced to rely solely on its confession of Christ.

## Key ideas

- The 1933 Reichskonkordat attempted to secure Catholic institutional rights, but the subsequent 1937 encyclical Mit brennender Sorge represented a formal papal condemnation of Nazi racial and state ideology.
- The Confessing Church and the Barmen Declaration stood against the German Christians movement by asserting that the church's sole authority is Jesus Christ rather than the state.
- Dietrich Bonhoeffer remains a primary example of sacrificial resistance, arguing that true discipleship requires opposing state evil even at the cost of one's life.
- The Soviet Union pursued a policy of state-sponsored atheism that resulted in the systematic destruction of church property and the widespread persecution of clergy and laity.
- The struggle to maintain integrity under totalitarian pressure forced Christians to grapple with the nature of their witness and the necessity of resisting state-mandated idolatry.
