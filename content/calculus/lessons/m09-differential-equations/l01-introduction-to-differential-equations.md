# Introduction to Differential Equations

Think about a car speeding down a highway. You know its velocity at any given moment, or perhaps you know how hard the driver is pressing the accelerator, which gives you its acceleration. But what you really want to know for your trip is position. You want a function that tells you exactly where the car is at any time. Moving backward from the rate of change of a quantity to the quantity itself is the master theme of calculus. In our last module, you mastered evaluating improper integrals, pushing the bounds of accumulation to infinity. Now, we take the next great leap by asking what happens when the rate of change of a function depends not just on time, but on the function's own current value.

This brings us to differential equations. A differential equation is simply an equation that contains one or more derivatives. While an ordinary algebraic equation asks you to find an unknown number, a differential equation asks you to find an unknown function. When you solved basic antiderivative problems earlier in your studies, you were looking for a function whose derivative was equal to a given expression involving just the independent variable. But true differential equations are far richer. They mix the function itself and its derivatives together in a single statement, creating a dynamic relationship that governs everything from cooling coffee to radioactive decay and population growth.

Before we dive deeper, we must distinguish between ordinary differential equations, which involve functions of a single independent variable and ordinary derivatives, and partial differential equations, which involve functions of multiple variables and their partial derivatives. Throughout this course, our focus rests entirely on ordinary differential equations. As we study these equations, we stand upon the shoulders of giants. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently founded calculus in the seventeenth century, a triumph that was overshadowed by a bitter and protracted priority dispute over who deserved the credit first. Each mathematician also introduced distinct notation that shaped how we write mathematics today. Leibniz gave us the differential notation d y over d x, which means the derivative of y with respect to x, while Newton used a system of dots placed above variables to denote time derivatives.

To understand differential equations deeply, let us look at what it means to be a solution. Unlike a standard equation whose solution is a fixed number like three, the solution to a differential equation is an entire function. Suppose we have a differential equation where the derivative of y with respect to x is equal to two times y. How do we verify whether a proposed function is a true solution? We test it by substitution, just as you have done with algebraic equations since your earliest math classes.

Let us take the function y equals three times e to the power of two x. We want to check if this function solves our differential equation. First, we find the derivative of y with respect to x. Using the chain rule for exponential functions, the derivative of three times e to the power of two x is six times e to the power of two x. Next, we look at the right side of our differential equation, which is two times y. Since y is three times e to the power of two x, multiplying two by y gives us two times three times e to the power of two x, which also simplifies to six times e to the power of two x. Because the derivative we calculated matches the right side of the equation, our proposed function is indeed a valid solution.

Notice something fascinating about this process. If we had started with any constant multiplier instead of three, say a constant c, our function would be y equals c times e to the power of two x. Its derivative would be two times c times e to the power of two x, which is still two times y. This means our differential equation does not have just one single solution. It has an entire family of solutions, infinite in number, parameterized by that constant c.

In the physical world, quantities do not float around with arbitrary constants attached forever. A specific physical system always starts somewhere. This brings us to initial value problems. An initial value problem consists of a differential equation paired with a specific condition, known as the initial condition, which gives the value of the unknown function at a particular starting point. These conditions act like a key that locks in the value of our constant c, turning a general family of solutions into one unique, predictable curve. If we know that our population or temperature or velocity was a specific amount at time zero, we substitute that starting point into our general solution to solve for c. For example, if y equals three when x is zero, then c times e to the power zero, which is just c, must equal three, giving back our earlier solution, three times e to the power of two x.

Verifying an initial value problem means checking two things. First, you must show that your function satisfies the differential equation itself. Second, you must show that your function satisfies the initial condition when you plug in the starting value of the independent variable. By walking through these steps, you bridge the gap between abstract calculus and the concrete modeling of reality.

## Key ideas
- A differential equation is an equation containing an unknown function and one or more of its derivatives, distinguishing ordinary differential equations from partial ones by the presence of single versus multiple independent variables.
- Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed calculus independently, sparking a bitter priority dispute while introducing enduring notations such as Leibniz's differential symbol d y over d x.
- The solution to a differential equation is not a single number, but an entire function or family of functions.
- Verifying a solution requires taking the derivative of the proposed function and substituting both it and the original function back into the differential equation to check for equality.
- An initial value problem pairs a differential equation with a specific starting condition, which allows us to find a unique solution by determining the value of any arbitrary constants.
