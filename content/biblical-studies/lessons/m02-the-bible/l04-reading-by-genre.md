# Reading the Bible by Genre

Imagine opening a book and finding the first chapter written as a legal contract, the second as a lyric poem, and the third as a piece of investigative journalism. If you read the legal contract as poetry, or the poetry as a tax audit, you would completely miss the author's intent. The Bible presents a similar challenge on a grand scale. In our last lesson, we traced how the English Bible came to be translated from ancient manuscripts. Now, we turn to how we actually read those texts by looking at their literary genres, which are the different categories and styles of writing that make up the scriptural library.

The word genre simply means a type or kind of literature. The Bible is not a single, uniform book. Instead, it is an anthology of distinct documents composed over more than a millennium by dozens of human authors. The Protestant canon includes sixty-six books, Catholic Bibles contain seventy-three, Eastern Orthodox Bibles a few more, and the Ethiopian Orthodox canon as many as eighty-one, reflecting ancient differences in the traditional collection of scripture. Across these texts, authors used a rich variety of literary forms, including historical narratives, legal codes, songs of lament, royal court records, apocalyptic visions, and personal letters. Recognizing these genres is not an academic luxury. It is the foundation of responsible interpretation, because an author's rules of communication depend entirely on the genre they choose to use.

Take, for example, the Book of Psalms. When the Psalmist writes in Psalm twenty-three, in the King James Version, "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want," we are reading Hebrew poetry. Poetry relies heavily on vivid imagery, emotional resonance, and structural parallelism rather than flat, prose-based reporting. If we forced a modern scientific literalism onto a poem, we would misunderstand its purpose. The poet is not offering a botanical description of grass, but an emotional and theological expression of trust in divine care. Reading poetry as poetry means letting metaphor function as metaphor, while still recognizing that it communicates profound truth about God and human experience.

Consider another major genre: narrative. Large portions of the Old Testament and the Gospels in the New Testament are told as stories. Within the Old Testament, these narratives flow alongside other literary forms, such as the legal and ritual codes found in Leviticus and parts of Numbers and Deuteronomy. Biblical narrative itself is often notoriously subtle. Authors frequently tell us what people did without explicitly telling us whether God approved of their actions. For instance, when a patriarch makes a questionable moral choice, the text often records the event without a direct editorial comment. Interpreting narrative requires us to watch how the overarching plot unfolds rather than treating every narrative description as a direct prescription for our own behavior. The story shows us human brokenness and divine grace playing out over centuries.

Law is another foundational genre, particularly within the first five books of the Bible. These legal materials, given the ancient covenant context of Israel, combined moral commands, civil regulations, and ritual instructions that shaped the identity of a unique community. Wisdom literature, found in books like Proverbs, Job, and Ecclesiastes, offers practical observations for daily living, often exploring the deep mysteries of suffering and the fear of the Lord through sayings and reflective essays.

Then we encounter prophetic and apocalyptic literature, found in books like Isaiah, Daniel, and Revelation. Prophecy often calls God's people back to covenant faithfulness, while apocalyptic writing uses intensely visual, symbolic imagery, strange beasts, and cosmic numbers to make theological claims about history and ultimate reality. Interpreters read these books in different ways. Some see Revelation as mainly about the first-century Roman world; others, including many evangelical Protestants, expect much of it to be fulfilled in the future; still others read its images as timeless pictures of the struggle between good and evil. Most scholars agree that, whatever else it does, apocalyptic language was meant to encourage persecuted believers by assuring them that God, not earthly empires, holds the final victory, and that its symbols must first be read in their ancient setting.

Epistles, or letters, form yet another distinct category. Written primarily by Paul, Peter, and other apostles, these documents were occasional letters addressed to specific first-century communities facing particular crises. When reading an epistle, we are essentially listening to one side of an ancient conversation. We have to reconstruct the historical and cultural context of the original recipients to understand why the author gave specific instructions about church order, theology, or daily ethics.

Ultimately, paying attention to genre protects us from misapplying scripture while opening our eyes to its literary brilliance. When we respect the literary shape of each biblical book, we honor both the divine inspiration behind the text and the human authorial choices that brought it to life. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will explore how historical and cultural context deepens this interpretive journey even further.

## Key ideas
- The Bible is an anthology of diverse literary genres, encompassing varying book counts across Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox traditions, and requiring readers to adapt their interpretive approach to each specific writing style.
- Major genre categories include law, narrative, wisdom, poetry, prophecy, and letters, each governed by its own rules of communication and authorial intent.
- Poetry and apocalyptic literature use vivid imagery and symbolism that must be understood according to their literary conventions rather than strict modern literalism.
- Biblical narratives tell stories of human history and divine interaction, often requiring readers to observe overarching plots rather than treating every narrative description as a moral prescription.
- Epistles are occasional letters addressed to specific historical communities, demanding careful attention to the original context and purpose of the communication.
