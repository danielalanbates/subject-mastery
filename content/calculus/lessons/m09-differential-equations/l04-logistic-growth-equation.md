# The Logistic Growth Equation

Imagine releasing a pair of rabbits into a remote island meadow with lush grass and no predators. At first, the population multiplies rapidly, doubling over and over. If we model this with exponential growth, the population shoots upward toward infinity. But we know reality steps in. Eventually, the rabbits crowd the meadow, the grass grows sparse, and disease spreads. The population slows down and levels off. In our previous work with separable differential equations, we looked at unrestricted growth where the rate of change is simply proportional to the population size itself. Today, we refine that model to account for a hard limit, a ceiling known as carrying capacity, which governs real-world populations from bacteria in a petri dish to fish in a lake.

To capture this ceiling mathematically, we introduce the logistic differential equation. Let p represent the population at any time t. Let k be the positive growth rate constant, and let capital m represent the carrying capacity, which is the maximum sustainable population the environment can support. The logistic model states that the derivative of p with respect to t equals k times p, multiplied by the quantity one minus p divided by m. Let us examine why this formula makes intuitive sense. When the population p is very small compared to the carrying capacity m, the fraction p over m is close to zero. That makes the quantity one minus p over m close to one. In this early phase, when the population is much smaller than the carrying capacity, the equation behaves almost exactly like unrestricted exponential growth, where the rate of growth is rapid and proportional to the current population.

As the population p grows larger, the growth rate accelerates until the population reaches half of the carrying capacity. At that midpoint, the growth rate reaches its maximal value. As the population continues to approach the carrying capacity m, the fraction p over m gets closer to one. Consequently, the quantity one minus p over m shrinks toward zero. This factor acts as a brake on the system. When the population finally reaches the carrying capacity m, the fraction becomes one, one minus one is zero, and the derivative of p with respect to t becomes zero. The population growth halts completely, creating a stable equilibrium level at p equal to m. By contrast, the equilibrium solution p equal to zero representing extinction is unstable, because any small introduction of population will cause growth away from zero. If the population somehow started above the carrying capacity m, the fraction p over m would exceed one, making the quantity one minus p over m negative. That would force the derivative to be negative, causing the population to decline back down toward the carrying capacity.

Let us walk through a concrete example of this behavior. Suppose a wildlife reserve has a carrying capacity of one thousand deer, represented by m equal to one thousand. The initial population at time zero is one hundred deer, which we call p sub zero. Let the growth constant k be two tenths. We want to find the differential equation modeling this population and understand its early rate of change. Substituting our values into the logistic formula, the derivative of p with respect to t equals two tenths times p, multiplied by the quantity one minus p divided by one thousand.

At the very beginning, when the population p is one hundred, we substitute one hundred into the right side. The expression becomes two tenths times one hundred, which is twenty, multiplied by the quantity one minus one hundred divided by one thousand. One hundred divided by one thousand is one tenth, so one minus one tenth is nine tenths. Multiplying twenty by nine tenths gives eighteen. Therefore, the initial rate of change is eighteen deer per year. The population is growing, but at a controlled pace.

If we integrate this separable differential equation using the method of partial fractions from our earlier techniques, we arrive at the explicit solution formula for the logistic equation, which states that p of t equals capital m divided by a denominator. That denominator is one plus a constant times e to the power negative k t, where the constant is capital m minus p sub zero, all divided by p sub zero. For our deer, the constant is nine hundred divided by one hundred, which is nine, so the population is one thousand divided by the quantity one plus nine times e to the power negative two tenths t. The resulting curve has a distinct shape called a sigmoidal or s-shaped curve. It begins with slow growth, accelerates until it reaches half of the carrying capacity, and then bends over, asymptotically approaching the carrying capacity line as time moves forward. Understanding how to manipulate these equations prepares us to tackle more complex differential models involving external influences and linear systems.

## Key ideas
- Unrestricted exponential growth fails in natural settings because environments have finite resources and hard limits.
- The carrying capacity represents the maximum sustainable population that an environment can support over time.
- The logistic differential equation multiplies the standard proportional growth rate by a dampening factor of one minus the population divided by the carrying capacity.
- The phase-line analysis reveals an unstable equilibrium at zero extinction and a stable equilibrium at the carrying capacity.
- Solutions to the logistic equation follow an explicit formula and form an s-shaped curve that transitions from acceleration to a steady plateau.
