# Classifying Matter

Imagine you are holding a cold glass of lemonade. You see ice cubes floating, liquid swirling, and perhaps a few lemon pulp particles suspended in the drink. To a chemist, this simple glass is a playground of classification. Everything in the universe that has mass and occupies space is matter, but matter is not a monolith. It exists in different states and displays different compositions. By learning to categorize what we see, we move beyond mere observation and begin to understand the fundamental architecture of the physical world.

We typically categorize matter by its state: solid, liquid, or gas. In a solid, particles are packed tightly in a fixed arrangement, giving the substance a definite shape and volume. In a liquid, the particles are still close together but can slide past one another, meaning the substance takes the shape of its container while maintaining a constant volume. Gases are far more energetic, with particles moving rapidly and spreading out to fill whatever container they occupy. These states are not permanent, as energy changes can shift matter from one form to another.

Beyond its state, we classify matter based on its chemical makeup. If you look at a pure gold ring, you are looking at an element. An element is a substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. Each element is defined by its unique atoms. We find these elements organized in the periodic table, where their positions reveal patterns in their behavior. Elements in the same vertical column, or group, often share similar chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons in their outermost shells.

When two or more elements combine in a fixed, definite ratio, we get a compound. Water is a classic example, consisting of two hydrogen atoms for every one oxygen atom. Unlike a simple mixture, a compound has properties that are entirely different from the elements that compose it. Hydrogen is a flammable gas and oxygen supports combustion, yet their compound, water, is the very substance we use to extinguish fires.

Most things we encounter are not pure substances but mixtures. A mixture is a combination of two or more substances where each retains its individual chemical identity. If you stir salt into water, you create a homogeneous mixture, or a solution, where the composition is uniform throughout. If you add sand to that same water, you create a heterogeneous mixture, where you can clearly see the different components. The beauty of a mixture is that it can be separated back into its original parts using physical methods like filtration or evaporation, because no chemical bonds were formed or broken to create it.

To understand these substances, chemists look at their properties and changes. A physical property is something you can measure or observe without changing the identity of the substance. You might observe the color of a substance or measure its density. You could also determine its melting point or its boiling point. If you melt that ice cube in your lemonade, you have undergone a physical change. The ice has changed its state from solid to liquid, but it is still just water. The chemical identity remains identical before and after the transition.

In contrast, a chemical property describes a substance's ability to undergo a specific chemical change. Flammability, toxicity, and acidity are chemical properties. A chemical change, or chemical reaction, occurs when a substance is converted into an entirely new substance with a different chemical composition. If you leave an iron nail outside, it reacts with oxygen in the air to form iron oxide, or rust. This is not just a change in appearance; the iron has chemically transformed into a new material. You cannot simply reverse this change by cooling it down or filtering it.

Let us look at a quick example to sharpen your intuition. If you take a piece of paper and tear it into small pieces, you have performed a physical change because the paper is still paper. If you take that same paper and set it on fire, you have initiated a chemical change. The cellulose in the paper reacts with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide, water, and ash. The original paper is gone, replaced by new substances. Distinguishing between these categories is the first step in predicting how matter behaves, which is essential for the precise measurements and numerical calculations we will tackle in our next lesson.

## Key ideas

- Matter is classified by its physical state as solid, liquid, or gas, depending on the arrangement and energy of its particles.
- Pure substances consist of elements, which are organized by periodic patterns of behavior, or compounds formed by the chemical combination of elements in fixed ratios.
- Mixtures are combinations of substances that retain their individual identities and can be separated by physical methods.
- Physical properties and changes relate to the state or appearance of matter, while chemical properties and changes involve the transformation of matter into entirely new substances.
