# Gilded Age Politics and Populism

Imagine a political system where the person you vote for is less important than the rewards you receive for supporting the winning party. This was the reality of the Gilded Age, the period spanning from the late 1860s to the 1890s. While our previous lesson explored the rapid growth of cities and the influx of immigrants, this lesson focuses on the machinery of government that struggled to manage these changes. Politics in this era was often more about patronage—the practice of rewarding political supporters with government jobs—than about addressing the deep economic anxieties of a nation shifting from farms to factories.

In this era, the two major political parties, the Republicans and the Democrats, were remarkably evenly matched. Because presidential elections were often decided by razor-thin margins in just a few swing states, party loyalty became intense. This was the height of the spoils system. When a new administration took office, it would fire thousands of federal employees to make room for party loyalists. This created a bloated, often inefficient bureaucracy. However, the assassination of President James Garfield in 1881 by a disgruntled office-seeker shocked the nation into action. It led to the Pendleton Civil Service Act of 1883, which required that many government jobs be filled through competitive exams rather than political connections. This was a critical first step toward a professional, merit-based federal government.

While politicians in Washington debated civil service reform, millions of Americans were struggling with a very different reality. Farmers in the South and the Great Plains faced a brutal economic squeeze. As global production increased, the prices for crops like wheat and cotton plummeted. Farmers were burdened by high railroad shipping rates and mounting debt. They felt the federal government favored big business and Eastern banking interests. To organize, farmers formed the Farmers' Alliances. These included the Northern Alliance, the Southern Alliance, and the Colored Farmers' Alliance. These organizations provided a foundation for the Populist Party, or the People's Party. Notably, the Colored Farmers' Alliance organized thousands of Black farmers, and in some parts of the South, Populists attempted biracial fusion politics. These efforts were eventually crushed by white-supremacy campaigns that used racial violence and disenfranchisement to divide the movement.

The Populists proposed the Subtreasury Plan, a central idea that was arguably more radical than their demand for silver. They wanted the federal government to establish warehouses where farmers could store nonperishable crops and receive low-interest government loans. This would allow farmers to hold their crops until prices rose, rather than selling immediately under duress.

The economic crisis intensified with the Panic of 1893. A massive financial collapse triggered a deep, multiyear depression. Unemployment soared, and labor unrest grew, setting the stage for the pivotal election of 1896. By then, the Populists were demanding a graduated income tax, government ownership of railroads, and the direct election of United States senators. They also championed the free coinage of silver to increase the money supply and provide relief to debtors.

The election of 1896 pitted the Democrat William Jennings Bryan against the Republican William McKinley. Bryan captured the Populist spirit with his famous Cross of Gold speech, where he argued that the nation should not be crucified upon a cross of gold. The Populists chose to endorse Bryan, effectively merging their movement into the Democratic Party. Opposing him, McKinley ran a sophisticated, well-funded campaign that appealed to urban workers and business owners who feared that inflation would destroy their savings. McKinley’s victory marked the decline of the Populist movement as a major political force. Yet, the Populist legacy persisted. Many of their core demands, including the income tax and the direct election of senators, were adopted by later reformers. The failure of the Populist movement did not mean the end of the public's desire for change; instead, it set the stage for a new period of political activism as the nation entered the twentieth century.

## Key ideas

- The Gilded Age was characterized by the spoils system, which prioritized political patronage until the Pendleton Act introduced merit-based hiring for federal jobs.
- Farmers' Alliances, including the Northern, Southern, and Colored Farmers' Alliances, organized rural Americans and laid the groundwork for the Populist Party.
- Populists proposed the Subtreasury Plan to provide federal crop-storage loans, alongside demands for silver coinage, a graduated income tax, and the direct election of senators.
- The Panic of 1893 intensified economic hardship, fueling the Populist-Democratic fusion in the 1896 election, which William McKinley won by appealing to urban and business interests.
- While the Populist movement collapsed after 1896, its platform influenced the subsequent Progressive Era reforms.
