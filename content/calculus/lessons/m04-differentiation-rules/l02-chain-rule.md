# The Chain Rule

Imagine watching a mechanical clock through a glass case. When one hidden gear turns by a fraction of a degree, it turns a second gear. That gear drives a larger wheel, which finally moves the minute hand. Each movement causes another, compounding the motion outward. In calculus, when we want to find the rate of change of a function that is nested inside another function, we encounter this exact mechanical nesting. Last time, we learned how to differentiate basic trigonometric functions like sine and cosine. But what happens when we need the derivative of the sine of the quantity three x, where an algebraic expression is tucked right inside the trigonometric function? To solve this, we need the chain rule, one of the most powerful tools in all of differentiation.

The historical development of calculus involved two brilliant minds, Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. Their historic priority dispute over who invented calculus first was famously bitter and lasted for decades. Each man introduced his own unique notation. Newton used dot notation, placing a small dot above a variable to represent its time derivative. Leibniz introduced the differential notation, writing expressions like d y over d x, which suggests that derivatives behave somewhat like fractions. Leibniz’s d-notation makes the chain rule particularly intuitive and easy to remember.

A composite function is built by taking one function and plugging it into the interior of another function. Think of it as a set of Russian nesting dolls. You have an outer function and an inner function. If we call the inner function g of x and the outer function f, the composite function evaluates f at the output of g of x. The chain rule states that the derivative of the composite function f of g of x equals f prime of g of x times g prime of x. This rule applies to any differentiable outer and inner functions, whether they are polynomials, trigonometric functions, or exponential expressions.

The intuition behind the chain rule rests on scaling rates of change and limits. If a small change in x produces a proportional change in the inner function, and that change in turn produces a proportional change in the outer function, the total magnification is the product of the two individual scaling factors. In mathematical terms, as the inner change approaches zero, the ratio of the change in the outer function to the change in x is the product of the two separate difference quotients, which translates directly to the product of their derivatives in the limit.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Suppose we want to find the derivative with respect to x of the quantity three x plus two, all raised to the fourth power. Here, our inner function is three x plus two. Our outer function is something to the fourth power. To apply the chain rule, we first differentiate the outer function while leaving the inner function completely alone. The derivative of something to the fourth power is four times that same thing cubed. So we write down four times the quantity three x plus two, all cubed. Next, we multiply this result by the derivative of the inner function, which is three. Multiplying the four by the three gives twelve. Thus, the derivative of the quantity three x plus two, all raised to the fourth power, is twelve times the quantity three x plus two, all cubed.

Let us try a second example involving an exponential function. Suppose we want to find the derivative with respect to x of the natural exponential of the quantity two x squared. The inner function is two x squared, and the outer function is the natural exponential function. First, we take the derivative of the outer function, keeping the inner function unchanged. The derivative of the natural exponential function is itself, a fact we will explain two lessons from now, so we write the natural exponential of the quantity two x squared. Then, we multiply by the derivative of the inner function, which is four x. Putting it all together, the derivative is four x times the natural exponential of the quantity two x squared.

Sometimes functions feature multiple layers of composition, requiring us to apply the chain rule repeatedly. If we have a function nested inside a second function, which is itself nested inside a third function, we simply peel the layers back one by one. We take the derivative of the outermost layer evaluated at the inner layers, and then multiply by the derivative of the middle layer, and finally multiply by the derivative of the innermost layer. Each successive gear in the mechanism contributes its own multiplicative rate of change to the final result.

## Key ideas
- The chain rule states that the derivative of the composite function f of g of x equals f prime of g of x times g prime of x.
- Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz engaged in a bitter priority dispute over calculus, introducing dot notation and differential notation respectively.
- The rule works because the rate of change of a nested function is the product of the rate of change of the outer function and the rate of change of the inner function.
- The chain rule applies to any differentiable functions, including polynomials, trigonometric expressions, and exponential functions, and can be applied repeatedly for multiple nested layers.
