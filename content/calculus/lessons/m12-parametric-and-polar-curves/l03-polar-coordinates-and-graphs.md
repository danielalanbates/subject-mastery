# Polar Coordinates and Graphs

Imagine you are standing at the center of a wide-open lawn, holding a laser pointer and a long measuring tape. Instead of describing your position by walking a certain number of paces east and a certain number of paces north, you decide on a different strategy. You turn toward a landmark, pivot by a specific angle, and stretch the tape straight out to a given distance. This simple shift from a rectangular grid to a system of angles and radial distances unlocks a completely new geometric perspective. In our last lesson, we traced motion using parametric equations, separating horizontal and vertical tracks into functions of time. Today, we leave behind the familiar x and y axes of Cartesian coordinates and step into the polar coordinate system, where every point in the plane is defined by its distance from the origin and its rotation around it.

To build this system mathematically, we place a fixed point called the pole, which corresponds to the origin in Cartesian coordinates. From the pole, we draw a horizontal ray pointing to the right, known as the polar axis, which plays the role of the positive x axis. Any point in the plane is then located by two numbers. The first number is r, representing the radial distance from the pole to the point. The second number is theta, representing the directed angle measured from the polar axis to the line connecting the pole to the point. Angles are measured in radians, with positive rotations moving counterclockwise. For example, the pair consisting of the distance two and the angle zero describes a point two units directly to the right of the pole along the polar axis.

One fascinating quirk of polar coordinates is that a single geometric point does not have a unique address. In the Cartesian plane, an ordered pair of numbers maps to exactly one location. In the polar plane, however, we can represent the same point in multiple ways. If we take the radial distance as negative, the point is reflected directly through the pole by one hundred eighty degrees, or pi radians. Furthermore, angles wrap around in full cycles of two pi. Adding or subtracting any integer multiple of two pi to the angle theta gives us the exact same terminal direction. This means the point with a radial distance of three and an angle of pi over two can also be written with a negative radial distance of negative three and an angle of three pi over two.

Transitioning between polar and Cartesian coordinates relies on basic trigonometry. Picture a right triangle formed by dropping a vertical line from our polar point down to the horizontal axis. The adjacent side has length x, the opposite side has length y, and the hypotenuse is the radial distance r. Using the definitions of sine and cosine, the horizontal coordinate x equals r times the cosine of theta, and the vertical coordinate y equals r times the sine of theta. Going the other way, the square of the radial distance r is equal to the sum of x squared plus y squared, and the tangent of theta is equal to y divided by x. These conversion rules allow us to translate equations freely between the two systems.

When sketching and analyzing polar curves, symmetry tests provide powerful shortcuts. To test for symmetry about the polar axis, we replace the angle theta with its negative, and if the equation remains unchanged, the graph is symmetric across the horizontal axis. To test for symmetry about the vertical line where theta equals pi over two, we replace the angle theta with pi minus theta, or simultaneously replace the radial distance with its negative and the angle with its negative. To test for symmetry about the pole itself, we replace the radial distance with its negative, or replace the angle theta with theta plus pi. Recognizing these symmetries helps us predict the behavior of the curve before plotting every point.

Let us trace a simple polar graph to see how these relationships shape curves in words. Consider the polar equation where the radial distance r is a constant number, say four. As the angle theta sweeps through all possible values from zero to two pi, the distance from the pole remains fixed at four. The resulting graph is a circle centered at the pole with a radius of four. Now, consider a different equation where the radial distance r is equal to the angle theta, for values of theta greater than or equal to zero. As the angle grows, the distance from the pole grows at the exact same rate. This describes a spiral that unwinds outward from the pole, looping around infinitely as the angle increases.

Polar graphs can also produce symmetric flower-like shapes known as rose curves, or cardioids that resemble the outline of a heart. For instance, the equation where the radial distance r equals three times the cosine of two theta creates a four-petaled rose. As the angle theta varies, the cosine term oscillates between negative one and one, causing the curve to stretch out and pull back toward the pole four times in a single rotation. Describing these graphs in words requires us to track how the radial distance responds dynamically as the angle rotates around the pole. In our next lesson, we will build upon these descriptions to calculate the area enclosed by polar curves using definite integrals.

## Key ideas
- The polar coordinate system locates points using a radial distance from the pole and a directed angle measured from the polar axis.
- Unlike Cartesian coordinates, polar coordinates allow multiple address pairs to represent the exact same geometric point.
- Equations relating polar and Cartesian coordinates use the sine and cosine functions alongside the relation stating that the square of the radial distance equals the sum of x squared plus y squared.
- Symmetry tests concerning the polar axis, the line theta equals pi over two, and the pole help us analyze and sketch polar graphs efficiently.
- Polar graphs such as circles, spirals, and rose curves are generated by describing how the radial distance changes as the angle rotates.
