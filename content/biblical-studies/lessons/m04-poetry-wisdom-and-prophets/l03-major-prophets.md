# The Major Prophets

Imagine looking at a magnificent building that has been reduced to smoldering rubble, and realizing with sudden, crushing clarity that you and your neighbors brought the hammers down yourselves. That was the stark reality facing ancient Israel on the brink of exile. In our last lesson, we explored how the poets and wisdom writers of Israel grappled with human suffering, practical ethics, and the meaning of life from the quiet corners of daily existence. Today, we step out of the study and into the town square, turning from wisdom to the prophetic voice. We examine the four books that Christian Bibles call the Major Prophets: Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Daniel. The Jewish Bible places Daniel among the Writings instead.

In biblical studies, the term prophet, or nabhi in Hebrew, does not primarily mean someone who predicts the distant future like a fortune teller. Instead, a biblical prophet is a forth-teller, a divinely commissioned spokesperson who calls God's covenant people back to faithfulness through repentance, urging them to turn away from sin and back to God. The division between major and minor prophets is simply a matter of length, not importance. Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Daniel are expansive literary works that capture centuries of political turmoil, moral failure, devastating loss, and stubborn, resilient hope.

We begin with Isaiah, a prophet who ministered in Jerusalem during a time of mounting anxiety over the looming Assyrian empire. Isaiah combines sweeping historical narrative with breathtaking poetic visions of God's holiness. The core message of Isaiah moves from severe judgment against social injustice and empty ritual to stunning promises of restoration. Mainstream academic scholarship sees the book as the product of multiple authors or prophetic circles across generations, reflecting historical shifts from the eighth century through the Babylonian exile and beyond. Traditional views attribute the entire book to the eighth-century prophet Isaiah son of Amoz. Regardless of literary development, the book confronts Israel's corrupt leaders and culminates in soaring portraits of a suffering servant who bears the sins of many.

Next, we encounter Jeremiah, known as the weeping prophet, whose ministry spanned the catastrophic final decades of the kingdom of Judah before the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BCE. Mainstream academic analysis often sees the present book of Jeremiah as a complex editorial layering of historical accounts, poetic oracles, and prose sermons that took shape over decades or even centuries, while traditional views hold that Jeremiah authored his prophecies with the aid of his scribe Baruch. Jeremiah walked the streets pleading for political submission to Babylon as God's disciplinary judgment, a message that earned him charges of treason, imprisonment, and deep personal anguish. Yet, right in the heart of impending doom, Jeremiah articulates one of the most profound theological concepts in the Hebrew Bible: the new covenant. In Jeremiah chapter thirty-one, verse thirty-one, in the King James Version, God promises: “Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah.” This covenant would not be written on stone tablets, but inscribed on human hearts, offering deep forgiveness and transformation.

Far away in Babylon among the first wave of exiles, Ezekiel experienced startling visions by the river Chebar. Mainstream academic scholarship views the book of Ezekiel as a unified priestly work largely originating from the sixth-century prophet himself, though bearing distinct editorial marks, while traditional views unhesitatingly attribute the entire text to the prophet Ezekiel during the exile. Ezekiel's writings are rich with bizarre, dramatic imagery, from wheels within wheels to a valley of dry bones springing back to life. Ezekiel addresses the painful theological crisis of the exile: had the God of Israel been defeated by the gods of Babylon? Ezekiel answers with a resounding no. The temple in Jerusalem had been abandoned by the glory of God because of rampant idolatry and injustice, and the exile was righteous judgment. Yet, the dry bones vision offers radical hope, symbolizing that God can resurrect a seemingly dead nation by breathing His Spirit into them.

Finally, the book of Daniel bridges the historical exile and the literary genre known as apocalypse. Traditional views attribute the book to the sixth-century statesman Daniel living through the Babylonian and Persian empires. In contrast, mainstream academic scholarship dates the composition of Daniel to the second century BCE, during the Maccabean period, viewing the narratives as historical fiction and the visions as apocalyptic literature written to encourage Jewish faithfulness under the Hellenistic persecution of Antiochus Epiphanes. Written partly in Hebrew and partly in Aramaic, Daniel tells the stories of young exiles maintaining their faith in the courts of foreign empires, alongside visions of beasts and kingdoms rising and falling. Daniel speaks powerfully to communities living under hostile foreign rule, assuring them that human empires are temporary, but the kingdom of God will endure forever. Across all four books, judgment is never the final word; it is the painful surgical removal of disease to make way for a restored people. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will explore how the shorter prophetic books, known as the Book of the Twelve, amplify these very themes of justice, repentance, and hope.

## Key ideas
- The biblical prophets were covenant spokespersons who called Israel to repentance, social justice, and fidelity rather than merely predicting distant future events.
- Isaiah combined warnings of judgment for social corruption with soaring visions of holiness, restoration, and a suffering servant, with scholars and traditions debating whether the book stems from one author or multiple prophetic circles.
- Jeremiah prophesied during the fall of Jerusalem, experienced severe personal rejection, and introduced the profound theological promise of a new covenant written on human hearts.
- Ezekiel ministered among the exiles in Babylon, explaining the destruction of the temple as righteous judgment while promising spiritual resurrection through the imagery of dry bones.
- Daniel combined court narratives and apocalyptic visions, with traditional interpreters viewing the book as a sixth-century historical record and modern scholars largely dating it to the second-century Maccabean era to comfort believers under persecution.
