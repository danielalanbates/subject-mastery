# Search and Problem Solving

Imagine you are standing in a vast, dark library where the books are not organized by subject or author. Instead, every book is a single move in a game, and every shelf represents a different path the game could take. To win, you must navigate this labyrinth to find the specific sequence of moves that leads to victory. This is how early pioneers of artificial intelligence viewed the challenge of creating a machine that could think. Today, we turn to the fundamental logic of how machines solve complex problems by treating them as a search through a sea of possibilities.

In the mid-twentieth century, researchers realized that many intellectual tasks could be broken down into a series of choices. A game like chess is a perfect example. At the start of a match, a player has a set number of legal moves. Each of those moves leads to a new board state, which in turn offers its own set of potential responses. If you map these out, you create a game tree. The initial position is the root, and every subsequent move branches out into new possibilities.

To navigate this tree, computer scientists use uninformed search strategies. Breadth-first search explores the tree level by level, while depth-first search dives as deep as possible into one branch before backtracking. Uniform-cost search expands the path with the lowest cumulative cost, and iterative deepening combines the benefits of both breadth and depth to explore efficiently. However, these methods often struggle with the sheer scale of the state space, which is the collection of all possible configurations of a problem.

When the state space is too large, we turn to informed search. This approach uses heuristic functions—rules of thumb that estimate the cost from a current state to the goal. A greedy best-first search always picks the node that appears closest to the goal. The A star algorithm is more sophisticated; it considers both the cost already spent to reach a node and the estimated cost to reach the finish. By combining these, it finds the most efficient path.

For two-player games, we use the minimax algorithm. It assumes that both players are playing perfectly. The computer tries to maximize its own score, while it assumes the opponent will try to minimize that same score. To make this faster, we use alpha-beta pruning. This technique allows the computer to ignore entire branches of the tree that it knows cannot possibly be better than the best option already found. This reduces the effective branching factor, allowing the machine to search much deeper. To judge these positions, the computer uses an evaluation function, which acts as a heuristic estimate of how good a particular board state is for the player.

For decades, this approach was the gold standard. In nineteen ninety-seven, a program called Deep Blue famously defeated the reigning world chess champion, Garry Kasparov. Deep Blue did not possess human intuition. Instead, it used immense computing power combined with sophisticated search algorithms and heuristics to evaluate millions of positions per second. It searched deeper into the game tree than any human could, identifying winning patterns that even a grandmaster might miss.

This success proved that machines could excel at complex, rule-bound problem solving, but it also highlighted the limitations of the search-based approach. The programs were often brittle. They could play a perfect game of chess but were completely unable to recognize a face or understand a spoken sentence. They were limited to the specific state spaces they were programmed to navigate.

By the mid-twenty-twenties, the field had shifted toward systems that can learn their own patterns from experience. While the concept of searching through states remains a bedrock of computer science, the methods we use to navigate those states have evolved. The transition from rigid search trees to dynamic, adaptable systems marks the difference between the early calculators of the eighties and the sophisticated tools we use today. As we move forward, we will see how these machines moved from merely searching through existing possibilities to learning how to generate their own knowledge.

## Key ideas

- Artificial intelligence problems are often modeled as a state space, where a computer searches through a tree of possible configurations to find an optimal solution.
- Uninformed search strategies like breadth-first and depth-first explore the search space systematically, while informed strategies like A star use heuristic functions to prioritize the most promising paths.
- The minimax algorithm and alpha-beta pruning allow computers to play adversarial games by evaluating potential outcomes while discarding branches that cannot improve the current position.
- The success of systems like Deep Blue demonstrated that high-speed search and clever heuristics allow machines to surpass human performance in structured, rule-based environments.
