# The Church Today

Imagine standing in a bustling train station in a global city like London, Lagos, or Seoul. As you look at the faces in the crowd, you are seeing a snapshot of the contemporary Christian story. It is a story that has shifted dramatically from the European-centered narrative of previous centuries to a truly global reality. As we conclude our journey through the history of the church, we find ourselves in the mid-twenty-twenties, observing a landscape that is both resilient and fragile, marked by profound growth and painful institutional reckoning.

Perhaps the most striking development in the post-Soviet era is the resurgence of Eastern Orthodoxy. For decades, under the strict atheistic policies of the Soviet Union and its satellite states, the church was pushed to the margins, its clergy silenced or exiled, and its cathedrals often repurposed. Following the collapse of the Soviet Union in the early nineteen-nineties, many Orthodox Christians describe a profound revival of faith. In Russia and across parts of Eastern Europe, ancient monasteries were restored and the liturgy returned to a central place in the lives of many believers. Orthodox leaders and laypeople often view this as a providential recovery of a suppressed identity, though this renewal also brings complex questions regarding the relationship between the church and state power.

While the church in the Global South continues to expand with vibrant energy, the West faces a different set of realities. In much of Europe and parts of North America, we see a trend toward secularization, where religious beliefs and practices lose their broad social influence. Many individuals now identify as spiritual but not religious, or as having no religious affiliation at all. This does not mean the end of the church in the West, but it does mean that Christianity has moved from being the dominant cultural default to being one voice among many in a pluralistic society. It is a return to a landscape more reminiscent of the early centuries of the church, where the faith must be lived out with intentionality rather than by tradition alone.

A major driver of contemporary global Christianity is the rapid growth of Pentecostal and Charismatic movements. These movements, which emphasize the present-day power of the Holy Spirit and personal conversion, have become a dominant force in Africa, Latin America, and parts of Asia. They offer a direct, experiential faith that has resonated deeply with millions of people, often providing strong social networks and a sense of empowerment in rapidly changing societies.

Alongside these cultural shifts, the church has had to confront its own internal failures. The global Christian community has been deeply shaken by the revelation of systemic sexual abuse. While this has been a central focus of the crisis within the Roman Catholic Church, various Protestant denominations and independent groups have also faced similar reckonings. These reports have brought to light the tragic reality of clergy abuse and the institutional failures that often followed. In response, the Catholic Church implemented the two thousand nineteen document known as Vos Estis Lux Mundi, which established new procedures for reporting abuse and holding leaders accountable. Many other denominations have likewise developed rigorous safeguarding policies. These efforts represent a painful period of repentance and structural reform, forcing the church to reconsider how it handles power, transparency, and the protection of the vulnerable.

Furthermore, we must acknowledge the reality of persecution. Today, millions of Christians live in areas where their faith makes them targets of violence or systemic legal pressure. This is particularly true for Oriental Orthodox communities, such as the Coptic Orthodox in Egypt and the Eritrean Orthodox Church, as well as the Church of the East and various Pentecostal groups in the Middle East, South Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa. Many of these believers face the threat of martyrdom, the destruction of their places of worship, and the loss of their homes, leading to significant migration. This suffering is a present-day reality that challenges the global church to practice solidarity and prayer.

As we step back from this final lesson, we see a church that is fundamentally different from the one that existed in the early twentieth century. It is a church that is increasingly non-Western, deeply diverse, and yet grappling with the same ancient questions of holiness, truth, and community. The story of the church is never static; it is always in motion, responding to the pressures of each new age while anchoring itself in the traditions and scriptures of the past. We have traced this movement from the upper room in Jerusalem to the global reach of the present day, recognizing that the history of the church is ultimately a human story of grace, struggle, and endurance.

## Key ideas

- The rise of Pentecostal and Charismatic movements in the Global South has become a primary engine of contemporary Christian growth and vitality.
- The Roman Catholic Church and various Protestant denominations are actively implementing safeguarding policies and structural reforms, such as the two thousand nineteen Vatican directive, to address the crisis of clergy abuse.
- Oriental Orthodox and Church of the East communities, along with other minority Christian groups, face significant persecution and displacement in various parts of the world.
- Eastern Orthodox Christians view the post-Soviet era as a significant revival of their faith, though this recovery involves ongoing debates about the church’s role in public and political life.
- Western Christianity is navigating a transition from a dominant cultural default to a more intentional, minority presence within an increasingly secularized society.
