# The Synoptic Gospels and Their Sources

If you lay the first three books of the New Testament side by side and read them together, a striking literary puzzle comes into view. Matthew, Mark, and Luke tell many of the same stories, often using similar phrasing, yet they also display distinct focal points and surprising variations. In our last lesson, we explored the turbulent political and religious landscape of the world between the testaments, which set the stage for these narratives. Today, we turn to the literary relationship among these three texts, known as the Synoptic Gospels, and examine the scholarly theories that explain where their striking similarities and differences come from.

The word synoptic comes from Greek roots meaning to see together. Because Matthew, Mark, and Luke share a common perspective and parallel structure, scholars have grouped them under this term to distinguish them from the fourth gospel, John, which follows a notably different literary and theological rhythm. When you read the synoptics closely, the puzzle deepens. Scholars categorize this overlapping material in specific ways. The triple tradition refers to narrative material shared by all three synoptic gospels. The double tradition refers to material found in Matthew and Luke, but absent from Mark. In addition, each gospel contains unique material, often designated by scholars as M for material exclusive to Matthew, and L for material exclusive to Luke.

To solve this intricate puzzle, modern biblical scholarship developed what is called source criticism, the study of the written and oral sources behind the biblical texts. The prevailing consensus among scholars is a solution known as the two-source hypothesis. According to this model, the Gospel of Mark was written first, around 70 CE. Both Matthew and Luke, usually dated by scholars between 80 and 90 CE, used Mark as a primary narrative framework, borrowing much of its outline and many of its stories. This explains why Mark is so compact and why its vocabulary frequently appears embedded inside Matthew and Luke.

Beyond Mark, however, Matthew and Luke share the double tradition: a large body of material that does not appear in Mark at all. This shared material consists primarily of sayings, parables, and teachings of Jesus, such as the Sermon on the Mount. To account for this, scholars propose a second major source, a hypothetical document designated by the German letter Q, short for Quelle, which simply means source. It is important to note that Q is entirely hypothetical, with no extant physical copies surviving today; its contents are inferred by scholars from the overlapping agreements and wording found in Matthew and Luke.

Under the two-source hypothesis, Matthew and Luke independently drew upon both Mark and the Q document, while also incorporating their unique M and L sources. Yet there are also minor agreements, places where Matthew and Luke share small details against Mark in passages Mark contains, such as the mocking question, who is it that struck you, at Jesus’ trial. These puzzles have led scholars to propose alternative models. The Farrer hypothesis, for example, argues for Markan priority without requiring a Q document, suggesting instead that Luke used Mark, and Matthew subsequently used both Mark and Luke.

Alternative theories have also been proposed throughout church history and modern research. The traditional view held for centuries in the church placed Matthew first, arguing that his gospel was written earliest by the apostle Matthew, after which Mark condensed Matthew, and Luke drew upon both. Another proposal, known as the Griesbach hypothesis, suggests that Matthew was written first, Luke used Matthew, and Mark subsequently wrote a brief condensation of both. Yet despite these ongoing debates, the two-source hypothesis remains the most widely accepted working model among academic researchers for explaining the complex literary dependencies among the first three gospels.

Understanding these literary relationships does not diminish the theological depth of the texts; rather, it highlights the careful craftsmanship of the evangelists. Each writer curated, arranged, and interpreted these sources under the guidance of the Holy Spirit to address the specific spiritual needs of his audience. Matthew emphasizes Jesus as the fulfillment of Jewish expectation, Mark portrays a dynamic and suffering servant in rapid motion, and Luke presents a universal savior drawing in the outsider and the marginalized. Recognizing how these authors utilized their sources allows us to appreciate both their historical roots and their enduring theological authority.

## Key ideas
- The Synoptic Gospels—Matthew, Mark, and Luke—share a similar literary structure and perspective, allowing them to be viewed and studied together.
- The two-source hypothesis proposes that Mark was written first and used by both Matthew and Luke, alongside a second hypothetical sayings source known as Q, which has no extant copies and is inferred from Matthew-Luke agreements.
- Scholars analyze this literature through the triple tradition of material in all three gospels, the double tradition in Matthew and Luke, and the unique M and L source materials.
- Alternative models such as the Farrer hypothesis, alongside traditional views of apostolic priority and the Griesbach hypothesis, offer different explanations for synoptic origins.
- Recognizing the sources behind the synoptic texts enriches our understanding of how each evangelist uniquely shaped their portrait of Jesus for their readers.
