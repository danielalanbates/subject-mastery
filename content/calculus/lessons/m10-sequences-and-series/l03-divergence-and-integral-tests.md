# Divergence and Integral Tests

Imagine you are stacking infinitely many heavy iron blocks at the edge of a tall table, each block slightly smaller and shifted further out than the one below it. You want to know if your precarious tower will stretch out infinitely far across the floor, or if it will eventually stay balanced within a finite distance from the wall. In calculus, when we add up an infinite sequence of numbers, we call the result an infinite series. Our last lesson explored geometric series, where every step scales down by a constant multiplier, letting us find an exact total sum. But most infinite series are far messier, and we need reliable tools just to discover whether their running totals explode toward infinity or settle down into a finite number.

Our first diagnostic tool is the divergence test. Suppose you have an infinite series whose terms are generated by some formula. If you add up infinitely many numbers and your running sum actually approaches a finite limit, what must happen to the individual sizes of the pieces you are adding at the very end of the line? They simply must shrink down to zero. If you are adding up blocks and the blocks themselves keep getting larger, or even stay the same size, your total sum is bound to shoot off to infinity. Therefore, if the limit of the terms themselves as the index grows toward infinity is anything other than zero, the series diverges immediately. For example, if your terms are given by the fraction two n over the quantity n plus one, the limit as n grows large is two. Because those added pieces do not vanish, the series diverges without a moment of doubt.

There is a vital catch here that trips up many students. What happens if the limit of the terms does equal zero? Does the series automatically converge? Not at all. Consider the harmonic series, where the terms are one over n, meaning one plus one half plus one third plus one quarter, and so on forever. The individual terms certainly shrink down to zero as n grows large. Yet, as Nicole Oresme proved in the fourteenth century, this series still diverges to infinity by grouping terms into clever bundles. Zero limit is a necessary condition for convergence, but it is never a guarantee.

When the divergence test fails because the terms shrink to zero, we can often turn to the integral test. Imagine an infinite sequence of positive terms represented as vertical bars of width one, standing side by side on the horizontal axis. Their total sum is equal to the total area of those rectangles. Now, imagine laying a smooth, continuous, decreasing curve right over the tops of those rectangles. The total area under that continuous curve from one to infinity can be evaluated using an improper integral, a topic we mastered in an earlier module. If the improper integral yields a finite number, the total area under the curve is finite, which forces the sum of our rectangles to be finite as well. If the improper integral diverges to infinity, the rectangle sum does too.

Let us watch this work on a concrete example. Suppose we want to check the convergence of the series where each term is one over the quantity n squared, starting at n equals one. First, check the divergence test. The limit of one over n squared as n grows large is zero, so the test is inconclusive. Next, we apply the integral test. We replace the discrete integer n with a continuous variable x, and evaluate the improper integral of one over x squared, d x, from one to infinity. We find the antiderivative of one over x squared, which is negative one over x. To check this antiderivative, we take the derivative of negative one over x with respect to x, which gives one over x squared, confirming our antiderivative is correct. Evaluating this from one to an upper bound and taking the limit as that bound goes to infinity, we get zero minus negative one, which equals one. Because this improper integral converges to the finite number one, the integral test guarantees that our original infinite series converges as well. Notice that the test tells us only that the series converges, not its sum: the series adds up to about one point six four five, not to one.

As we move forward, we will discover even more specialized tests, such as comparison methods that let us benchmark messy series against simpler ones we already understand.

## Key ideas
- An infinite series is the sum of the terms of an infinite sequence, and determining whether its running total settles down or explodes is our primary goal.
- The divergence test states that if the limit of the terms as the index grows large is not zero, the series must diverge.
- A limit of zero for the terms is necessary for convergence, but not sufficient, as seen in the divergent harmonic series.
- The integral test connects the sum of discrete series terms to the value of a continuous improper integral.
