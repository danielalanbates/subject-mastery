# Current, Resistance and Ohm's Law

Imagine turning on a garden hose connected to a pressurized water tank. The pressure pushes the water forward, but the inner walls of the hose push back with a certain friction, slowing the rush of liquid. Electric circuits behave in a remarkably similar way. In our last lesson, we explored electric potential and voltage, which acts much like the water pressure in our tank, creating a push that can drive electric charges through a conducting path. When that charge actually begins to flow through a material rather than just sitting in static fields, we have an electric current.

Electric current is simply the rate at which charge moves past a given point in a circuit, measured in amperes. One ampere of current means that one coulomb of charge flows through the cross section of a wire every single second. Inside a solid metal wire, the actual charge carriers are microscopic electrons, which drift through a lattice of fixed metal ions. Historically, physicists defined conventional current as the direction that positive charge would flow, moving from areas of high potential to areas of low potential. Even though we now know that electrons actually drift in the opposite direction, this standard convention of positive current remains the universal language of circuit analysis.

Not every material lets charge move with the same ease, however. The property that describes how much a material opposes the flow of charge is called resistance, measured in ohms. Metals like copper and aluminum have very low resistance, making them excellent conductors. Rubber, glass, and dry wood have extraordinarily high resistance, which is why we use them as insulators to keep currents safely contained where we want them. This resistance arises because the moving charges constantly collide with the vibrating atoms of the material, scattering their paths and losing some of their forward momentum as thermal energy.

To control currents precisely, engineers manufacture specialized components called resistors. A resistor is a small device designed to introduce a specific amount of resistance into a circuit. By placing resistors in a pathway, we can throttle down the flow of charge, keeping delicate components from receiving more current than they can safely handle. The relationship connecting the voltage push, the resulting current, and the hindering resistance was discovered in the early nineteenth century by the German physicist Georg Simon Ohm.

Ohm found a wonderfully simple proportionality governing most standard metallic conductors at a constant temperature. This relationship, known as Ohm's law, states that the electric current flowing through a conductor equals the voltage applied across it divided by the resistance of the path. Put another way, the voltage drop across a resistor equals the current multiplied by the resistance. If you double the voltage push while keeping the resistance steady, the current doubles as well. If you increase the resistance while keeping the voltage constant, the current drops proportionally.

Let us walk through a quick example to see how this works in practice. Suppose you connect a nine volt battery to a small ceramic heating element whose resistance has been manufactured to stay at a steady three ohms. To find the current flowing through the element, you divide the voltage of nine volts by the resistance of three ohms. Nine divided by three gives three, so the current flowing through the circuit is three amperes. That steady stream of charge warms the resistor, converting electrical energy into thermal energy at a predictable rate.

Ohm's law provides our foundational tool for analyzing how circuits behave under different loads, but real world devices often require combining multiple paths and components. Understanding how currents divide and share their paths when we hook up multiple resistors in sequence or side by side will be our next major step forward.

## Key ideas
- Electric current measures the rate at which electric charge flows past a point, expressed in amperes, with conventional current defined as the direction of positive charge flow.
- Resistance describes how strongly a material opposes the flow of charge, measured in ohms.
- Georg Simon Ohm discovered that current equals the applied voltage divided by the circuit resistance.
- Resistors are designed components used to limit and control the flow of charge in an electrical pathway.
