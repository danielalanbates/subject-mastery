# The Fall of Constantinople and Russian Orthodoxy

Imagine standing on the ancient walls of Constantinople, in what is now Turkey, during the spring of 1453. For over a thousand years, these massive stone fortifications had protected the heart of the Byzantine Empire, the jewel of Eastern Christianity. But now, the horizon was filled with the bronze cannons of the Ottoman Turks. The city was exhausted, its population had dwindled, and its political influence was reduced to little more than the city walls themselves. This moment marked the final chapter of the Roman world in the East, a tragedy that would reshape the geography of the Christian faith.

To understand why the city found itself so isolated, we must look back to the Council of Florence, held in 1439. As the Ottoman threat loomed, the Byzantine Emperor sought military aid from the West. He believed the only way to secure help from the Pope and European monarchs was to end the long-standing schism between the Greek East and the Latin West. At the council, representatives signed a decree of union. They addressed the filioque, the Latin addition to the Nicene Creed stating that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. The Roman Catholic position held that this addition was theologically sound and necessary to clarify the divinity of the Son. The Orthodox position, however, maintained that the original creed was sufficient and that the addition was an unauthorized, heretical innovation that obscured the unique role of the Father as the sole source of the Trinity. Regarding papal primacy, Rome argued that the Pope held universal jurisdiction over the entire church, while the Orthodox insisted that the Pope was merely the first among equals, with no authority to unilaterally dictate doctrine or practice. When the delegates returned to Constantinople, the people and clergy largely rejected the agreement. They felt their faith had been sold for military protection. The union remained a paper promise, and Western help proved far too little: a crusade was crushed at Varna, in what is now Bulgaria, in 1444, and only a small force of Italian volunteers joined the city's defenders in 1453.

The final siege was brief but absolute. On May 29, 1453, the walls were breached. The last Byzantine Emperor, Constantine the Eleventh, died in the fighting. The great cathedral of Hagia Sophia was converted into a mosque. Under the subsequent Ottoman rule, Christians were organized into a millet system. This gave the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople the status of an ethnarch, or civil leader, responsible for the legal and social affairs of all Orthodox Christians within the empire.

While the Byzantine world collapsed, a new power was rising in the north. The Grand Duchy of Moscow was still under Mongol suzerainty, a status that would not end until the Great Stand on the Ugra River in 1480. However, the prestige of Moscow grew significantly through the marriage of Ivan the Third to Sophia Palaiologina, the niece of the last Byzantine emperor. This union brought Byzantine symbols, such as the double-headed eagle, and imperial claims to Moscow. As news of Constantinople’s fall reached Russia, church leaders began to see their role in a new light. If Rome had fallen to what they considered heresy and Constantinople had fallen to the Ottomans, Moscow remained the last bastion of pure, uncorrupted Christianity.

This belief gave rise to the concept of Moscow as the Third Rome. The Russian state and church viewed themselves as the rightful heirs to the imperial legacy of the Caesars. The Russian Church had already acted on its own in 1448, when its bishops chose their own metropolitan without Constantinople's approval. The vision of Moscow as the Third Rome was solidified in 1589, when the Patriarch of Constantinople raised Moscow to the rank of a patriarchate, formally recognizing the Russian Church's autocephaly, or full administrative independence.

The fall of Constantinople was a hinge point in history. It signaled the end of the Middle Ages in the East and forced the Orthodox Church to adapt to life under the Ottoman sultans, while simultaneously fostering a new, self-confident center of power in the Russian north. The loss of the city also sent Greek scholars fleeing to Italy, carrying with them the ancient manuscripts that fueled the Renaissance humanism we discussed in our last lesson. As the medieval order shattered, the stage was set for a new, turbulent era of religious transformation. The old structures of authority were crumbling, and across the borders of the Holy Roman Empire, a German monk was beginning to question the very foundations of the Western Church.

## Key ideas
- The Council of Florence failed to unite the Eastern and Western churches because of deep theological disagreements over the filioque and papal authority, leaving Constantinople isolated.
- Following the fall of Constantinople in 1453, the Ottoman millet system granted the Ecumenical Patriarch civil authority over Orthodox subjects.
- The marriage of Ivan the Third to a Byzantine princess helped transmit the imperial prestige and symbols of the fallen empire to the growing Russian state.
- The establishment of the Patriarchate of Moscow in 1589 marked the formal independence of the Russian Church and solidified its identity as the Third Rome.
