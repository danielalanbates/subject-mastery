# Perfect Competition

Imagine walking into a massive open-air farmers market where twenty different stalls are selling identical, bright red summer strawberries. If one farmer tries to charge even a single cent more than the others, not a single customer will buy from that stall. Every buyer simply walks one step to the left and purchases the exact same berries for less. At the same time, because each individual farmer produces only a tiny fraction of the total berries brought to market, no single grower can influence the going market price. This vibrant, unyielding environment is the classic portrait of what economists call perfect competition.

In our last lesson, we looked under the hood of the firm to examine how production costs behave as output expands. Now, we place those cost structures directly into the marketplace to see how a firm decides what quantity to produce. Perfect competition is an idealized market structure, a theoretical benchmark against which all real-world industries are measured. It rests upon a few strict pillars. First, there are many buyers and sellers, so many that none are large enough to swing market conditions. Second, the products sold are entirely standardized or homogeneous. A bushel of wheat from one farm is indistinguishable from a bushel of wheat from another. Third, firms can enter or exit the industry freely without facing legal, financial, or technical barriers. Fourth, buyers and sellers possess perfect information about prices and product quality.

Because a perfectly competitive firm is so small relative to the total market, it possesses zero market power over price. It cannot bargain, scheme, or set its own price tag. Instead, it is a price taker. The market supply and demand curves interact out in the wider economy to establish the equilibrium price, and the individual firm must simply accept that price as given. If the market price for a bushel of wheat is six dollars, the farmer sells at six dollars. If the farmer tries to ask seven dollars, demand drops instantly to zero.

For a price taker, the demand curve facing the individual firm is completely flat, or perfectly elastic, at the market price line. This means that every additional unit sold brings in revenue equal to that exact market price. In economic terms, marginal revenue, which is the extra revenue gained from selling one more unit, is always equal to the constant market price. To maximize its profit, the firm must compare this constant marginal revenue against the marginal cost of production we explored previously.

A profit-maximizing firm will expand its output up to the exact point where the marginal cost of producing the very last unit equals the marginal revenue it generates. As long as marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost, producing another unit adds more to total revenue than it does to total cost, so profit grows. Once production pushes past that intersection, marginal cost rises above marginal revenue, and producing additional units begins to erode profit. Therefore, the golden rule for our wheat farmer, or any perfectly competitive firm, is to set output where price equals marginal cost.

Let us trace this with a simple numerical example. Suppose the market price for a basket of apples is ten dollars. Our orchard owner calculates that the marginal cost of the tenth basket is six dollars, and the eleventh basket is eight dollars. Because ten dollars is greater than both six and eight, producing those baskets adds nicely to profit. The twelfth basket has a marginal cost of exactly ten dollars. Here, price equals marginal cost, and total profit reaches its peak. If the orchard owner pushes onward to a thirteenth basket, the marginal cost climbs to twelve dollars. Since twelve dollars exceeds the ten dollar market price, that thirteenth basket loses money, and production should stop at twelve.

In the long run, this market mechanism achieves astonishing efficiency. If existing firms are earning economic profits, new firms will easily enter the industry, attracted by the open doors. This new entry shifts the overall market supply curve outward to the right, driving the market price down until economic profits are squeezed to zero. Conversely, if firms are suffering losses, some will exit, shifting market supply to the left and pushing the price back up. Perfectly competitive markets thus hover around zero economic profit in the long run, ensuring goods are produced at the lowest possible cost to society. As we transition to our next lesson, we will step away from this ideal of endless, powerless competitors to explore the opposite extreme: a single dominant firm that holds the power to set its own prices.

## Key ideas
- A perfectly competitive market features many buyers and sellers, standardized products, free entry and exit, and complete market information.
- Perfectly competitive firms are price takers, meaning they must accept the market price because they are too small to influence it.
- To maximize profit, a firm produces the quantity where the market price equals its marginal cost.
- Long-run adjustments in a competitive market drive economic profits to zero and force production at minimum possible costs.
