# Costs of Production

Imagine you decide to open a specialty coffee shop. You sign a lease on a small storefront downtown for two thousand dollars a month. Whether you sell zero cups of coffee or ten thousand cups, that rent check stays exactly the same. But to actually make those lattes, you need milk, coffee beans, paper cups, and baristas. If you sell zero cups, you need none of those supplies and hire no workers. If you sell ten thousand cups, your supply and labor bills skyrocket. This everyday distinction between bills that change with your output and bills that do not is the engine driving how firms make decisions, and it forms the bedrock of our study of market structures.

In our last module, we explored how consumers behave, balancing their preferences against a budget constraint to find utility. Now, we cross to the other side of the market transaction to look at the firm. Economists divide the life of a firm into two distinct horizons: the short run and the long run. The short run is a period during which at least one input of production is fixed, meaning it cannot be easily changed. For our coffee shop, that is the physical restaurant lease or the commercial espresso machine. In contrast, the long run is a time horizon long enough for all inputs to become variable. You can buy a larger building, sell your espresso machines, or exit the industry entirely.

Let us look closer at how costs behave in the short run. Total cost is made up of two parts. Fixed costs do not change with the level of production; they are the bills you owe even if your doors are locked. Variable costs change directly with output, rising as you produce more and falling as you produce less. When we divide these costs by the total quantity of output produced, we get average figures. Average fixed cost declines continuously as output increases because the fixed expense is spread over a larger and larger number of units. Average variable cost and average total cost, however, typically form a U shape.

This U shape happens because of diminishing marginal returns. Suppose you start adding baristas to your single espresso machine. At first, a second worker helps you take orders while the first pulls shots, and output surges. But eventually, if you cram a sixth or seventh barista behind that same counter, they start bumping into each other, waiting for the steam wand, and getting in the way. Each extra worker adds less to total output than the worker before them. Now think about marginal cost, the extra cost of producing one more unit of output. Because each extra cup takes more labor to make, marginal cost begins to rise as diminishing returns set in, eventually pulling the average cost curves upward with it.

When the firm transitions to the long run, every input becomes variable, changing the cost landscape entirely. In the long run, a firm can choose the optimal plant size for whatever scale of production it desires. If a small coffee shop expands into a massive regional roasting company, it may experience economies of scale. Economies of scale occur when the long run average cost of production falls as the quantity of output increases. This can happen for several reasons, such as bulk purchasing discounts on green coffee beans, specialized managerial talent, or the use of highly sophisticated machinery that is only affordable at massive scale.

If a firm grows too large, however, it might eventually encounter diseconomies of scale. Communication breaks down between the executive suite and the warehouse floor, bureaucracy swells, and management loses touch with daily operations, causing long run average costs to rise. Between these two zones, many industries have a range where long run average cost stays roughly flat as output grows; economists call this constant returns to scale. These cost dynamics are crucial because they dictate whether an industry is naturally dominated by a few giant corporations or populated by thousands of small businesses. Understanding how these production expenses interact gives us the exact tools we need to step inside a perfectly competitive market and see how firms decide what price to charge and how much to sell.

## Key ideas
- Fixed costs do not change with the level of output in the short run, whereas variable costs rise and fall depending on how much is produced.
- Diminishing marginal returns ensure that marginal costs eventually rise in the short run, giving average cost curves their characteristic U shape.
- The long run is a time horizon in which all inputs are variable, allowing firms to adjust their scale of operations.
- Economies of scale occur when long run average costs decrease as output expands, deeply influencing the structure of an industry.
