# Inverse Functions and Inverse Trigonometry

Imagine you are looking at a machine that takes a number, doubles it, and adds three. If you feed in four, the machine hands you eleven. But what if you find eleven sitting on the output tray and you want to know what went in? You need to run time backward. You need an inverse machine that undoes the original operation. In calculus, we often need to find the derivative of such an undone function without actually building it from scratch. Building on our recent work with exponential and natural logarithm derivatives, today we tackle how to differentiate inverse functions in general, and inverse trigonometric functions in particular.

To find the derivative of an inverse function, we rely on a wonderful partnership between a function and its undoer. Suppose a function named f takes an input x and produces an output y. Its inverse takes y and returns x. If we compose them, the result is just the input x. We must require that the original function's derivative is not zero at this point, so the inverse function is differentiable. When we take the derivative of both sides of this composite equation with respect to x, the chain rule gives us a product. We multiply the derivative of f inverse evaluated at y by the derivative of f evaluated at x, and the result is equal to one. Dividing both sides by the derivative of f, we discover that the derivative of the inverse function at y is simply one divided by the derivative of f at x.

Let us see this in action with a familiar pair. Consider the function f of x equal to two x, whose inverse is y divided by two. The derivative of f is constantly two. Using our new rule, the derivative of the inverse function at y is one over the derivative of f at x, which is one half. That matches what we get when we differentiate y over two directly. But the real power of this rule shines when the derivative is not constant, particularly with trigonometric functions that have been restricted so they can be inverted.

Take the inverse sine function, often called arcsine. The sine function is not one-to-one over all real numbers, so we restrict its domain to the closed interval from negative pi over two to positive pi over two. If y is equal to the arcsine of x, then the sine of y must equal x. We can differentiate both sides implicitly with respect to x. The derivative of the sine of y is the cosine of y times y prime, and the derivative of x is one. Solving for y prime gives us one divided by the cosine of y.

To express this entirely in terms of x, we draw on a fundamental trigonometric identity. We know that the square of the sine of y plus the square of the cosine of y equals one. Since x is the sine of y, the square of the cosine of y must equal one minus x squared. Taking the positive square root because our restricted cosine is positive in this interval, we find that the cosine of y is the square root of the quantity one minus x squared. Substituting this back into our derivative expression, we learn that the derivative of the arcsine of x is one divided by the square root of the quantity one minus x squared.

We can apply this exact same geometric reasoning to the inverse cosine function, which we call arccosine. The range of the arccosine function is the closed interval from zero to pi. Following the same implicit differentiation steps, we find that the derivative of the arccosine of x is negative one divided by the square root of the quantity one minus x squared.

Next, consider the inverse tangent function, which we call arctangent, whose range is the open interval from negative pi over two to positive pi over two. If y is equal to the arctangent of x, then the tangent of y equals x. Differentiating implicitly, the derivative of the tangent of y, which is the secant squared of y times y prime, equals one. Solving for y prime yields one divided by the secant squared of y. This derivative formula holds for all real numbers x. Using the identity relating secant and tangent, the secant squared of y is equal to one plus the square of the tangent of y, which is simply one plus x squared. Therefore, the derivative of the arctangent of x is one divided by the quantity one plus x squared. The other common inverse trigonometric functions, including arccotangent, arcsecant, and arccosecant, follow similarly through appropriate domain restrictions and implicit differentiation. Notice how remarkably clean these algebraic derivatives become, even though they spring from transcendental trigonometric roots. These rules give us new tools to analyze rates of change in systems involving angles, geometry, and bounded behaviors.

## Key ideas
- The derivative of an inverse function at a given point is equal to one divided by the derivative of the original function evaluated at the corresponding input, provided the original derivative is not zero.
- Restricting the domains of trigonometric functions allows them to be inverted, creating functions such as arcsine, arccosine, and arctangent with specific ranges.
- Implicit differentiation combined with trigonometric identities lets us find clean algebraic derivatives for inverse trigonometric functions.
- The derivative of the arcsine of x is one over the square root of the quantity one minus x squared, and the derivative of the arccosine of x is negative one over the square root of the quantity one minus x squared.
- The arctangent function has a range from negative pi over two to positive pi over two, its derivative formula holds for all real numbers x, and its derivative is one over the quantity one plus x squared.
