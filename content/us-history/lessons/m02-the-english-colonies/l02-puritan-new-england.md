# Puritan New England

Imagine standing on the deck of a small, creaking wooden ship in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean in 1620. You are surrounded by freezing spray and the constant fear of storms. You are not searching for gold, like the settlers in Jamestown, nor are you looking for a quick profit. Instead, you are leaving your home in England because you believe the official state church is corrupted beyond repair. You are a Separatist, a religious radical who believes the only way to save your soul is to start over in a new world. These voyagers, whom we call the Pilgrims, arrived at Plymouth, Massachusetts, not to build an empire of trade, but to build a community of believers.

While the Jamestown colony focused on growing tobacco for export, the New England colonies were driven by a different mandate. A decade after the Pilgrims, a much larger group known as the Puritans arrived in Massachusetts Bay. Unlike the Pilgrims, the Puritans did not want to separate from the Church of England; they wanted to purify it. They believed that the English church had kept too many rituals from the Roman Catholic tradition. They sought to create a city upon a hill, a model of holy living that would inspire their fellow Christians back in Europe to reform their own lives and institutions.

To the Puritans, society was a covenant, or a binding agreement, between God and the community. This belief dictated how they lived. They valued literacy so that every person could read the Bible for themselves, which led to the founding of Harvard College in 1636. Their daily lives were strictly regulated by religious doctrine. However, the requirement for absolute conformity created immediate tension. When individuals like Roger Williams or Anne Hutchinson questioned the authority of the ministers or suggested that personal revelation was more important than church law, they were branded as dissenters. Williams was eventually banished and founded Rhode Island on the principle of religious freedom, while Hutchinson was exiled for challenging the gender roles and theological rigidity of the Puritan elite.

As the population grew, the religious fervor of the first generation began to fade. By the 1660s, many children of the original settlers were not experiencing the intense conversions required for full church membership. To prevent the collapse of their social order, the leaders adopted the Half-Way Covenant in 1662. This allowed the unconverted children of church members to participate in some church affairs and have their own children baptized. It was a pragmatic adaptation that signaled a shift in the Puritan mission.

This focus on community and land ownership put the English settlers on a collision course with the indigenous people who already lived in the region. Many Native nations, such as the Wampanoag, practiced agriculture and lived in semi-permanent settlements, supplementing their diet with seasonal hunting and fishing. Tensions flared early. In the Pequot War of 1636 to 1638, the settlers and their allies crushed the Pequot people in a brutal conflict that established a pattern of English dominance.

By the 1670s, the relationship between the English and the Native nations had reached a breaking point. Metacom, the son of Massasoit—whom the English called King Philip—saw that his people were being squeezed out by colonial courts and land sales. In 1675, he led a massive coalition of tribes in an uprising known as King Philip’s War. This was not a minor skirmish. It was one of the bloodiest conflicts in American history relative to the size of the population. Towns were burned, and thousands of people on both sides were killed. When the war ended, the power of the Native nations in southern New England was effectively broken, and the English settlers secured total control over the region.

The legacy of Puritan New England is complex. On one hand, they established a tradition of local self-governance through town meetings and a deep commitment to education. On the other hand, their society was defined by a rigid intolerance that viewed anyone outside their faith or their culture as a threat to their survival. By 1763, the region had evolved from a small, isolated cluster of religious enclaves into a bustling, commercial society.

## Key ideas
- The Pilgrims and Puritans settled in New England primarily to build societies based on their specific interpretations of the Bible.
- The Half-Way Covenant of 1662 allowed the colonies to maintain social cohesion as religious zeal declined among the younger generations.
- Early conflicts like the Pequot War and the later King Philip's War demonstrated the violent struggle for land and power between settlers and Native nations.
- The Puritan belief in religious conformity led to the exile of dissenters like Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson, who helped establish the idea of religious freedom in neighboring areas.
