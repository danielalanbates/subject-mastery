# Electrolysis

Imagine you have a piece of dull, inexpensive jewelry that you want to coat in a thin, shimmering layer of silver. You cannot simply dip it into a silver solution and expect a beautiful finish; nature does not work that way. If you try, nothing happens because the reaction is not spontaneous. In our last lesson, we looked at how galvanic cells use spontaneous chemical reactions to produce electricity, like the batteries in your phone. Today, we explore the opposite process. We look at electrolytic cells, where we force an external current of electricity to drive a nonspontaneous chemical reaction.

An electrolytic cell uses electrical energy to push electrons in a direction they would not naturally go. Think of it like pumping water uphill. Water flows down naturally, but to get it to the top, you need a pump and an energy source. In electrochemistry, the battery or power supply acts as that pump. It forces electrons onto the cathode and pulls them away from the anode, compelling ions in a solution or a molten liquid to undergo reduction and oxidation against their thermodynamic preference.

When we perform electrolysis in an aqueous solution, we must also consider the solvent itself. Water molecules can compete with the dissolved ions for reduction or oxidation. For example, if you electrolyze a solution of sodium chloride, you might expect sodium metal to form at the cathode. However, water is easier to reduce than sodium ions. Therefore, water molecules gain electrons to form hydrogen gas and hydroxide ions instead of producing sodium metal. Similarly, at the anode, water can be oxidized to form oxygen gas. Chemists must carefully choose the voltage and concentration to ensure the desired reaction occurs rather than the electrolysis of water.

This process is the backbone of modern industry. Refining metals is a classic example. By using an electrolytic cell, we can dissolve impure copper at the anode and plate out pure copper at the cathode. To understand how much material we can produce, we look at Faraday’s law of electrolysis. The amount of substance changed at an electrode is directly proportional to the total electric charge that passes through the cell. One mole of electrons carries a charge of approximately ninety-six thousand, four hundred and eighty-five coulombs.

Let us walk through a calculation for a metal with a higher charge, such as copper. Suppose we want to plate copper from a solution of copper two ions onto a surface. We pass a current of two amperes through the solution for one thousand seconds. First, we find the total charge in coulombs by multiplying two amperes by one thousand seconds, which gives us two thousand coulombs. Next, we divide this by Faraday’s constant to find the moles of electrons. Two thousand coulombs divided by ninety-six thousand, four hundred and eighty-five coulombs per mole equals approximately zero point zero two one moles of electrons.

Now, we must account for the stoichiometry. Copper two ions have a charge of positive two, so it takes two moles of electrons to reduce one mole of copper two ions into one mole of solid copper metal. We divide our zero point zero two one moles of electrons by the stoichiometric factor of two. This gives us zero point zero one zero five moles of copper metal. Finally, we multiply this by the molar mass of copper, which is about sixty-three point five grams per mole. Zero point zero one zero five moles times sixty-three point five grams per mole equals about zero point six seven grams of copper deposited.

This ability to control chemical change with precision is why electrolysis is so vital. It allows us to produce aluminum from its ore, create chlorine gas from salt water, and manufacture countless specialty chemicals. By mastering the flow of electrons, we turn energy into matter, effectively building and refining the materials that define our modern world. As we move forward, we will begin to explore the chemistry of carbon-based compounds, starting with the simplest molecules that form the foundation of fuels and plastics.

## Key ideas

- Electrolysis uses an external source of electrical energy to drive nonspontaneous chemical reactions.
- In aqueous solutions, water often competes with solute ions for reduction or oxidation, which chemists must manage by controlling cell conditions.
- The amount of product formed is determined by the total charge passed through the cell and the stoichiometric ratio of electrons required for the specific reduction or oxidation half-reaction.
- Faraday’s constant relates the quantity of electricity to the number of moles of electrons, allowing for precise calculations of mass deposited or consumed at electrodes.
