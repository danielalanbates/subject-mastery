# Dissociative and Personality Disorders

Imagine you are reading a book and suddenly realize you have reached the bottom of the page, but you have no memory of the last few paragraphs. Now, imagine that experience of being checked out becoming so intense that you lose access to entire memories of your life or even develop a completely different way of interacting with the world. While our previous lesson explored the profound disruptions to reality found in psychotic disorders, today we turn to conditions that disrupt the very core of identity and the stable patterns of behavior we call personality.

Dissociative disorders are marked by a separation in consciousness, memory, or identity. The most common form is dissociative amnesia. This is not the typical forgetting of where you left your keys. Instead, it involves an inability to recall important personal information, usually following a traumatic or stressful event. While clinical case studies suggest this is a protective response to trauma, the evidence is largely observational and difficult to test experimentally. In rare cases, this may escalate into a dissociative fugue, where a person abruptly leaves their home and starts a new life, often with no recollection of their previous identity.

More controversial is dissociative identity disorder, formerly known as multiple personality disorder. People with this condition experience the presence of two or more distinct personality states that alternately control their behavior. The scientific community remains deeply divided over this diagnosis. The trauma model suggests it is a genuine response to severe, chronic childhood trauma, allowing the mind to compartmentalize unbearable experiences. However, other researchers point to the fact that the prevalence of the diagnosis spiked dramatically after it gained popularity in media and film. Skeptics suggest that the condition might be inadvertently encouraged by clinical techniques that lead patients to adopt different identities, making it a subject of ongoing debate.

Moving from identity to character, we arrive at personality disorders. These are enduring, inflexible patterns of behavior, cognition, and inner experience that deviate significantly from cultural expectations. These patterns are pervasive across situations and stable over time, usually emerging by late adolescence. Psychologists organize these into three clusters. Cluster A includes odd or eccentric behaviors. Cluster B, which includes antisocial and borderline personality disorders, involves dramatic, emotional, or erratic behaviors. Cluster C includes anxious or fearful behaviors. A defining feature of these disorders is that they are generally ego-syntonic. This means individuals often view their traits as consistent with their self-image and may not see their behavior as problematic, which can make them less likely to seek treatment.

Antisocial personality disorder involves a long-term pattern of violating the rights of others, often characterized by a lack of remorse, deceitfulness, and impulsivity. Research using twin and adoption studies provides moderate evidence for a genetic contribution, alongside environmental factors like early childhood adversity. However, this is a correlation, not a direct cause. Currently, there is a lack of well-validated, evidence-based treatments for this condition.

Borderline personality disorder is characterized by profound instability in moods, self-image, and relationships. Individuals often experience intense fears of abandonment and may struggle with chronic feelings of emptiness and impulsive actions. The current scientific consensus, supported by longitudinal research, views this as a disorder of emotional regulation. Unlike antisocial personality disorder, this condition has effective, evidence-based treatments. Dialectical behavior therapy has shown success in clinical trials, helping individuals build more stable lives by teaching them skills to manage intense emotional surges.

The diagnostic criteria for all personality disorders are currently debated. Critics argue that the current system is too categorical, forcing patients into all or nothing boxes. Many experts advocate for a dimensional model, which would measure traits like antagonism or emotional instability on a sliding scale rather than labeling them as present or absent. This shift would reflect the reality that human personality exists on a spectrum. As we continue to refine these definitions, psychologists remain focused on how these long-standing patterns impact a person’s ability to function and find fulfillment.

## Key ideas

- Dissociative disorders involve a disruption in the integration of memory, consciousness, or identity, often hypothesized as a defense mechanism against extreme stress.
- Personality disorders are grouped into three clusters, with antisocial and borderline personality disorders falling into Cluster B, characterized by dramatic or erratic behaviors.
- Because personality disorders are typically ego-syntonic, individuals often do not view their own traits as problematic, which frequently complicates the process of seeking and engaging in treatment.
- While antisocial personality disorder currently lacks well-validated, evidence-based treatments, borderline personality disorder is treated effectively with dialectical behavior therapy.
- Many experts advocate for moving from a categorical diagnostic system to a dimensional model that measures personality traits on a spectrum.
