# The Papacy and Charlemagne

Imagine the scene on Christmas Day in the year 800 CE. The great basilica of Saint Peter in Rome is packed with worshippers. Pope Leo the Third stands before the high altar, preparing to celebrate the liturgy. Suddenly, as the Frankish king Charlemagne rises from his prayers, the Pope steps forward and places a crown upon his head. The congregation erupts in shouts, declaring him the Emperor of the Romans. This moment was not merely a ceremony. It was the culmination of a political and spiritual shift that had been building for decades, forever changing the relationship between the Church and the powers of Europe.

To understand why this happened, we must look at the precarious situation of the Papacy in the mid-eighth century. The popes in Rome were increasingly isolated from the Byzantine Empire in the east, which was preoccupied with its own defensive struggles. At the same time, the popes faced immediate military threats from the Lombards, a Germanic tribe that had settled in Italy and was encroaching on papal lands. The popes needed a protector. They found their answer in the rising power of the Frankish kingdom, located in what is now France and Germany.

This alliance began in earnest with Pepin the Short, the father of Charlemagne. Pepin sought the Pope’s blessing to officially claim the Frankish throne, which he held in all but title. In exchange, he marched into Italy, defeated the Lombards, and granted the conquered territories to the Pope. This gift, known as the Donation of Pepin, established the Papal States. This was a sovereign territory in central Italy governed directly by the church. It gave the papacy a degree of political independence that would define its role for the next millennium, though it also entangled the spiritual office of the bishop of Rome in the complex world of land ownership and territorial defense.

When Charlemagne succeeded his father, he took this relationship to a new level. He was a vigorous warrior and a devout Christian who saw his role as the defender of the faith. He expanded his kingdom through constant warfare, often forcing the pagan tribes on his borders to accept baptism. While historians today rightly criticize the use of coercion in conversion, Charlemagne saw himself as a new King David, unifying the Christian people under one God and one monarch. His coronation in 800 CE was a public declaration that the West no longer looked to the Byzantine emperor in Constantinople as its supreme political head.

Beyond the battlefield, Charlemagne initiated what we call the Carolingian Renaissance. He recognized that the church could not thrive if its clergy were illiterate or its texts were filled with errors. He invited scholars from across Europe, including the brilliant Alcuin of York, to his court at Aachen, in modern-day Germany. He tasked these scholars with establishing schools. He ordered them to standardize the Latin liturgy, and he directed them to copy ancient manuscripts to preserve both Christian scripture and classical Latin literature. This effort saved countless works from being lost to time and ensured that the Latin language remained a common thread of communication for the educated across a fractured Europe.

During this era, a significant theological tension emerged regarding the Creed. The Frankish court began inserting the word "filioque," meaning "and the Son," into the Nicene Creed to describe the procession of the Holy Spirit. While the West adopted this addition to emphasize the divinity of Christ, the Eastern churches viewed it as an unauthorized change to a universal confession. This disagreement became a major point of friction between the East and the West.

Church life under Charlemagne was marked by a new sense of order and uniformity. He appointed bishops as local administrators, ensuring that his laws and the church’s moral teachings reached the farthest corners of his realm. He promoted the Benedictine rule for monasteries. This rule emphasized a life of discipline. It also required manual labor and constant prayer. While this centralization brought stability and a renewed interest in learning, it also blurred the lines between royal authority and ecclesiastical independence. The king was now the head of the church’s structure, a reality that would lead to future conflicts over who truly held the ultimate authority in Christendom.

By the time of his death in 814 CE, Charlemagne had forged a vision of a unified Christian empire that would shape the European imagination for centuries. The alliance between the papacy and the Frankish crown had brought security to the church, but it had also tethered the spiritual mission of the gospel to the changing fortunes of dynasties. As the Carolingian empire eventually began to fracture under his successors, the church would have to navigate a world where the protection of kings was no longer guaranteed.

## Key ideas

- The Papacy sought an alliance with the Frankish kings to secure military protection against the Lombards in Italy.
- The Donation of Pepin established the Papal States, granting the Pope territorial sovereignty and political influence.
- Charlemagne’s coronation as Emperor in 800 CE symbolized the independence of the Western church from the Byzantine Empire and the birth of a new Christian order.
- The Carolingian Renaissance fostered a revival of education, scholarship, and liturgical standardization that preserved vital texts and improved clerical literacy.
- The addition of the filioque clause to the Creed in the Frankish court created a lasting theological dispute between the Eastern and Western churches.
