# Gravity and Free Fall

Imagine holding a heavy stone in one hand and a small wooden coin in the other. If you release them at the exact same instant from the same height, which one strikes the ground first? For centuries, common sense suggested that the heavier object should fall faster. Yet if you eliminate the air surrounding them, both objects hit the floor at precisely the same moment. This remarkable feature of nature is the foundation of free fall.

In physics, free fall describes the motion of an object when gravity is the only force acting upon it. The Italian scientist Galileo Galilei famously demonstrated that, in the absence of air resistance, all falling bodies accelerate toward the Earth at the exact same rate regardless of their mass or shape. Why do all objects fall at the same rate? According to Newton's second law, acceleration equals the net force divided by mass. The gravitational force on an object is proportional to its mass, so when you divide that gravitational force by the mass of the object, the mass cancels out entirely. This mathematical cancellation guarantees that heavy and light objects accelerate identically.

On Earth, this natural acceleration due to gravity is approximately nine point eight meters per second squared. For quick estimates, physicists and engineers often round this value to ten meters per second squared. This means that for every second an object falls, its downward speed increases by nine point eight meters per second.

In our previous lesson, we learned the equations of constant acceleration. Because the force of gravity produces a steady, unchanging acceleration near Earth's surface, those exact same kinematic equations govern free fall. To use them accurately, we first choose a coordinate system. A common choice is setting upward as the positive vertical direction. Because gravity pulls downward toward the center of Earth, the downward direction is negative, and the acceleration due to gravity is written as negative nine point eight meters per second squared.

Let us walk through a practical example of an object dropped from rest. Suppose you release a stone from the top of a tall bridge. Because you drop it rather than throw it, its initial velocity is zero meters per second. After two seconds of falling, what is its velocity, and how far has it traveled? Using our kinematic equation, the final velocity equals the initial velocity plus the acceleration multiplied by time. Zero plus negative nine point eight meters per second squared times two seconds yields a final velocity of negative nineteen point six meters per second. The negative sign simply tells us the stone is moving downward.

To find the vertical distance fallen in those two seconds, we use the position formula: initial velocity times time plus one half of the acceleration times the square of the time. Since the initial velocity is zero, the first term vanishes. We multiply one half by negative nine point eight meters per second squared, then multiply by the square of two seconds, which is four seconds squared. This calculation gives negative nineteen point six meters. The stone has dropped nineteen point six meters below its starting point. For context, the speed of nineteen point six meters per second is roughly forty-four miles per hour, which fits our everyday experience of a stone falling for two full seconds.

Now consider what happens when you throw an object straight upward. Imagine tossing a tennis ball straight into the air with an initial upward velocity of nineteen point six meters per second. As the ball rises, gravity continuously accelerates it downward, slowing it down. At its highest point, the ball momentarily stops before reversing direction. Its vertical velocity at that peak moment is exactly zero meters per second.

How long does it take the ball to reach that peak? We set the final velocity to zero and solve for time. Zero equals nineteen point six meters per second plus negative nine point eight meters per second squared times time. Solving this equation shows that time equals two seconds. Gravity removes nine point eight meters per second of upward speed each second, so two seconds are required to reduce nineteen point six to zero. Because gravity acts continuously, the motion is perfectly symmetric: the ball takes another two seconds to fall back to your hand.

Of course, real-world experience sometimes seems to contradict this model. A flat sheet of paper floats down slowly while a crumpled paper ball plunges straight to the floor. This difference occurs because air resistance opposes the motion of the flat paper far more than the crumpled paper ball. Free fall is an idealized model that assumes air resistance is negligible or absent altogether, such as in a vacuum chamber or on the airless surface of the Moon. Furthermore, this constant acceleration model assumes we remain near Earth's surface; at very high altitudes far out in space, the distance from the center of the planet increases, and the gravitational acceleration decreases slightly.

Understanding how gravity creates constant vertical acceleration allows us to predict the speed, height, and timing of any falling body. Up to now, we have described these motions entirely using numbers and equations. In our next lesson, we will see how these exact journeys look when plotted on position, velocity, and acceleration graphs.

## Key ideas

- Free fall occurs when gravity is the only force acting on an object, causing all objects regardless of mass to accelerate at the same rate in a vacuum because mass cancels out in Newton's second law.
- Near Earth's surface, the downward acceleration due to gravity is constant and equals approximately nine point eight meters per second squared, though it is often rounded to ten meters per second squared for quick estimates.
- The constant acceleration assumption for gravity is only valid near Earth's surface, as gravity decreases slightly at very high altitudes.
- Because gravitational acceleration is constant in this idealized model that neglects air resistance, the standard equations of constant acceleration describe vertical free fall motion.
- Symmetrical vertical motion implies that an object thrown upward loses speed at nine point eight meters per second squared until its velocity reaches zero at the top, then gains speed at the exact same rate on its descent.
