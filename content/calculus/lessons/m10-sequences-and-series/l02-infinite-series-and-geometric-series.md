# Infinite Series and Geometric Series

Imagine standing ten feet away from a wall, and with every step you take forward, you cover half the remaining distance. First you walk five feet, leaving five feet. Then you walk two and a half feet, leaving two and a half feet. You keep halving the remaining gap, taking steps of one and one-quarter feet, then seven point five inches, and so on, forever. Will you ever actually touch the wall? Common sense tells us you reach the wall quite quickly in reality, but mathematically, you are adding an infinite string of numbers together. That endless sum is what we call an infinite series, and today we are going to explore how adding infinitely many numbers can actually result in a finite, exact total.

In our previous lesson, we looked at sequences, which are just endless lists of numbers ordered one after another. A series is different because it represents the sum of the terms of a sequence. If we have a sequence of numbers, we can form what we call partial sums. We add the first term, then we add the first two terms, then the first three, and so on. This creates a brand new sequence of partial sums. If this sequence of partial sums settles down and approaches a single finite number as we include more and more terms, we say the infinite series converges, and that final limit is the sum of the series. If the partial sums grow without bound or bounce around forever, the series diverges, meaning it has no sum at all.

One of the most important and well-behaved types of infinite series is the geometric series. A geometric series is built by starting with an initial number and multiplying by a fixed constant ratio over and over again to generate each subsequent term. For example, if we start with one and multiply by one-half repeatedly, we get the terms one, one-half, one-quarter, one-eighth, and so on. When we add them all up, we get one plus one-half plus one-quarter plus one-eighth, continuing forever.

Let us see what happens to the partial sums of this specific geometric series. The first partial sum is simply one. The second partial sum is one plus one-half, which equals one and one-half. The third partial sum is one plus one-half plus one-quarter, which equals one and three-quarters. Each time we add the next term, we close half of the remaining distance to the number two. As we take more and more terms, the sum gets closer and closer to two. Therefore, this geometric series converges, and its sum is precisely two.

The secret to whether any geometric series converges lies entirely in the magnitude of its common ratio. Let us call the starting term the constant b, and let us call the common multiplier the constant r. If the absolute value of r is strictly less than one, meaning the multiplier is a proper fraction between negative one and one, the terms shrink rapidly toward zero. When we sum them up, the formula for the sum of an infinite geometric series turns out to be remarkably simple. The sum equals the starting term b divided by the quantity one minus r.

Let us test this formula on our earlier example where the starting term is one and the ratio is one-half. Following the rule, we divide the starting term one by the quantity one minus one-half. The denominator one minus one-half simplifies to one-half. Dividing one by one-half gives us two, matching our earlier physical intuition about walking toward the wall. What happens if the absolute value of r is greater than or equal to one? In that case, the terms do not shrink toward zero. They either stay the same, grow larger, or alternate wildly in magnitude, making it impossible for the partial sums to settle down. Thus, any geometric series where the absolute value of r is greater than or equal to one must diverge.

Understanding how to find the sum of a geometric series gives us a powerful tool for analyzing infinite processes. Yet, many series we encounter in calculus are not geometric, and their terms do not follow such a simple multiplicative pattern. To handle those more complex sums, we must develop other methods to test for convergence, which is precisely where our journey leads next as we investigate convergence tests for general series.

## Key ideas
- An infinite series is the sum of the terms of an infinite sequence, evaluated by taking the limit of its running partial sums.
- An infinite series converges if its sequence of partial sums approaches a finite limit, and diverges if it does not.
- A geometric series has a constant ratio between consecutive terms and converges if and only if the absolute value of that common ratio is strictly less than one.
- The exact sum of a convergent geometric series is found by dividing the starting term by the quantity one minus the common ratio.
