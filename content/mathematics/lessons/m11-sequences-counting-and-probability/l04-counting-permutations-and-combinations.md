# Counting, Permutations and Combinations

Imagine standing before a digital padlock with a four-digit code, wondering how many possible combinations could keep your bicycle secure. In our previous work, we explored patterns that grow by multiplication step by step. Today, we turn that multiplicative thinking toward a fundamental question of discrete mathematics: how many ways can we arrange or select objects from a collection? Counting sounds simple, but when collections grow large, direct listing becomes impossible. We need systematic rules.

The foundation of all counting is the multiplication principle. Suppose a task consists of two successive stages. If the first stage can be performed in m different ways, and for each of those ways the second stage can be performed in n different ways, then the entire task can be performed in m times n ways. Think of choosing an outfit with three different shirts and four different pairs of trousers. For every single shirt you might pick, there are four independent trousers choices waiting. Three times four gives twelve possible outfits, let us call this total outcome count product one. This rule extends naturally to any number of sequential stages, whether you are building license plates from six letters or selecting menu items across four courses.

When we arrange a set of distinct objects in a row, order matters deeply. If you have four books named book one, book two, book three, and book four, and you want to line them all up on a shelf, how many arrangements exist? By the multiplication principle, you have four choices for the first spot. Once that book is placed, three choices remain for the second spot, two for the third spot, and only one choice for the final spot. Multiplying four times three times two times one gives twenty-four possible arrangements, let us call this result arrangement total one. We can check this by retracing our steps: starting with four choices, then reducing by one at each of the four sequential positions, the product confirms twenty-four valid ordered rows.

This product of descending integers appears so frequently that mathematicians gave it a special name and symbol: the factorial. The factorial of a positive integer is the product of all positive integers less than or equal to that number. We write it with an exclamation point, and we say it aloud as n factorial. For instance, four factorial equals twenty-four. What about zero items? By convention, zero factorial equals one, a definition that keeps our formulas consistent when choosing zero items from a set.

What if we only want to arrange a smaller group taken from a larger set? Suppose we have five books, but our shelf only holds three of them. We call this a permutation, which is an arrangement where order matters. To count permutations of choosing three items from five, we apply the multiplication principle across three sequential stages. There are five choices for the first position, four choices for the second position, and three choices for the third position. Five times four times three equals sixty, let us call this permutation count sixty.

Expressed as a general formula, the number of permutations of choosing k items from a set of n items, written as n P k, is found by dividing n factorial by the quantity n minus k all in factorial. For our books, five factorial divided by the quantity five minus three all in factorial is one hundred twenty divided by two, which equals sixty. We can check this by expanding five factorial as five times four times three times two times one, and dividing by two times one, leaving exactly five times four times three, or sixty.

Sometimes, however, the order of our selection does not matter at all. If you are dealt a hand of five cards from a standard deck, the order in which the dealer handed them to you changes nothing about the hand you hold. A selection where order is ignored is called a combination. Because a permutation treats different orders of the exact same items as distinct outcomes, it overcounts selections where order is irrelevant.

To correct for this, we take our permutation count and divide by the number of ways those selected items could be arranged among themselves. The relationship n C k equals n P k divided by k factorial gives us our combinations formula, which expands to n factorial divided by the quantity k factorial times the quantity n minus k all in factorial. Suppose we choose three items from a set of five. The number of permutations is sixty. But those three items can be arranged among themselves in three factorial, or six, different ways. Dividing sixty by six yields ten, let us call this combination count ten. There are precisely ten combinations of three items chosen from a set of five. We can check this by multiplying our combination count of ten by the internal arrangement count of six, which successfully returns our permutation count of sixty. This powerful coefficient lets us count subsets without worrying about sequence.

## Key ideas
- The multiplication principle states that if a task has sequential stages, the total number of outcomes is the product of the number of ways each stage can occur, extending to any number of sequential stages.
- A factorial represents the product of all positive integers up to a given number, with zero factorial defined as equal to one.
- The explicit formula for permutations is n P k equals n factorial divided by the quantity n minus k all in factorial.
- The explicit formula for combinations is n C k equals n factorial divided by the product of k factorial and the quantity n minus k all in factorial, which equals n P k divided by k factorial.
- Combinations count selections where order does not matter by dividing permutations out by the internal arrangements of the chosen items.
