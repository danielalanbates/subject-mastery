# Martin Luther and Wittenberg

Imagine a man caught in a violent thunderstorm, terrified not by the lightning, but by the sudden, visceral certainty of his own standing before a holy God. This was the young Martin Luther, a law student who, in 1505, made a desperate vow to Saint Anne that he would become a monk if he survived. He entered the Augustinian monastery in Erfurt, driven by an overwhelming sense of his own sinfulness. Luther was haunted by a single, agonizing question: how could a sinful human being ever find peace with a righteous and demanding God?

He fasted, prayed, and confessed his sins for hours, yet the peace he sought remained elusive. In 1510, he traveled to Rome, hoping to find spiritual renewal. Instead, he returned disillusioned by the corruption he witnessed within the heart of the church. His superiors sent him to study theology at the University of Wittenberg, hoping that academic work might settle his restless spirit. There, his study of the Bible led him to a startling conclusion. He realized that the righteousness of God was not a standard that humans had to meet through their own efforts, but a gift that God freely offered to those who trusted in Him.

While Luther wrestled with these realizations, the church was engaged in a massive building project in Rome. To fund the construction of Saint Peter’s Basilica, the Pope authorized the sale of indulgences. A Dominican friar named Johann Tetzel became famous for his aggressive marketing of these certificates, which were marketed as a way to reduce the temporal punishment for sins. Many people were led to believe that a monetary payment could guarantee the release of their loved ones from purgatory. To Luther, this was a dangerous distortion of the Gospel.

In October of 1517, Luther wrote his Ninety-five Theses. He sent them to Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz and, according to a tradition that some historians question, posted them in Latin on the Wittenberg Castle Church door, following standard academic practice as an invitation to a scholarly debate. He did not act in a fit of rage, but as a concerned professor. However, the printing press allowed these theses to spread rapidly throughout Germany. The controversy deepened in 1518 at the Heidelberg Disputation, where Luther developed his theology of the cross, and in 1519 at the Leipzig debate, where he was forced to confront the limits of papal authority.

By 1520, the conflict reached a breaking point. Pope Leo the Tenth issued a papal bull, or formal decree, titled Exsurge Domine, which condemned Luther’s teachings and gave him sixty days to recant. In December of that year, Luther responded by publicly burning the papal bull and copies of canon law. During this same year, he published three major treatises. In these works, he addressed the Christian nobility of the German nation, argued against what he saw as the Babylonian captivity of the church, and described the freedom of a Christian.

The conflict moved to the political stage in 1521 at the Diet of Worms, a formal assembly of the Holy Roman Empire. Elector Frederick the Wise of Saxony, Luther’s protector, arranged for his safe conduct to the assembly. There, the young Emperor, Charles the Fifth, demanded that Luther recant. Luther refused, stating that his conscience was held captive by the word of God. Following the assembly, the Edict of Worms declared Luther an outlaw and banned his works.

While the edict was meant to silence him, Frederick the Wise orchestrated a secret kidnapping, hiding Luther at the Wartburg Castle. During his time in hiding, Luther translated the New Testament into German, making the scriptures accessible to the common people. This act, combined with his earlier writings, signaled the end of the medieval consensus and the beginning of a new, fractured reality for the church in the West. The question was no longer whether the church needed reform, but how far that reform would go.

## Key ideas

- Martin Luther’s internal crisis of faith, sparked by his 1505 vow and subsequent monastic life, led him to the conviction that justification is a gift of God received by faith alone.
- The indulgence controversy, fueled by the aggressive sales tactics of Johann Tetzel, became the catalyst for Luther to challenge both church practice and the limits of papal authority.
- Through a series of 1520 treatises and public acts of defiance, Luther moved from academic debate to a fundamental rejection of the existing ecclesiastical structure.
- The 1521 Diet of Worms and the subsequent Edict of Worms solidified the break between Luther and the Roman Catholic Church, forcing the movement into a new phase of development.
- While in hiding at the Wartburg Castle under the protection of Frederick the Wise, Luther translated the New Testament into German, fundamentally changing the landscape of European Christianity.
