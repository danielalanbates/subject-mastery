# Vector Addition and Components

Imagine piloting a small boat across a wide river. You point your boat directly east, aiming for a dock. But a strong current flows south. If you maintain your eastward heading, you won't reach the dock; you'll land south of your target, carried downstream. Your boat's velocity relative to the water and the river's current velocity act simultaneously. While we've analyzed motion along a single line, the real world often involves movement in multiple directions. To understand situations like our boat, we need a powerful tool: breaking down vectors into their components.

Recall that vectors, unlike scalars, possess both magnitude and direction. Displacement, velocity, and force are all vector quantities. When vectors act along the same line, adding or subtracting them is straightforward. However, when they point in different directions, such as our boat moving east while the current pulls it south, we cannot simply add their magnitudes. Instead, we break each vector into simpler, perpendicular pieces called components. We typically use a coordinate system with a horizontal X-axis and a vertical Y-axis. Any vector can then be viewed as the sum of its horizontal and vertical components.

To express this concisely, we often use unit vectors. A unit vector has a magnitude of one and points in a specific direction. We use 'i-hat' to represent a unit vector along the positive X-axis, and 'j-hat' for a unit vector along the positive Y-axis. So, any vector, say vector V, can be written as V sub X i-hat plus V sub Y j-hat, where V sub X and V sub Y are its scalar components.

To find these components, we use trigonometry. Imagine a vector, say, a force, originating from the origin of a coordinate system and extending into the first quadrant. This vector forms the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle. Its horizontal component is the side adjacent to the angle it makes with the positive X-axis, and its vertical component is the side opposite that angle. If we denote the vector's magnitude as F and the angle it makes with the positive X-axis as theta, then the horizontal component, F sub X, equals F times the cosine of theta. The vertical component, F sub Y, equals F times the sine of theta.

Let's try an example. Suppose a force of one hundred newtons acts at an angle of thirty degrees above the positive X-axis. To find its horizontal component, F sub X, we calculate one hundred newtons multiplied by the cosine of thirty degrees, which is approximately zero point eight six six. This gives F sub X as eighty-six point six newtons. For the vertical component, F sub Y, we calculate one hundred newtons multiplied by the sine of thirty degrees, which is zero point five. This yields F sub Y as fifty newtons. Thus, our one hundred newton force at thirty degrees has the same effect as an eighty-six point six newton horizontal force and a fifty newton vertical force.

Now, how do we add multiple vectors using this component method? First, break every vector you want to add into its horizontal, X, and vertical, Y, components. Be careful with the signs; components pointing left or down will be negative. It is important to remember that vector addition is commutative, meaning the order in which you add the vectors or their components does not affect the final resultant vector. Next, sum all the X components to get a single total horizontal component for the resultant vector. Do the same for all the Y components to find the total vertical component.

Finally, with a single resultant X component and a single resultant Y component, you can find the magnitude of the resultant vector using the Pythagorean theorem. Its direction or angle is found using the inverse tangent function. A crucial step is considering the signs of your resultant X and Y components. The inverse tangent function typically gives an angle between negative ninety and positive ninety degrees. To determine the correct quadrant, you must look at the signs of both components. If the resultant X component is negative, add one hundred eighty degrees to the calculator's angle. If the X component is positive and the Y component is negative, you can add three hundred sixty degrees to express the angle as a positive value measured from the positive X-axis.

Let's add two displacement vectors: ten meters due East and ten meters due North. For the East vector, its X component is ten meters, Y is zero. For the North vector, its X component is zero, Y is ten meters. Summing components: resultant X is ten meters, resultant Y is ten meters. Using the Pythagorean theorem, the magnitude is the square root of (ten squared plus ten squared), which is the square root of two hundred, or approximately fourteen point one four meters. The direction is the inverse tangent of (ten divided by ten), which is forty-five degrees. Thus, the resultant displacement is fourteen point one four meters at forty-five degrees North of East. This method works for any number of vectors.

By breaking down complex motion into independent horizontal and vertical components, we simplify multi-directional problems into two separate one-dimensional problems. This component method is universally applicable not only to displacement vectors but also to velocities, forces, and accelerations, providing a consistent and powerful way to handle all vector quantities. This technique will be essential as we explore more complex scenarios, such as the curved paths of objects launched into the air.

## Key ideas
- Any vector can be broken down into two perpendicular components, typically a horizontal (X) component and a vertical (Y) component, and can be concisely expressed using unit vectors i-hat and j-hat.
- The horizontal component of a vector is found by multiplying its magnitude by the cosine of the angle it makes with the positive X-axis, and the vertical component by the sine of that angle.
- To add multiple vectors, first decompose each vector into its X and Y components, then sum all X components and all Y components separately, noting that vector addition is commutative.
- The magnitude of the resultant vector is found using the Pythagorean theorem with the total X and Y components, and its direction is found using the inverse tangent function, carefully adjusting for the correct quadrant based on the signs of the components.
- The component method is a general technique applicable to all vector quantities, including displacement, velocity, force, and acceleration.
