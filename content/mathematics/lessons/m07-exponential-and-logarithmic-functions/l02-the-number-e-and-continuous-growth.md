# The Number e and Continuous Growth

Imagine you deposit one hundred dollars into a bank account that promises one hundred percent interest every year. If the bank compounds that interest just once at the end of the year, your money doubles to two hundred dollars. But what happens if the bank agrees to compound that interest twice a year, splitting the one hundred percent rate into two fifty percent payments? Your money grows during the first six months, and then that new total earns interest for the second six months. Suddenly, you end up with two hundred twenty-five dollars. If you compound quarterly, monthly, or even daily, your final amount creeps higher, but something fascinating happens. It does not shoot off to infinity. It slows down and presses up against a specific ceiling.

Let us look at the actual numbers for our one-dollar investment at one hundred percent interest over a single year. If you compound quarterly, meaning four times a year, your one dollar grows to roughly two point four four dollars. If you compound monthly, meaning twelve times a year, it grows to roughly two point six one dollars. If you compound daily, meaning three hundred sixty-five times a year, it reaches about two point seven one dollars. As the number of compounding periods grows larger and larger, the amount presses closer and closer to a fixed limit.

In our previous lesson, we explored discrete exponential growth and decay, where populations double or investments grow at fixed, regular intervals like years or months. But nature and finance often operate without waiting for the clock to strike a specific hour. Bacteria multiply continuously, radioactive atoms decay atom by atom without pausing, and continuous interest compounds every second of the day. To understand how this works, let us return to our bank account. Suppose you invest one dollar at one hundred percent interest, compounded over smaller and smaller intervals. If you divide the year into fractions of one over n, where n represents the number of compounding periods, the formula for your final amount is the quantity one plus one over n, all raised to the power of n.

As you let n grow larger—compounding weekly, daily, hourly, and down to every split second—the value of that expression does not explode. It settles. It approaches a fundamental constant of mathematics, an irrational number known as the number e. Pronounced simply as e, this number begins with two point seven one eight two eight, and its decimals continue forever without repeating, much like pi. Leonhard Euler popularized this letter for the constant in the eighteenth century, and it stands at the very heart of growth and decay that happens smoothly and without interruption.

To build models for continuous growth and decay with arbitrary starting amounts and rates, we scale our base growth factor. When interest compounds continuously at a nominal annual rate r over t years, dividing the annual rate into n compounding periods gives the expression the quantity one plus r over n, all raised to the power of n times t. As n approaches infinity, this expression transforms into the base e raised to the power of r times t. Multiplying this exponential factor by our initial principal p gives us our general continuous growth model, p times e raised to the power of r times t.

For instance, if a colony of bacteria starts with one hundred cells and grows continuously at a rate of ten percent per hour, the population after three hours is one hundred times e raised to the power of zero point three, which is ten percent times three hours. Because e raised to zero point three is approximately one point three five, the population reaches about one hundred thirty-five cells.

Decay works under the exact same principle, except the rate in the exponent is negative. If you begin with fifty grams of a radioactive substance that decays continuously at a rate of twenty percent per day, the amount remaining after four days is fifty times e raised to the power of negative zero point eight. Since the exponent is negative, the value of e to that power is a fraction less than one, representing a smooth, unbroken decline. Calculating this gives roughly twenty-two point five grams remaining. Whether tracking money in a continuously compounding account or the cooling of a warm object in a room, the number e provides the ultimate mathematical bridge for processes that never stop to take a breath. As we move forward, we will discover how to reverse these exponential processes by exploring logarithms as inverses.

## Key ideas
- The number e, approximately equal to two point seven one eight, arises naturally when compound interest periods are made infinitely frequent.
- Taking the limit of the compound interest formula as compounding periods approach infinity scales our unit investment limit into the general continuous growth model with principal p, rate r, and time t.
- Continuous growth and decay models use the base e raised to an exponent containing the rate multiplied by time.
- A positive rate in the exponent of a base e function creates continuous growth, while a negative rate creates continuous decay.
- Continuous models reflect real-world processes that happen smoothly and constantly rather than in discrete, separated steps.
