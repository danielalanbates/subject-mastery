# Conformity, Compliance and Obedience

Imagine you are sitting in a room with several other people. A researcher shows you a card with a single vertical line on it, and then a second card with three lines of varying lengths. Your task is simple: identify which of the three lines matches the first one. When it is your turn, you look at the lines and see clearly that the answer is line B. But the person sitting next to you says "Line A." Then the next person says "Line A." By the time it reaches you, everyone else has chosen the wrong answer. Do you trust your own eyes, or do you go along with the group?

This is the classic setup of Solomon Asch’s experimental laboratory research on conformity. Conformity is the act of adjusting our behavior or thinking to align with a group standard. In the nineteen-fifties, Asch found that about seventy-five percent of participants conformed at least once during the experiment, while the average conformity rate across all trials was roughly one-third. This evidence is considered robust, as the findings have been repeatedly replicated in various settings. Asch also discovered that conformity increases as the group size grows, but only up to about three or four confederates, after which the effect levels off. Additionally, conformity drops significantly if a participant is allowed to write their answer down privately rather than stating it aloud, which reduces the pressure to conform.

People often conform for two reasons. First, they want to be liked, which is known as normative social influence. Second, they assume the group must know something they do not, known as informational social influence. While Asch’s work showed how social pressure can sway our judgment, conformity also drops significantly if just one other person in the room disagrees with the group, providing a crucial sense of social support.

While conformity involves changing to fit in with a group, compliance is slightly different. Compliance is a response to a direct request, often from someone who has no real authority over us. Salespeople and fundraisers use various techniques to gain our compliance. One of the most studied is the foot-in-the-door phenomenon. This is the tendency for people who have first agreed to a small, trivial request to later comply with a much larger one. Experimental evidence suggests that agreeing to the small request alters our self-perception, making us more likely to agree to the follow-up request to remain consistent with our earlier behavior.

Perhaps the most chilling area of social influence research is obedience, which is compliance with an order from an authority figure. In the nineteen-sixties, Stanley Milgram conducted a series of experimental laboratory studies where participants were told to deliver increasingly painful electric shocks to a learner in another room every time the learner made a mistake on a memory task. In reality, the learner was an actor and no shocks were given, but the participants believed they were causing real harm. Despite their visible distress, a high percentage of participants continued to obey the researcher’s commands to keep shocking the learner until the end of the scale.

Milgram’s studies have faced significant scrutiny over the decades, both for their methodology and for the intense psychological stress they placed on participants. Critics point out that the experimental design was highly artificial. However, later partial replications, such as those conducted by Jerry Burger in two thousand nine, have found comparable obedience levels when ethical safeguards are added. These findings suggest that obedience is not an inevitable human trait, but rather one that depends heavily on the perceived legitimacy of the authority, the distance from the victim, and the presence of others who might dissent. These studies remain a landmark in ethics, as they led to modern requirements for informed consent and institutional review boards to protect research participants from harm.

Ultimately, these studies show that while we like to think of ourselves as independent thinkers, our behavior is frequently shaped by the hidden currents of social pressure. Whether we are conforming to a peer group, complying with a polite request, or obeying an authority figure, the situation often exerts more power over us than we care to admit. Understanding these mechanisms is the first step in maintaining our autonomy.

## Key ideas

- Conformity is the tendency to adjust our behavior or thinking to match a group, a robust finding that increases with group size up to a point and decreases when dissenters are present.
- The foot-in-the-door phenomenon demonstrates that people are more likely to agree to a large request if they have already committed to a smaller, related one first.
- Milgram’s obedience studies highlighted how individuals may follow orders from authority figures even when those orders conflict with their personal values, a finding supported by some modern replications.
- Social influence is not absolute; the presence of a dissenter or the ability to respond privately can significantly reduce the pressure to conform or obey.
