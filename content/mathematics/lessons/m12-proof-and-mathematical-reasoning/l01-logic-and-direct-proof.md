# Logic, Implication and Direct Proof

Imagine a courtroom lawyer building an airtight case from a stack of undisputed facts, or a programmer tracking down a bug by following the strict rules of a compiler. In both worlds, persuasion and truth do not rely on a gut feeling or a lucky guess. They rely on logic. Up to this point in our mathematical journey, we have calculated probabilities, solved complex equations, and manipulated functions, trusting rules that someone else established. Today, we step behind the curtain to examine how mathematicians establish those rules in the first place, moving from calculation to certainty through the art of proof.

Every mathematical proof is built out of statements, which are sentences that are definitively either true or false. We combine and modify these statements using logical connectives, specifically and, or, and not. When we say two statements are joined by and, both must be true for the compound statement to hold. When we join them with or, the compound statement is true if at least one of the parts is true. The word not simply inverts the truth value of a statement, turning a truth into a falsehood and a falsehood into a truth.

The engine of mathematical progress is the conditional statement, often phrased as an if-then proposition. We say that if a hypothesis is true, then a conclusion must follow. Consider a simple example from algebra: if a number is divisible by ten, then it is even. Every conditional statement comes with two helpful relatives. The converse swaps the hypothesis and the conclusion, so the converse of our example would be that if a number is even, then it is divisible by ten. Notice that while our original statement is true, its converse is false, because six is even and not divisible by ten. The contrapositive swaps and negates both parts, stating that if a number is not even, then it is not divisible by ten. A foundational rule of logic is that a conditional statement and its contrapositive always share the exact same truth value. Why does this rule work? Think about what the original statement means: whenever the hypothesis is true, the conclusion is guaranteed to follow, which means there is never a scenario where the hypothesis is true and the conclusion is false. The contrapositive simply looks at the flip side of that same rule, stating that if the conclusion is false, the hypothesis could never have been true in the first place, leaving the exact same set of allowed and forbidden scenarios.

Sometimes, proving a statement false is remarkably easy because of what we call a counterexample. If someone claims that every prime number is odd, you need only point to the number two, which is prime and yet even, to shatter the claim entirely. But proving a statement true requires a systematic argument, and the most straightforward method is the direct proof. In a direct proof, we assume our hypothesis is true and use definitions, axioms, and previously established facts to march step by step toward the inevitable truth of the conclusion.

Let us walk through a classic direct proof. We will prove that the sum of any two even numbers is always an even number. We start by giving our arbitrary even numbers algebraic names, since variables let us talk about numbers in general rather than specific instances. Let our first even number be two times x, and our second even number be two times y, where x and y are integers. By definition, an integer is even if it can be expressed as two times another integer. Now we form their sum, which is two times x plus two times y. We factor out the common factor of two, writing the expression as two times the quantity x plus y. Because the sum of two integers is itself an integer, let us call that integer z, making our sum equal to two times z. Because our sum is written as two times an integer, it is by definition an even number, which completes our proof. To see this concretely, let us substitute back specific numbers, such as four and six, which are two times two and two times three. Their sum is ten, which is two times five, verifying our general argument with actual numbers.

We have now seen how logical connections build bridges from hypotheses to conclusions, and how direct arguments guarantee our results. But direct arguments do not work for every situation, and sometimes assuming the exact opposite of what we want to show is the key to unlocking a stubborn truth. In our next lesson, we will explore how to prove statements by contradiction.

## Key ideas
- A statement is a declarative sentence that is definitively either true or false, combined using the logical connectives and, or, and not.
- A conditional statement asserts that if a hypothesis is true, then a conclusion must follow, and it shares its truth value with its contrapositive because both forbid the exact same combination of a true hypothesis and a false conclusion.
- A single counterexample is enough to prove that a universal claim is false, while a direct proof must show that a claim holds for all cases.
- A direct proof begins with the assumed truth of a hypothesis and uses definitions and algebra to logically reach the conclusion, which we can check by substituting back specific numbers.
