# Secession and the Coming of War

In October 1859, a man named John Brown led a small band of armed followers into Harpers Ferry, Virginia. His goal was to seize the federal arsenal, distribute weapons to enslaved people, and ignite a widespread uprising against the institution of slavery. Brown was captured by federal troops led by Robert E. Lee, tried for treason, and executed. While many in the North initially viewed him as a dangerous fanatic, his dignity during his trial and his final words helped transform him into a martyr for the antislavery cause. For the South, the raid served as a terrifying confirmation of their deepest fear: that the North was not merely interested in political debate, but was actively plotting to destroy their social and economic order through violent insurrection.

This event deepened the chasm between the sections. By the time the election of 1860 arrived, the national political landscape had shattered. The Democratic Party split into northern and southern factions, unable to reconcile their positions on the expansion of slavery into the territories. Abraham Lincoln, the candidate for the newly formed Republican Party, campaigned on a platform that opposed the extension of slavery, though he promised not to interfere with it where it already existed. Despite his assurances, Southerners viewed his victory in November 1860 as an existential threat. Lincoln did not receive a single electoral vote from the South, signaling to many there that they had lost their voice in the federal government.

South Carolina acted first, passing an ordinance of secession in December 1860. It argued that the federal government had failed to protect the rights of slaveholding states and that the election of a president hostile to their interests rendered the Union a failure. Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas followed in quick succession. These states formed the Confederate States of America, adopting a constitution that explicitly protected the institution of slavery as a cornerstone of their new republic.

As the crisis deepened, some leaders sought a peaceful resolution. In December 1860, Senator John Crittenden proposed a series of constitutional amendments to preserve the Union by extending the old Missouri Compromise line to the Pacific, effectively protecting slavery in all current and future southern territories. In March 1861, Congress also proposed the Corwin Amendment, which would have constitutionally barred the federal government from ever interfering with slavery in the states. Both efforts failed, as neither side could agree on the fundamental issue of slavery’s expansion.

The Border States of Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri faced an agonizing choice. These slaveholding states remained in the Union, though their citizens were deeply divided. Many residents in these regions felt caught between their economic ties to the South and their political loyalty to the North. Their eventual decisions to remain in the Union were shaped by a mix of political pressure, military presence, and a desire to avoid the destruction of war.

Historians have long debated these events. In the 1930s and 1940s, some historians, often called revisionists, argued that the war was a needless conflict brought on by a blundering generation of politicians. Modern scholarship has largely moved past this, emphasizing that the conflict was rooted in irreconcilable differences over the future of slavery. By looking at the declarations of the seceding states, we see they identified the protection and expansion of slavery as the primary reason for their departure. While constitutional arguments about states' rights were frequently cited, those rights were almost exclusively invoked to defend the ownership of human beings.

The situation at Fort Sumter, in the harbor of Charleston, South Carolina, became the flashpoint for war. The fort was held by a small federal garrison under Major Robert Anderson. As supplies ran low, President Lincoln faced a dilemma. If he reinforced the fort, he risked provoking an attack. If he abandoned it, he would be conceding the legitimacy of the Confederacy. Lincoln chose to send provisions, not troops. On April 12, 1861, Confederate forces opened fire on the fort. The garrison surrendered the next day.

The firing on Fort Sumter effectively ended the period of political maneuvering. Lincoln called for seventy-five thousand volunteers to suppress the rebellion. This prompted Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, and Tennessee to secede. The crisis had reached a point of no return. What began as a debate over the expansion of slavery had transformed into a military struggle for the survival of the American nation. The question now was whether a republic built on the premise of self-government could endure when its core institution of human bondage had pushed it to the brink of dissolution.

## Key ideas

- John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry intensified southern fears of northern-led slave insurrections and polarized the nation.
- The election of 1860 served as the immediate catalyst for the secession of the Deep South, despite last-minute legislative attempts like the Crittenden Compromise and the Corwin Amendment to avert conflict.
- The Border States of Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri ultimately remained in the Union, though their populations were deeply divided over the decision.
- The Confederate States of America was founded on the explicit goal of protecting and expanding the institution of slavery, which historians identify as the central cause of the war.
- The Confederate bombardment of Fort Sumter in April 1861 transformed the political crisis into an armed military conflict.
