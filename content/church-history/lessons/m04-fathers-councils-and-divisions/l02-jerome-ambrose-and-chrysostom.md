# Jerome, Ambrose and John Chrysostom

Imagine walking into a library in the late fourth century. You would likely find rows of scrolls and heavy codices, the pages filled with Greek or perhaps a clunky, older Latin translation of the scriptures. For the Roman Empire, which was rapidly Christianizing, the lack of a standard, elegant, and accurate Latin Bible was a major problem. As we look at the period leading up to the great councils of the fifth century, we encounter three giants of the late Roman church whose lives defined how the Bible was read, how bishops wielded power, and how preachers challenged the conscience of the powerful.

Jerome is perhaps the most famous scholar of this era. A man of fiery temperament and immense intellect, he traveled from Rome to a monastic house near Bethlehem, in what is now the West Bank. He spent decades there, mastering Hebrew and Greek to produce a new Latin translation of the scriptures. This work, which eventually became known as the Vulgate, was revolutionary. By translating the Bible into the common, or vulgar, Latin of the people, Jerome ensured that the scriptures could be accessed by those living across the Western provinces of the empire. Jerome also navigated the complex issue of the biblical canon. He translated the books of the Hebrew Bible but also included the deuterocanonical books, which were later accepted by the Catholic and Orthodox traditions but became a point of contention during the Protestant Reformation. His commitment to the original languages set a standard for biblical scholarship that would endure for over a thousand years.

While Jerome was working in his study, Ambrose was at the center of the political storm in Milan, in modern-day Italy. Ambrose served as bishop from 374 to 397 CE. He had been a provincial governor before he was unexpectedly elected bishop by the people. He brought the skills of an imperial administrator to the church. He famously stood his ground against powerful emperors, most notably when he barred Emperor Theodosius from the cathedral after the emperor ordered a massacre in the city of Thessalonica, in modern-day Greece. Ambrose insisted that even the highest ruler was subject to the moral law of God and the discipline of the church. Beyond his political courage, he was a mentor to Augustine of Hippo, shaping the theological direction of the West. He also influenced Western liturgy through his development of the Ambrosian chant, which remains a part of the church's musical heritage.

In the East, the voice of the church was found in the pulpit of John Chrysostom. His name, Chrysostom, actually means golden-mouthed, a title earned by his extraordinary skill as a preacher. Based first in Antioch, in modern-day southern Turkey, and later as the Archbishop of Constantinople, in modern-day Istanbul, from 398 to 404 CE, John was a master of biblical exposition. He is remembered for his extensive homilies on the Gospel of Matthew, which were widely read in both the East and the West. He also contributed to the development of the Eastern liturgy, which is still celebrated today as the Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom.

John did not merely lecture; he applied the scriptures to the daily lives of his hearers. He spoke with biting honesty about the greed of the wealthy and the neglect of the poor. His sermons were so popular that people would often applaud in the middle of his addresses. However, he frequently tried to discourage this practice. He wanted his listeners to focus on the message rather than the messenger.

John’s commitment to moral reform often put him in danger. He criticized the luxury of the imperial court, which eventually led to his exile. His life shows the precarious balance church leaders faced when navigating the influence of the imperial throne. While Ambrose managed to maintain a working relationship with emperors while still holding them to account, John’s directness made him many enemies among the political and ecclesiastical elite. Together, these three men—Jerome, the translator; Ambrose, the statesman; and John, the preacher—shaped the intellectual and moral landscape of the church in the late Roman period. They represent the dual identity of the church at this time. It was a church that was becoming institutionalized, yet it was also deeply concerned with the personal holiness and biblical literacy of the individual believer.

## Key ideas
- Jerome produced the Vulgate, a Latin translation of the Bible that included the deuterocanonical books and served as the standard text for the Western church for over a millennium.
- Ambrose of Milan, serving as bishop from 374 to 397 CE, asserted the church’s moral independence from the state and mentored Augustine of Hippo.
- John Chrysostom, serving as Archbishop of Constantinople from 398 to 404 CE, left a lasting legacy through his homilies on the Gospel of Matthew and the development of the Eastern Divine Liturgy.
- These three figures established essential foundations for biblical scholarship, episcopal authority, and the role of the preacher in society during the final decades of the Roman Empire.
