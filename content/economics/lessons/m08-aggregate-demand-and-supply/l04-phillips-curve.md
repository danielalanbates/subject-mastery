# The Phillips Curve

Imagine you are sitting in a central bank meeting room, and the economy is struggling. Unemployment is high, and workers are anxious. The head of the bank suggests stepping on the monetary gas pedal, lowering interest rates to stimulate spending and hire back the unemployed. But a dissenting voice pipes up, warning that printing more money and heating up demand will unleash inflation, eroding everyone's purchasing power. Can you have low unemployment and low inflation at the same time, or does the economic machine force us to choose between them? That fundamental puzzle is mapped by one of the most famous and debated relationships in macroeconomics, known as the Phillips curve.

Building on our look at aggregate supply, where long-run output settles at its natural level, the Phillips curve shifts our focus to the visible tension between price stability and joblessness. In 1958, economist A.W. Phillips published a study of British wage data spanning nearly a century. He noticed a striking pattern: when unemployment was low, wage growth tended to be high. When unemployment was high, wage growth slowed or even fell. Economists quickly translated this wage trade-off into a relationship between general price inflation and unemployment. The logic seemed intuitive. If aggregate demand surges, businesses hire more workers to ramp up production, driving down unemployment. As labor markets tighten, workers demand higher wages, and firms pass those rising labor costs on to consumers through higher prices, creating inflation.

For policy makers in the 1960s, this downward-sloping curve looked like a menu of choices. Keynesian economists at the time believed governments could permanently trade a little more inflation for a permanently lower rate of unemployment, sliding up and down the curve at will. If a government wanted fewer people out of work, it simply had to accept a predictable, manageable bump in inflation. But economic reality soon shattered that illusion. During the stagflation of the 1970s, advanced economies suffered through high unemployment and high inflation simultaneously, defying the clean, inverse trade-off that the original curve promised.

To understand why the simple trade-off broke down, monetarist and neoclassical economists, notably Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps, introduced a crucial distinction between expected and unexpected inflation. Only unexpected inflation affects unemployment in the short run. Suppose a central bank expands the money supply unexpectedly. In the short run, businesses see higher demand for their specific products, hire more staff, and push unemployment down. Workers take the job, initially cheering their new paycheck, failing to notice right away that prices across the whole economy are also creeping up.

Yet people quickly catch on to the reality of inflation. Once workers and businesses factor rising prices into their daily expectations, the illusion fades. Workers demand cost-of-living adjustments, unions negotiate higher wages to protect their purchasing power, and firms adjust their pricing. The temporary drop in unemployment evaporates, leaving the economy right back at its natural rate of unemployment, but with a permanently higher rate of inflation. This insight produced the expectations-augmented Phillips curve, implying that any attempt to keep unemployment artificially below its natural rate through monetary stimulus only generates accelerating inflation over time.

In the long run, therefore, the Phillips curve is vertical. Just as neoclassical aggregate supply tells us that long-run output is determined by technology, capital, and labor rather than the price level, long-run unemployment is anchored by the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment, often called the NAIRU. Whether inflation is running at two percent or twenty percent, the economy settles at this structural rate of joblessness.

Modern economists continue to refine these models, noting that supply shocks, such as a sudden spike in global oil prices, shift the short-run Phillips curve upward. That upward shift increases both inflation and unemployment at the same time, producing the painful stagflation that baffled early policy makers. While the neat permanent trade-off of the mid-twentieth century has vanished from professional consensus, understanding how expectations and supply shocks interact remains vital for managing economic fluctuations.

## Key ideas
- The Phillips curve illustrates the empirical relationship between the rate of inflation and the rate of unemployment.
- In the short run, an inverse trade-off exists where unexpected inflation can temporarily lower unemployment, though expected inflation does not.
- In the long run, the Phillips curve is vertical at the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment, meaning monetary policy cannot permanently lower joblessness below structural limits.
- Supply shocks such as oil price spikes shift the short-run Phillips curve upward, causing the combination of high inflation and high unemployment known as stagflation.
