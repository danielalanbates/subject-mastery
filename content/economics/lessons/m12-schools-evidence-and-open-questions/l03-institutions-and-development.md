# Institutions and Economic Development

Imagine standing on a riverbank where a bustling city sits on the north shore, while the south shore holds only quiet farmland and sparse brush. A bridge crosses the water, connecting two communities that share the exact same soil, climate, and ancestral history. Yet residents on the north side earn several times the income of those on the south. Real cases exist, such as the city of Nogales, split by the border between Arizona and the Mexican state of Sonora. In the last lesson, we saw how economists use empirical methods like natural experiments to isolate causes in complex data. When we apply those methods to split cities and border regions across the globe, we discover that geography and climate alone cannot explain why some nations grow rich while others remain poor.

The missing piece of the puzzle is institutions. In the words of the economist Douglass North, institutions are the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction. Think of them as the rules of the game in a society. They include formal rules, such as written laws and court systems, as well as informal norms like trust, cultural habits, and social conventions. When economists ask why wealth varies so dramatically across the world, they look directly at whether these rules encourage investment, innovation, and trade, or whether they invite corruption, predation, and economic stagnation.

Consider the role of property rights, which secure a person's ownership over their land, physical capital, and intellectual creations. Many economists argue that intellectual property rights, such as patents and copyrights, are especially important for innovation, giving creators the legal protection they need to invest years of research into new technologies. Imagine a farmer who invests his savings to dig an irrigation ditch and plant fruit trees, knowing he will reap the harvest next year. That productive behavior requires confidence that a corrupt government or a powerful neighbor will not simply seize the land once the trees bear fruit. Secure property rights guarantee that individuals can keep the fruits of their labor. Without them, the incentive to save and invest evaporates, and an economy grinds to a halt.

Alongside property rights sits the rule of law, the principle that laws apply equally to all citizens, including the powerful and the politically connected. When contracts are reliably enforced by impartial courts, strangers can do business with one another without fear of being cheated. A small entrepreneur can sign a supply contract with a large manufacturer, trusting that if the manufacturer fails to pay, the legal system will provide a remedy. This predictability lowers transaction costs, which are the hidden frictions of doing business, and allows complex, large-scale markets to emerge.

The economists Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, who shared the 2024 Nobel prize with Simon Johnson for this work, divide institutions into two categories: inclusive and extractive. Inclusive institutions encourage broad economic participation and require political constraints on elites, such as checks and balances and a robust rule of law that limits executive power. They protect private property for the majority, enforce impartial laws, and maintain open markets where anyone can start a business. Extractive institutions, by contrast, are designed to siphon resources from the many to benefit a small ruling elite. Rather than relying on open competition, they depend on state-granted monopolies, forced labor, arbitrary taxation, and blocked market entry. Nations with inclusive institutions tend to achieve sustained economic growth, while those trapped under extractive institutions struggle with persistent poverty.

We can see the role of institutions through modern empirical examples of institutional divergence, such as the contrasting paths of North and South Korea or the former East and West Germany. In each case, populations with shared culture, history, and geography were abruptly split by political events. Where societies adopted inclusive markets and secure property rights, living standards soared. Where societies fell under centralized, extractive planning, economic decay followed. Another classic historical example can be seen in the contrasting development paths of North and South America following European colonization. In regions where indigenous populations were dense, colonizers established extractive systems centered on forced mining and large plantations, keeping wealth and political power concentrated in a few hands. In regions with sparse native populations, such as the northern English colonies, settlers built self-governing towns, secured broad property rights, and established inclusive legal systems. Those early choices set trajectories that still resonate today.

Institutions are remarkably persistent through time. Economists call this path dependence, a concept explaining why change is often difficult because existing social arrangements, legal frameworks, and power structures create an institutional lock in. Societies often remain trapped in suboptimal arrangements until a major crisis, known as a critical juncture, disrupts the old order and opens a window for reform.

Researchers measure institutional quality with indexes such as the World Bank Governance Indicators, the Heritage Foundation Index of Economic Freedom, and the Polity data series, which quantify variables like corruption control, bureaucratic quality, and political competition across countries. While mainstream economists agree that institutions matter deeply for development, debates persist about how they change and what policy levers can improve them. Some scholars argue that foreign aid and technical assistance can help build better courts and tax agencies from the outside. Others, drawing on institutional and historical economics, argue that institutions must evolve organically from within a society's own political struggles, making outside intervention difficult and prone to failure. Critics such as Jeffrey Sachs add that geography, disease and education matter more than the institutional view allows. As we move forward, we will examine the great unresolved questions that continue to challenge economists as they seek to understand the future of global prosperity.

## Key ideas
- Institutions, encompassing formal rules and informal norms, are widely seen as a primary reason why some nations grow rich while others remain poor.
- Secure property rights and intellectual property protections give individuals and inventors the essential incentive to save, invest, and innovate without fear of arbitrary seizure.
- Inclusive institutions require both economic openness and political constraints on elites, such as checks and balances and the rule of law.
- Economists measure institutional quality using empirical tools like the World Bank Governance Indicators and the Heritage Index of Economic Freedom.
- Societies often experience institutional lock in due to path dependence, making fundamental change difficult except during rare critical junctures.
