# Variables and Algebraic Expressions

Imagine you run a small bakery and charge five dollars for each loaf of bread you bake, alongside a fixed daily delivery fee of ten dollars. If you sell three loaves in a day, your total revenue is five times three plus ten, which gives twenty-five dollars. But what if you sell twelve loaves tomorrow, or fifty the day after? To capture every possible day at once, arithmetic is no longer enough because your numbers keep changing. You need a way to hold a space for a quantity that varies. That placeholder is a variable, and the phrase five times your variable plus ten is an algebraic expression.

Building on our past work with exponents and scientific notation, where we learned to manage very large and very small fixed numbers, algebra now lets us work with numbers that can take on any value. A variable is simply a letter, such as the letter x or the letter y, that represents an unspecified number. When we combine numbers, variables, and operations like addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, we form an algebraic expression. Unlike an equation, which states that two things are equal and includes an equals sign, an expression is just a mathematical phrase. It does not have an equals sign, and it cannot be solved, but it can be evaluated, simplified, and translated.

Translating spoken language into algebraic expressions is a foundational skill for modeling the world. Suppose a puzzle asks you to write an expression for three more than twice a number. If we let our variable be the letter n to stand for the unknown number, then twice a number is represented as two times n. Three more than that means we add three, giving us the expression two times n plus three. Going the other way, when working with the expression four times the quantity x minus five, you can read it aloud as four multiplied by the difference between a number and five. Notice how grouping words like the quantity are essential so that the subtraction happens before the multiplication.

Often, we want to know the value of an expression when our variable takes on a specific number. This process is called evaluating the expression. Consider the expression three times the quantity x plus four minus two times x, and let us evaluate it when x is equal to five. First, we substitute the number five in place of every occurrence of the letter x. Inside the grouping, five plus four is nine, so the first part becomes three times nine, which is twenty-seven. Then, the second part becomes two times five, which is ten. Subtracting ten from twenty-seven leaves us with seventeen. We have successfully evaluated our expression for a single value.

What if we want to make the expression shorter before we substitute anything? That brings us to combining like terms. Terms are the individual parts of an expression separated by addition or subtraction. Like terms share the exact same variable parts raised to the exact same powers. For instance, in the expression six times y squared plus three times y minus two times y squared plus five, the terms six times y squared and negative two times y squared are like terms. To combine them, we keep the variable part identical and add or subtract their coefficients, which are the numbers multiplying the variables. Here, six times y squared minus two times y squared combines to make four times y squared. The term three times y has no partner, and the number five is a constant with no variable. So our fully simplified expression is four times y squared plus three times y plus five. By merging what is alike, we strip away clutter and reveal the underlying structure of the mathematics, preparing the way to perform arithmetic on entire polynomials in our next lesson.

## Key ideas
- A variable is a letter used to represent an unspecified number, allowing us to describe general patterns rather than single arithmetic calculations.
- An algebraic expression is a combination of numbers, variables, and operations without an equals sign, serving as a mathematical phrase rather than a statement to solve.
- Evaluating an expression means substituting a specific numerical value for each variable and following the order of operations to find a final result.
- Combining like terms allows us to simplify expressions by adding or subtracting the coefficients of terms that share identical variable parts.
