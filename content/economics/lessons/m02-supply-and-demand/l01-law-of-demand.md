# The Law of Demand

Imagine walking into your favorite local café tomorrow morning, only to find that the price of a standard cup of coffee has jumped from three dollars to six dollars. What do you do? You might decide to buy a smaller size, switch to tea, or skip your morning caffeine boost altogether. This everyday reaction illustrates a fundamental principle of economics. In our last lesson, we saw how specialization and trade lead to the creation of markets where buyers and sellers interact. Today, we focus on the buyer’s side of that equation. We are exploring the Law of Demand, which explains how consumers react to price changes and what causes their overall buying habits to shift.

At its core, the Law of Demand states that, other things being equal, as the price of a good or service rises, the quantity demanded of that good or service falls. Conversely, as the price drops, the quantity demanded increases. Economists use the Latin phrase ceteris paribus, meaning "all other things held constant," to isolate this relationship. We assume for a moment that your income, your tastes, and the prices of other goods remain completely unchanged. Under these conditions, price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions. Two forces drive this. When a good becomes more expensive, people switch to cheaper substitutes, and the higher price also makes their income stretch less far. Economists call these the substitution effect and the income effect. Drawn as a graph with price going up the side and quantity along the bottom, this inverse relationship gives the demand curve its downward slope.

It is crucial to distinguish between a change in quantity demanded and a change in demand. A change in quantity demanded is a simple movement along the existing demand curve. This movement is caused solely by a change in the price of the good itself. For example, if the price of a coffee drops from three dollars to two dollars, you move down and to the right along your demand curve, increasing your quantity demanded from, say, five cups a week to seven cups. Nothing about your underlying desire for coffee has changed; you are simply responding to a lower price.

But what happens when those "other things" we held constant actually do change? When factors other than the price of the good itself alter consumer behavior, the entire demand curve shifts. A shift means that at every possible price, consumers are now willing to buy more or less of the product than before. If demand increases, the entire curve shifts to the right. If demand decreases, it shifts to the left. Economists track several primary drivers of these shifts, starting with consumer income.

How income affects your demand depends on the type of good. For most items, which we call normal goods, an increase in income leads to an increase in demand. If you get a raise at work, you might buy more fresh steak and fewer canned beans. The demand curve for steak shifts to the right. However, for some items, known as inferior goods, an increase in income actually causes demand to fall, shifting the curve to the left. Instant noodles and public bus rides are classic examples of inferior goods; as people earn more, they tend to substitute them with more expensive alternatives.

A second shifter is consumer tastes and preferences. If a major health study reveals that drinking coffee daily significantly improves memory, people’s preference for coffee will rise, shifting the demand curve to the right, even if the price stays the same. Related goods also shift demand, and they fall into two categories: substitutes and complements. Substitutes are goods used in place of one another, like coffee and tea. If the price of tea skyrockets, people will buy less tea and more coffee, shifting the demand curve for coffee to the right. Complements are goods used together, like coffee and cream. If the price of cream doubles, you might buy less coffee, shifting the coffee demand curve to the left.

Finally, buyer expectations about the future can shift current demand. If you expect the price of coffee to double next week due to a predicted harvest freeze, you will likely run to the grocery store to stock up today. This expectation shifts your current demand curve for coffee to the right, before any actual price change has occurred.

By understanding these dynamics, we can see how buyers constantly navigate changing prices and shifting circumstances. But demand is only half of the market equation. To understand how market prices are ultimately settled, we must look at the behavior of the people on the other side of the counter. In our next lesson, we will turn our attention to the sellers and examine the Law of Supply.

## Key ideas
- The Law of Demand states that, all else being equal, price and quantity demanded have an inverse relationship.
- A change in the price of a good itself causes a movement along the existing demand curve, which represents a change in the quantity demanded.
- A shift of the entire demand curve is caused by changes in factors other than the good's own price, such as income, tastes, the prices of related goods, and expectations.
- Normal goods experience an increase in demand when consumer income rises, while inferior goods experience a decrease in demand when income rises.
- Substitutes are goods that can replace one another, whereas complements are goods that are consumed together, and price changes in one will shift the demand curve of the other.
