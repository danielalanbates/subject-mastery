# Refraction and Lenses

Imagine looking down at a straight metal rod half-submerged in a clear pool of water. To your eye, the rod appears sharply bent at the surface, as if a hand has snapped it. Of course, when you reach in and pull the rod out, it is completely straight. In our previous lesson, we explored how light bounces off smooth boundaries through reflection. Today, we turn to what happens when light crosses a boundary rather than bouncing off it. We study refraction, the phenomenon where light changes direction as it passes from one transparent medium into another, and how we shape glass to bend light with purpose in lenses.

When a beam of light travels through empty space or air, it moves at a blistering top speed of about three hundred million meters per second. But when that beam strikes a denser material, such as water or window glass, it interacts with the microscopic structure of the substance and slows down. Light in water travels at roughly three quarters of its speed in a vacuum. This change in speed is the root cause of refraction. If the light strikes the boundary straight on at a right angle, it continues along the same path, just moving slower. But if the light hits the boundary at an angle, one side of the light wave enters the slower medium first. That side slows down while the other side races ahead briefly in the faster medium, causing the entire wave front to pivot. The ray bends.

Willebrord Snellius, a seventeenth-century Dutch mathematician, worked out the precise mathematical relationship governing this bending, known today as Snell's law. He was not the first: the Persian scholar Ibn Sahl described the same rule more than six centuries earlier, and the English astronomer Thomas Harriot found it independently about two decades before Snellius. The law relates the angle of the incoming ray in the first medium and the angle of the refracted ray in the second medium to the ratio of the speeds of light in the two substances. We capture this property of materials through the index of refraction, which is simply the speed of light in a vacuum divided by the speed of light in that specific material. Air has an index very close to one. Water has an index of about one point three three, and common crown glass sits near one point five. Higher indices mean slower speeds and sharper bending.

When light travels from a medium with a higher index of refraction to one with a lower index, such as from water into air, the ray bends away from the perpendicular line. If you increase the angle of the incoming ray beyond a certain threshold called the critical angle, the refracted ray cannot escape into the air. Instead, it reflects entirely back into the water, a phenomenon known as total internal reflection that keeps fiber optic cables glowing brightly from end to end.

Suppose a beam of light travels through air and strikes a flat pane of crown glass at an angle of incidence measuring thirty degrees relative to the line perpendicular to the surface. Because glass has an index of one point five, the light slows down and bends inward toward that perpendicular line, emerging inside the glass at an angle of roughly twenty degrees. If the light then exits the other side of the glass back into the air, it bends outward by the exact same amount, returning to its original direction of travel while shifting slightly sideways.

This bending becomes wonderfully useful when we shape the boundaries of transparent materials into curves, creating lenses. A convex lens bulges outward in the center, much like two opposite-facing bowls glued together at their rims. It has a positive focal length, meaning its focal length is treated as a positive number in calculations. When parallel rays of light strike a convex lens, every ray hits a slightly different angle of the curved glass surface. The rays striking the outer edges bend inward more sharply than the rays striking the flat center. This guides the entire bundle of parallel rays to converge at a single point on the opposite side, known as the focal point. Depending on where you place an object relative to this focal point, a convex lens can form a real image that can be projected onto a screen and is upside down, or a virtual image that appears right side up and cannot be projected.

Conversely, a concave lens curves inward, thinning at the middle and thick at the edges. It has a negative focal length, meaning its focal length is treated as a negative number. Instead of focusing light to a real point, a concave lens causes parallel rays to spread apart, diverging as if they originated from a single hidden point behind the lens. A concave lens always forms upright virtual images that appear smaller than the actual object.

To calculate precisely where these images form, physicists use the thin lens equation. This equation states that one divided by the object distance plus one divided by the image distance equals one divided by the focal length. By combining convex and concave surfaces, opticians and physicists can magnify tiny cells under a microscope, capture distant galaxies in a telescope, or correct the focal quirks of a human eye.

## Key ideas
- Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one transparent medium into another due to a change in the speed of light.
- Snell law and the index of refraction mathematically relate the bending angles to the speeds of light in different media, leading to total internal reflection when light strikes a boundary beyond the critical angle.
- Convex lenses have a positive focal length and can form real or virtual images depending on the object distance, while concave lenses have a negative focal length and always form upright virtual images.
- The thin lens equation relates object distance, image distance, and focal length to predict where images will form.
