# Glycolysis and the Citric Acid Cycle

Imagine a bustling municipal power plant that does not burn coal all at once, but instead disassembles trainloads of raw fuel piece by piece, pocketing the valuable high-energy electrons at every step. In our last lesson, we saw how adenosine triphosphate acts as the immediate energy currency of the cell, and how enzymes lower activation barriers to make cellular chemistry possible. Today, we trace the precise, step-by-step demolition of a single glucose molecule to see how cells actually manufacture the raw ingredients that drive that energy economy. We are diving into glycolysis and the citric acid cycle.

Our story begins in the cytoplasm, the jelly-like interior of the cell, with glycolysis. The word literally means the splitting of sugar. Glucose, a stable six-carbon sugar that forms a ring in water, enters the cell from your bloodstream. The cell does not immediately harvest energy from it; instead, it invests a little bit of capital. It spends two molecules of adenosine triphosphate to phosphorylate the glucose, adding phosphate groups that destabilize the molecule and trap it inside the cell boundary.

From the perspective of evolutionary history, glycolysis is an ancient and nearly universal pathway. Because it requires no oxygen and takes place in the cytoplasm, it predates both mitochondria and oxygenic photosynthesis, serving as a metabolic heritage shared across nearly all living domains.

Once primed, this unstable six-carbon intermediate splits right down the middle into two three-carbon molecules. Now the payoff begins. Through a series of enzyme-catalyzed steps, these three-carbon fragments are oxidized. Oxidation, as you may recall, means the loss of electrons. As these fragments are reshaped, enzymes strip away high-energy electrons along with hydrogen ions and hand them off to a vital carrier molecule called nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. When this carrier accepts those electrons, it becomes reduced into NADH. Think of NADH as a fully charged battery holding high-energy cargo, ready to deliver it downstream.

During this oxidation process, enough free energy is released to directly forge a few molecules of adenosine triphosphate through what we call substrate-level phosphorylation. By the time glycolysis finishes, that single six-carbon glucose has been transformed into two three-carbon molecules of pyruvate. The net yield looks modest: two molecules of adenosine triphosphate and two molecules of reduced NADH per glucose. But the pyruvate still holds the vast majority of the original chemical energy.

Next, that pyruvate must cross the double membrane into the mitochondrion, the powerhouse of the cell. The mitochondrial location of the citric acid cycle reflects the endosymbiotic origin of eukaryotes, tracing back to an ancient bacterial ancestor that took up residence inside another cell. In the mitochondrial matrix, a transition enzyme complex strips off one carbon atom from each pyruvate, releasing it as waste carbon dioxide and loading one more NADH. The remaining two-carbon fragment binds to a carrier molecule called coenzyme A, forming acetyl coenzyme A, or simply acetyl CoA. This compound serves as the key entry ticket into the next major stage of cellular metabolism: the citric acid cycle.

The citric acid cycle, also known as the Krebs cycle after its discoverer Hans Krebs, is a closed loop of enzymatic reactions. Acetyl CoA donates its two-carbon acetyl group to a four-carbon molecule already waiting in the cycle, creating a six-carbon molecule called citrate. Over the next several steps of a single turn, this citrate is systematically dismantled, oxidized, and rearranged back into that original four-carbon starting molecule, called oxaloacetate.

As the cycle spins, enzymes strip away more electrons, loading up additional electron carriers including several more molecules of NADH and a related carrier called flavin adenine dinucleotide, or FADH2. A bit more adenosine triphosphate is synthesized directly by substrate-level phosphorylation, and the remaining carbon atoms of the original glucose are completely exhaled away as carbon dioxide molecules.

Step back and look at what has been accomplished. The cell has taken a stable sugar molecule and completely dismantled it. The direct yield of adenosine triphosphate from both glycolysis and the citric acid cycle is remarkably small. The true triumph of these pathways is the massive stockpile of reduced electron carriers, NADH and FADH2, now swimming with high-energy electrons. In our next lesson, we will follow those loaded carriers to the inner mitochondrial membrane, where their cargo powers the grand finale of cellular respiration and produces the vast majority of your cellular energy.

## Key ideas
- Glycolysis is an ancient, universally conserved pathway taking place in the cytoplasm that splits a six-carbon glucose ring into two three-carbon pyruvate molecules, yielding a net total of two adenosine triphosphate and two NADH molecules.
- Substrate-level phosphorylation is the direct enzymatic transfer of a phosphate group to adenosine diphosphate, producing adenosine triphosphate without the need for a proton gradient.
- Pyruvate is transported into the mitochondrial matrix, reflecting the endosymbiotic origin of eukaryotes, where it is converted into acetyl CoA while releasing carbon dioxide and generating additional electron carriers.
- The citric acid cycle operates in a continuous loop within the mitochondria, fully oxidizing the remaining carbon fragments into carbon dioxide while transferring high-energy electrons to NADH and FADH2.
