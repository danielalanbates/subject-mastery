# Exchange Rates and Capital Flows

Imagine you are standing at a currency exchange counter in London, swapping your dollars for British pounds. The screen says one pound costs one dollar and thirty cents. Why is it not one to one? Why does that price tick up and down every single day, sometimes by fractions of a cent, sometimes by entire pennies? The price of a currency is not set by a government committee or a central bank decree, at least not most of the time. It is set in the vast, churning waters of the foreign exchange market, where trillions of dollars change hands around the clock, driven by the constant global demand for goods, services, and investments.

In our last lesson, we looked at the balance of payments, tracking the broad ledger of a nation's trade and financial transactions with the rest of the world. Today, we zoom in on the specific mechanism that connects those transactions: the exchange rate, which is simply the price of one currency expressed in terms of another. This is our nominal exchange rate. But economists also look at the real exchange rate, which adjusts the nominal rate by the relative price levels in two countries, telling us how many goods and services from one country can be exchanged for goods and services from another.

When you buy a foreign currency, you are participating in the largest financial market on Earth. Like any market we have studied, exchange rates are ultimately determined by supply and demand. If more people want to buy British goods or invest in British businesses, they need pounds. That surge in demand pushes up the price of the pound relative to the dollar. We say the pound appreciates, meaning it gains value, while the dollar depreciates, meaning it loses value.

Why do these exchange rates move so dramatically? In the short run, the primary engine of movement is international capital flows. Investors constantly hunt for the highest risk-adjusted returns across the globe. If interest rates rise in the United States relative to Europe, global investors rush to snap up American bonds and other financial assets. To buy those American assets, they must first sell euros to buy dollars. That massive influx of demand strengthens the dollar. Conversely, if a country experiences political instability or inflation that erodes the purchasing power of its money, investors pull their capital out, flooding the market with that currency and causing its exchange rate to plummet.

Over the long run, however, economists rely on the concept of purchasing power parity to explain exchange rates. This principle suggests that a nominal exchange rate between two currencies should adjust so that an identical basket of goods costs the exact same amount in both countries, neutralizing differences in national price levels. In practice, purchasing power parity holds only roughly, because many goods and services are not traded and deviations can last for years, so economists treat it as a long-run anchor rather than a forecast.

These currency movements instantly ripple through international trade, reshaping the choices of consumers and firms. When a currency appreciates, its goods become more expensive for foreign buyers, which can hurt exports. At the same time, foreign goods become cheaper for domestic consumers, which boosts imports. Think about what happens to an American manufacturer when the dollar strengthens. Suddenly, their machinery and labor costs look high to international buyers, and foreign competitors can undercut their prices at home. On the flip side, a depreciating currency acts like a natural discount on a country's exports, helping local factories sell more abroad while making imported raw materials pricier.

Governments and central banks sometimes try to manage these turbulent waters. Under a floating exchange rate system, market forces of supply and demand are left in the driver seat, allowing currencies to drift up and down freely. Under a fixed or pegged exchange rate system, a government officially ties the value of its currency to another major currency, like the United States dollar, or to a basket of currencies. To maintain that fixed price, the central bank must constantly stand ready to buy or sell its own currency using its foreign exchange reserves, stepping in whenever market pressures threaten to break the peg. While fixed rates can provide stability for international trade and tame wild speculation, they require immense discipline and can leave a country vulnerable if its economic fundamentals diverge from the currency it is pegged to.

Ultimately, exchange rates and capital flows act as the nervous system of the global economy, transmitting shocks, rewarding productivity, and balancing the books between nations. As we turn our attention to the broader social and economic impacts of these integrated markets, we will examine the fierce debates surrounding globalization and its critics.

## Key ideas
- An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, distinguished between nominal rates and real rates that account for relative price levels.
- A currency appreciates when its value rises against others, making its exports more expensive and imports cheaper, while depreciation has the opposite effect.
- International capital flows, driven by shifting global interest rates and investor sentiment, are the primary engine of short-term exchange rate movements.
- Purchasing power parity serves as a key benchmark for understanding how exchange rates adjust over the long run to equalize the cost of baskets of goods across nations.
- Under a floating exchange rate system, market forces dictate currency values, whereas a fixed system requires central bank intervention to maintain a set price peg.
