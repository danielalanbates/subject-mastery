# Byzantium and Iconoclasm

Imagine walking into a church where the walls are entirely bare. There are no paintings of saints, no golden mosaics shimmering in the candlelight, and no wooden panels depicting the life of Christ. For many Christians today, this feels like a minimalist choice. But in the Byzantine Empire of the eighth century, this was a matter of intense, state-sponsored controversy that divided the church for over a hundred years. This lesson turns to the heart of the Byzantine Empire, centered in Constantinople, in what is now Istanbul, Turkey, to explore the fierce debate over the use of icons in worship.

An icon is a sacred image, usually painted on wood, representing Christ, the Virgin Mary, or the saints. For Byzantine Christians, these were not merely decorations. They were windows into the divine, meant to draw the worshipper’s mind toward the reality of the person depicted. During the early 700s, a movement known as Iconoclasm, or image-breaking, gained momentum. Some scholars suggest that the rise of Islamic aniconism, which strictly avoids images in religious contexts, may have influenced Byzantine concerns about the purity of their own worship. Emperor Leo the Third, fearing that the veneration of icons had slipped into idolatry, ordered their removal and destruction. To the iconoclasts, the use of images violated the Second Commandment. They argued that because Christ is both human and divine, it is impossible to represent him in paint and wood without either separating his natures or falsely limiting his divinity.

On the other side were the Iconodules, or venerators of images. Their most famous defender was John of Damascus, a monk living under Islamic rule. He argued that the incarnation of Christ changed everything. Since God had become human and taken on a physical body, he had made himself visible. Therefore, painting an image of Christ was a way to honor his humanity. The iconodules articulated a vital theological distinction: worship, or latreia, is reserved for God alone, while veneration, or proskynesis, is the honor shown to the person represented by the image. Monastic defenders, such as Theodore the Studite, played a crucial role in resisting imperial iconoclasm, often suffering exile for their insistence that the church, not the emperor, must define the boundaries of faith.

The conflict reached a turning point in 787 CE. Empress Irene convened the Second Council of Nicaea. The bishops affirmed that icons were essential to the faith, declaring that the honor paid to an image passes to the one it represents. Pope Adrian the First accepted these decrees, strengthening the bond between Rome and the East. However, the controversy reignited in the early 800s under emperors Leo the Fifth, Michael the Second, and Theophilus. This second wave of iconoclasm brought renewed persecution against those who kept icons.

The final resolution came in 843 CE under Empress Theodora. This event is remembered as the Triumph of Orthodoxy. Today, it is celebrated annually on the first Sunday of Great Lent, featuring a solemn procession of icons and the reading of the Synodikon, a document affirming the council's decrees. The victory of the iconodules paved the way for the Macedonian Renaissance, a period of brilliant artistic achievement that produced the iconic mosaics and frescoes defining Byzantine beauty.

This controversy also deepened the divide between East and West. While the Roman papacy supported the iconodules, the imperial attempts to enforce iconoclasm from Constantinople created political and theological friction. These growing tensions over authority and doctrine eventually contributed to the Great Schism of 1054. Despite these divisions, the triumph of the icons solidified a visual and sensory expression of faith that would characterize the Eastern Church for the next millennium, setting the stage for the Byzantine missionary expansion into the lands of the north.

## Key ideas
- Iconoclasm was a movement within the Byzantine Empire that sought to destroy religious images, potentially influenced by the aniconic practices of neighboring cultures.
- Iconodules distinguished between the worship of God, known as latreia, and the veneration of images, known as proskynesis, arguing that the incarnation of Christ made his depiction possible.
- Monastic leaders like Theodore the Studite and the support of the Roman papacy under Pope Adrian the First were instrumental in defending the use of icons against imperial suppression.
- The Triumph of Orthodoxy in 843 CE ended the second wave of iconoclasm and remains a major liturgical celebration in the Orthodox Church today.
- The prolonged conflict over icons intensified the cultural and political alienation between the Eastern and Western churches, contributing to the eventual Schism of 1054.
