# Integrated Rate Laws and Half-Life

Imagine you are watching a pot of water boil. If you track the temperature, it rises steadily until it hits the boiling point. Now, imagine you are watching a chemical reaction unfold. Unlike the temperature of that boiling water, the speed of a reaction rarely stays the same. As the reactants get used up, there are fewer molecules left to collide, so the reaction typically slows down. In our last lesson, we looked at rate laws, which tell us how the speed of a reaction depends on concentration at any single moment. Today, we go a step further to ask a different question. Instead of asking how fast the reaction is going right now, we want to know exactly how much reactant will remain after a specific amount of time has passed.

To answer this, we use integrated rate laws. These are mathematical equations that relate the concentration of a reactant to the time elapsed since the reaction began. The specific form of this equation depends on the order of the reaction. Chemists identify the reaction order by creating linearized plots. For a zero order reaction, the rate is constant, so a plot of concentration versus time yields a straight line with a negative slope. The units for the rate constant in a zero order reaction are moles per liter per second. For a first order reaction, the integrated rate law says that the natural logarithm of the concentration at any time equals the natural logarithm of the starting concentration minus the rate constant times the time. So a plot of the natural logarithm of the concentration versus time yields a straight line whose slope is the negative of the rate constant, and the rate constant has units of per second. For a second order reaction, plotting the reciprocal of the concentration versus time yields a straight line with a positive slope, and the rate constant has units of liters per mole per second.

The concept of half-life is a vital tool for understanding these kinetics. The half-life is the time it takes for the concentration of a reactant to drop to exactly half of its initial value. For a first order reaction, the half-life is constant. It does not matter if you start with one mole per liter or ten moles per liter; the time it takes to reach fifty percent of that starting amount remains exactly the same. You calculate this by dividing the natural logarithm of two, which is approximately zero point six nine three, by the rate constant.

Let us look at an example. Suppose a first order reaction has a rate constant of zero point zero seven per minute. To find the half-life, we divide zero point six nine three by zero point zero seven. This gives us a half-life of approximately nine point nine minutes. If you start with a concentration of zero point four moles per liter, after nine point nine minutes, you will have zero point two moles per liter. After another nine point nine minutes, that amount is halved again to zero point one moles per liter.

Unlike first order reactions, the half-life of zero and second order reactions depends on the initial concentration. For a zero order reaction, the half-life is the initial concentration divided by two times the rate constant. This means the half-life gets shorter as the reaction progresses and the reactant is consumed. For a second order reaction, the half-life is one divided by the product of the rate constant and the initial concentration. In this case, the half-life actually gets longer as the reaction proceeds. This predictability is why these models are so useful in fields like medicine and environmental science. If a drug has a known half-life in your bloodstream, doctors can reliably calculate how long it takes for the drug to clear your system. Understanding these mathematical models allows us to predict the future of a reaction, but to truly understand why these rates differ so drastically, we must look at the molecules themselves and how they physically collide to break bonds.

## Key ideas

- Integrated rate laws allow us to calculate the exact concentration of a reactant at any given time during a reaction.
- Chemists determine reaction order by identifying which plot—concentration, natural log of concentration, or reciprocal of concentration versus time—yields a straight line.
- The half-life of a first order reaction is constant, while the half-lives of zero and second order reactions depend on the initial concentration of the reactant.
- The units of the rate constant change depending on the reaction order, reflecting the different mathematical relationships between concentration and time.
