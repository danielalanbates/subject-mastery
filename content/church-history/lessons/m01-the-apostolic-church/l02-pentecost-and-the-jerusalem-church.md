# Pentecost and the Jerusalem Church

Imagine a crowded city street in Jerusalem during the Feast of Weeks, or Shavuot. It is a time when thousands of Jewish pilgrims from across the Mediterranean world have traveled to the temple to celebrate the harvest and the giving of the Law at Mount Sinai. Amidst this bustling sea of languages, a small group of followers of Jesus experiences a profound transformation. They are filled with the Holy Spirit, fulfilling the promise Jesus made to his disciples that they would receive power to be his witnesses, as recorded in the first chapter of the book of Acts. This power enables them to speak in languages they have never learned, causing a stir among the diverse crowds.

The reaction of the onlookers is mixed. Some are amazed, while others mock the believers, accusing them of being drunk on new wine. In response, Peter stands up to address the crowd. He explains that this is not intoxication but the fulfillment of the prophecy of Joel, who wrote in the Old Testament that God would pour out his spirit upon all people in the last days. Peter quotes Joel chapter two, verses twenty-eight through thirty-two, framing the events of that day as the arrival of the messianic age. He calls the people to repent and be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of their sins. The impact is immediate. About three thousand persons are baptized and added to the community that day, marking the birth of the early church.

The community that emerges is defined by a radical sense of shared life. Acts describes a group that gathers daily in the temple courts and breaks bread in their homes. This is not merely a social experiment; it is an expression of their conviction that the reign of God has arrived. They sell their possessions and distribute the proceeds to anyone in need, ensuring that no one among them goes hungry. Yet, this life of communal sharing faces its first internal test with the story of Ananias and Sapphira. Their attempt to deceive the community regarding their contribution illustrates the seriousness of honesty and integrity within this new, Spirit-led fellowship.

While they remain deeply rooted in Jewish tradition, their worship takes on a distinct character. They continue to observe the daily prayers of the temple, yet they add the breaking of bread. This practice, performed in memory of Jesus, becomes the central ritual of their gathering. For these early Christians, this is not a departure from their ancestral faith, but its culmination. They see themselves as the faithful remnant of Israel.

Leadership in this early church is collaborative, centering first on the apostles, with Peter as their spokesman; later James, the brother of Jesus, becomes the leading figure in Jerusalem. As the community grows, they appoint seven men to handle the practical needs of the congregation, particularly the fair distribution of food to widows. The community also faces external pressure. Even before the seven are chosen, the apostles Peter and John have been arrested by the Temple authorities, who view their message as a threat to public order. This tension escalates, culminating in the martyrdom of Stephen, the first Christian to die for his faith. Stephen’s death triggers a wave of persecution that forces many believers to scatter from Jerusalem. While this scattering is a time of great suffering, it also serves as the catalyst that sets the stage for the mission to spread beyond the city walls into the wider world.

The Jerusalem community serves as the heart of the movement for the first years, but it is not intended to remain a local phenomenon. The spirit that descended at Pentecost pushes the boundaries of the faith. As the community faces these internal and external challenges, a new figure begins to emerge from the fringes, someone whose background and training will fundamentally change the trajectory of the mission to the Gentile world.

## Key ideas
- The events of Pentecost fulfill the promise of the Holy Spirit, transforming the disciples into public witnesses and initiating the rapid growth of the church through Peter's sermon and the baptism of three thousand people.
- The early Jerusalem church practiced a radical form of communal living, characterized by shared property and a commitment to honesty, as seen in the sobering account of Ananias and Sapphira.
- Worship in the first community combined traditional Jewish temple practices with the distinct, new ritual of breaking bread in memory of Jesus.
- Leadership in the Jerusalem church was collaborative, involving the apostles and appointed assistants who managed both the spiritual teaching and the practical welfare of the group.
- External persecution, beginning with the arrest of the apostles and culminating in the martyrdom of Stephen, forced the believers to scatter, thereby initiating the expansion of the faith beyond Jerusalem.
