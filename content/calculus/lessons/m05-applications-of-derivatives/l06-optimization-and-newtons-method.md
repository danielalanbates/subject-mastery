# Optimization and Newton's Method

Imagine standing at the edge of a large farm with one hundred yards of fencing. You want to build a rectangular garden against a long, straight stone barn wall that needs no fence on that side. How should you shape the rectangle to enclose the largest possible patch of green? If you make it very long and thin, like a ribbon, you enclose almost nothing. If you make it short and wide, you also limit your area. Somewhere in between lies a sweet spot. Finding that maximum area without guessing is one of the most powerful uses of calculus. It allows us to balance competing constraints and squeeze the absolute best outcome from a real-world problem.

In our previous lesson, we used limits to untangle messy indeterminate forms with L'Hôpital's rule. Now, we turn our attention from limits of fractions to practical extremes. Optimization problems ask us to find the absolute maximum or minimum value of a function over a given domain. To solve them, we first translate a physical situation into a mathematical equation. We write a function for the quantity we want to maximize or minimize, making sure it depends on only one variable by using any given constraints. Then, we find the derivative of that function, set it equal to zero to locate the critical points, and test those points. To ensure we find the absolute maximum or minimum, we must also check the endpoints of the domain.

Let us return to our garden with one hundred yards of fencing. Let the side parallel to the stone barn have a length we will call the variable letter w for width. The two fences running perpendicular from the barn out to the edge will each have a length we will call the variable letter h for height. The total fence used is the quantity two times h plus w, which equals one hundred. We want to maximize the area, which is the product of h and w. From our fencing constraint, we can express the height as fifty minus one half of the variable letter w. Substitute this expression into our area function so that the area depends only on w. The area function is now w multiplied by the quantity fifty minus one half of w, which expands to fifty w minus one half of w squared.

To find the critical point, we take the derivative of this area function with respect to w. The derivative of fifty w minus one half of w squared is fifty minus w. Setting this derivative equal to zero gives us our critical value: the variable letter w must equal fifty. To check if this gives a maximum, we look at the second derivative of our area function, which is negative one. Because the second derivative is always negative, the graph of our area function curves downward everywhere like an upside down bowl, meaning this critical point must be an absolute maximum. When the width is fifty yards, the height is twenty-five yards, and the maximum enclosed area is 1,250 square yards.

Beyond maximizing shapes, calculus also helps us solve equations that resist traditional algebra. Sometimes, finding where a function crosses zero is impossible with standard factoring or quadratic formulas. For these stubborn equations, we approximate roots of equations of the form f of x equals zero using a brilliant numerical technique developed by Isaac Newton, known as Newton's method. Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz shared a famously bitter priority dispute over the invention of calculus. Newton favored dot notations for rates, while Leibniz gave us the fractional d x notation we still use today. Newton's method uses tangent lines to zoom in on an invisible root step by step.

Suppose we want to find where the function f of x equals zero. We start with a rough guess, which we can call the variable letter x sub zero. We evaluate the function and its derivative at that guess. We then find the place where the tangent line to the curve at our guess crosses the horizontal axis. That intersection gives us a much better second guess, which we call the variable letter x sub one. The formula for this new guess is our old guess minus the fraction whose numerator is f evaluated at the old guess and whose denominator is f prime evaluated at the old guess. By repeating this process, generating a sequence of better and better approximations, we can pinpoint roots to any desired accuracy with astonishing speed. For example, to approximate the square root of two, take f of x equals x squared minus two, whose derivative is two x. From the guess one, the function value is negative one and the derivative is two, so the next guess is one minus negative one half, which is one point five. One more step subtracts the new function value, zero point two five, divided by the new derivative, three, from one point five, giving about one point four one six seven, already close to the true value of about one point four one four two.

Newton's method exhibits quadratic convergence, meaning the number of correct decimal places roughly doubles with each step, giving it a massive advantage over simpler iteration methods. Yet it requires care. The method may fail if the function lacks a continuous derivative or if the derivative at the current guess is zero, which causes a division by zero error. Success also depends on choosing a good initial guess that lies close enough to the true root. Otherwise, the tangent lines may send our successive guesses ricocheting away from the solution instead of converging toward it.

As we look toward the horizon of our calculus journey, these tools of finding extremes and approximating values prepare us to reverse our thinking entirely. We will soon move from rates of change back toward accumulated totals as we cross the threshold into the study of the integral.

## Key ideas
- Optimization problems require translating real-world constraints into a single-variable function, testing its critical points, and checking domain endpoints to find absolute maximums or minimums.
- The second derivative test helps confirm whether a critical point in an optimization problem is indeed a maximum or a minimum.
- Newton's method approximates roots of equations of the form f of x equals zero by using tangent lines and successive approximations, though it requires a good initial guess, a non-zero derivative, and continuous derivatives to avoid divergence or failure.
- The quadratic convergence property of Newton's method allows it to zoom in on roots with astonishing speed compared to simpler iteration methods.
- Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz engaged in a bitter priority dispute over calculus, leaving behind distinct notations that both enrich modern mathematics.
