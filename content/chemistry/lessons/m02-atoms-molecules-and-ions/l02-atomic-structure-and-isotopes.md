# Atomic Structure and Isotopes

Imagine you are holding a tiny, invisible grain of carbon. If you could zoom in far enough to see the individual atoms, you would find they are not solid, featureless spheres. Instead, each atom is a complex, busy system of subatomic particles. Every atom is built from three fundamental particles: protons, neutrons, and electrons. To measure these, we use the atomic mass unit, which is defined as one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-twelve atom. On this scale, a proton has a mass of approximately one atomic mass unit, and a neutron also has a mass of approximately one atomic mass unit. An electron is much smaller, with a mass of approximately one divided by one thousand eight hundred thirty-six atomic mass units.

At the very center of the atom sits the nucleus. This is a tiny, dense core that holds almost all of the atom’s mass. Within this nucleus, you find protons and neutrons. A proton carries a positive electrical charge of positive one. A neutron is neutral, meaning it has no charge at all. Surrounding this nucleus is a vast, mostly empty space occupied by electrons. An electron carries a negative electrical charge of negative one. In a neutral atom, the number of negatively charged electrons perfectly balances the number of positively charged protons.

The identity of an element is determined entirely by its number of protons. We call this the atomic number. If an atom has six protons, it is carbon. If it has eight, it is oxygen. The atomic number is like a fingerprint for the element. However, the number of neutrons can vary. Atoms of the same element that have different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes. Because they have the same number of protons, isotopes behave identically in chemical reactions, but they differ in mass.

To distinguish between isotopes, we use the mass number, which is the sum of the protons and neutrons in the nucleus. The mass number is always an integer. In contrast, the actual isotopic mass of a specific atom is a precise value measured in atomic mass units that is very close to, but not exactly, an integer. For example, the most common form of carbon has six protons and six neutrons, giving it a mass number of twelve. Another isotope, carbon thirteen, has six protons and seven neutrons.

If you look at a periodic table, you will notice that the atomic masses are rarely whole numbers. They are often decimals, like twelve point zero one atomic mass units for carbon or sixteen point zero zero atomic mass units for oxygen. This is because the value listed is the weighted average of all naturally occurring isotopes for that element. To calculate this average atomic mass, you multiply the isotopic mass of each isotope by its natural abundance, expressed as a decimal, and then add those values together.

Let us walk through a quick example. Imagine an element exists in nature as two isotopes. The first isotope has a mass of ten atomic mass units and an abundance of twenty percent. The second isotope has a mass of eleven atomic mass units and an abundance of eighty percent. To find the average atomic mass, we first convert the percentages to decimals, giving us zero point two zero and zero point eight zero. We multiply ten atomic mass units by zero point two zero to get two atomic mass units. Then, we multiply eleven atomic mass units by zero point eight zero to get eight point eight atomic mass units. Adding two atomic mass units and eight point eight atomic mass units gives us an average atomic mass of ten point eight atomic mass units. This calculation explains why the numbers on the periodic table represent a statistical average rather than the mass of a single specific atom.

Understanding these subatomic particles and their isotopes provides the foundation for everything that follows in chemistry. It explains why atoms have mass, why they are neutral, and why they organize themselves the way they do. With this knowledge of the internal architecture of the atom, we are finally ready to examine the map that chemists use to organize these elements based on their unique properties and repeating patterns.

## Key ideas

- Atoms consist of a dense, positive nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of negatively charged electrons.
- The atomic mass unit is the standard unit for subatomic particles, where protons and neutrons each weigh approximately one atomic mass unit.
- The atomic number represents the number of protons in an atom, while the mass number is the integer sum of protons and neutrons.
- Isotopes are variants of an element that contain the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons, resulting in different isotopic masses.
- The average atomic mass displayed on the periodic table is a weighted average of all naturally occurring isotopes, expressed in atomic mass units.
