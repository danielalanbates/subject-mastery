# The Unit Circle

Imagine you are standing at the center of a giant clock face drawn on the ground. You have a piece of string exactly one unit long pinned to the center. If you pull that string taut and walk in a full circle, the path you trace is the most important shape in all of trigonometry. We call this the unit circle. In our previous lesson, we defined trigonometric ratios using the sides of a right triangle. But triangles are limited to angles between zero and ninety degrees. By moving to the unit circle, we can define sine and cosine for any angle at all, whether it is negative, zero, or even thousands of degrees.

The unit circle is defined as the set of all points that are exactly one unit away from the origin in a standard coordinate plane. If we take any point on this circle and call its horizontal position x and its vertical position y, the distance formula tells us that the square root of the quantity x squared plus y squared equals one. To make our lives simpler, we square both sides, giving us the fundamental equation x squared plus y squared equals one. This simple relationship is actually the Pythagorean theorem in disguise. It confirms that for any point on the circle, the x coordinate and the y coordinate form the legs of a right triangle with a hypotenuse of one.

Here is the breakthrough. If we draw a line from the origin to a point on the circle at an angle theta, the horizontal distance is the cosine of theta and the vertical distance is the sine of theta. Because the hypotenuse of our triangle is exactly one, the cosine is simply the x coordinate and the sine is simply the y coordinate. This means that every single point on the unit circle can be written as the ordered pair of cosine of theta and sine of theta.

As you move around the circle, the signs of your coordinates change depending on which quadrant you are in. In the first quadrant, both x and y are positive, so both cosine and sine are positive. In the second quadrant, x is negative but y is positive, so cosine is negative and sine is positive. In the third quadrant, both are negative. In the fourth quadrant, x is positive but y is negative, making cosine positive and sine negative.

To find these values, we often use radian measure. A full circle is two pi radians. A quarter circle is pi over two radians. We can identify special angles using these measures. For example, pi over six radians is thirty degrees, pi over four radians is forty-five degrees, and pi over three radians is sixty degrees. A reference angle is the acute angle between your terminal side and the horizontal x axis. If you are at an angle of five pi over six radians, your terminal side is in the second quadrant. The angle back to the horizontal axis is pi minus five pi over six, which is pi over six radians. Because pi over six is in the first quadrant, we know its sine is one half and its cosine is the square root of three over two. Since we are in the second quadrant, we apply the correct sign. The sine remains one half, but the cosine becomes negative the square root of three over two.

Let us look at another example. Consider the angle five pi over four radians. This lands in the third quadrant. The reference angle is five pi over four minus pi, which is pi over four radians. We know that at pi over four, both sine and cosine are the square root of two over two. Since we are in the third quadrant, both coordinates must be negative. Therefore, at five pi over four, the cosine is negative the square root of two over two and the sine is also negative the square root of two over two.

This approach allows us to find exact values for all the special angles, no matter where they appear around the circle. By mastering these coordinates, you no longer need to rely on triangles alone to understand how waves and rotations behave. You have transformed trigonometry from a study of static shapes into a fluid, dynamic system of coordinates. Now that we can locate any point on the circle, we are ready to see how these values repeat and oscillate over time.

## Key ideas

- The unit circle is the set of all points exactly one unit from the origin, satisfying the equation x squared plus y squared equals one.
- Any point on the unit circle corresponds to the coordinates cosine of theta and sine of theta, where theta is the angle from the positive x axis.
- The signs of sine and cosine are determined by the quadrant where the terminal side of the angle lies.
- Reference angles allow us to calculate the exact sine and cosine values for any angle by relating it back to a corresponding angle in the first quadrant using either degrees or radians.
