# Exponential and Logarithmic Integrals

Imagine you are standing at the base of a grand staircase where each step upward doubles the height of the previous one. The growth is relentless and swift. In our last lesson, we mastered integration by substitution, learning how to peel back the layers of a composite function to find its antiderivative. Today, we turn that powerful technique toward two of the most essential occupants of the mathematical landscape: exponential functions and natural logarithms. These functions are the twin engines of growth and decay, governing everything from the cooling of a warm cup of coffee to the compounding of interest in a savings account. Understanding how to integrate them completes a vital piece of our calculus puzzle.

Let us begin with the natural logarithm, which arises from a curious gap in our standard power rule. Recall that the power rule for integration tells us to add one to the exponent and divide by the new exponent, provided our exponent is not negative one. If you try to apply that rule to the integral of one over x with respect to x, your denominator becomes zero, leaving you stranded. What function, we must ask, has a derivative equal to one over x? The answer, discovered through the study of logarithmic properties, is the natural logarithm of the absolute value of x. When we integrate one over x with respect to x, the result is the natural logarithm of the absolute value of x, plus the constant of integration. We include absolute value bars because the natural logarithm function only accepts positive inputs, yet the domain of one over x includes negative numbers.

To see this in action, suppose we wish to evaluate the integral of two over the quantity three x plus one, with respect to x. This is where our recent work with substitution proves invaluable. We can factor out the constant two and then let our substitution variable, which we will call u, equal the denominator three x plus one. The derivative of u with respect to x is simply three, meaning that d x is equal to d u divided by three. Making our substitution, our integral transforms into two thirds times the integral of one over u with respect to u. Integrating this gives us two thirds times the natural logarithm of the absolute value of u, plus the constant of integration. Substituting our original expression back in for u, we find our final antiderivative is two thirds times the natural logarithm of the absolute value of the quantity three x plus one, plus the constant of integration. If we differentiate this result using the chain rule, we drop the constant, multiply by the derivative of the inner function which is three, and watch the three cancel with our two thirds to leave the original integrand of two over the quantity three x plus one.

Now let us turn our attention to exponential functions, which behave with a wonderful sort of stubborn self reliance. The exponential function with base e is famously its own derivative, meaning that when you differentiate it, you get right back what you started with. Consequently, going backward is just as straightforward. The integral of e raised to the x power with respect to x is simply e raised to the x power, plus the constant of integration. If our exponent is a linear multiple of x, such as e raised to the negative four x power, substitution steps in smoothly. We let u equal negative four x, meaning d u is negative four times d x. Adjusting our constants, the integral of e raised to the negative four x power with respect to x evaluates to negative one fourth times e raised to the negative four x power, plus the constant of integration.

What happens if our base is not the constant e, but some other positive number like two or ten? The derivative of b raised to the x power introduces a scaling factor equal to the natural logarithm of the base b. Therefore, integration must do the exact opposite. The integral of b raised to the x power with respect to x is b raised to the x power divided by the natural logarithm of b, plus our constant. Every exponential curve carries its own specific growth rate locked inside its base, and dividing by that natural logarithm scales our area under the curve to match. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will use these foundational integration tools to calculate the physical area trapped between two intersecting curves, turning abstract functions into tangible geometric shapes.

## Key ideas
- The integral of one over x with respect to x yields the natural logarithm of the absolute value of x, plus the constant of integration, filling the gap left by the standard power rule.
- Integration by substitution allows us to evaluate logarithmic integrals where the integrand is a fraction whose numerator is the derivative of its denominator.
- The exponential function with base e serves as its own antiderivative, while linear scalings in the exponent require a simple reciprocal coefficient adjustment.
- Integrating exponential functions with arbitrary bases requires dividing by the natural logarithm of the base to account for the inherent growth scaling factor.
