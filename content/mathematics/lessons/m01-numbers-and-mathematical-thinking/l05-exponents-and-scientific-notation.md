# Exponents and Scientific Notation

Imagine trying to write the mass of the planet Earth in kilograms without any shorthand. You would need a number followed by twenty-four zeros, a string so long it would stretch across a page and invite counting errors every single time. Mathematics solves this dizzying problem with exponents, a powerful notation that replaces clumsy chains of multiplication with compact shorthand. In our last lesson, we worked through fractions, decimals, and ratios to compare quantities of different sizes. Today, we expand that toolkit to handle numbers that are unfathomably large or microscopic, laying the groundwork for the algebraic expressions we will build in our next lesson.

At its core, a whole-number exponent is simply repeated multiplication. When we write three to the fourth power, the three is the base and the four is the exponent. The base tells you what number is multiplying, and the exponent tells you how many times to use it as a factor. So, three to the fourth power means three times three times three times three. Let us calculate that step by step. Three times three is nine. Multiply that nine by the next three to get twenty-seven. Finally, multiply twenty-seven by the last three to get eighty-one. To verify this result, we can check it by working backward through division, dividing eighty-one by three three successive times, through twenty-seven and nine, until we return to three.

Because exponents count multiplications, they obey elegant structural rules that make heavy arithmetic manageable. Consider what happens when you multiply two powers of the same base, such as two to the third power multiplied by two to the fourth power. Two to the third power expands to two times two times two, and two to the fourth power expands to two times two times two times two. When you multiply them together, you have a single unbroken string of seven twos multiplied in a row. That is two to the seventh power. Notice that the resulting exponent, seven, is simply the sum of the original exponents, three plus four. This is the product rule: to multiply expressions with the same base, keep the base and add the exponents.

Next, what happens when we divide? If we divide two to the fifth power by two to the second power, we write five twos in the numerator and two twos in the denominator. Two factors of two in the numerator cancel with the two factors in the denominator, leaving three twos multiplied together, which is two to the third power. Here, the resulting exponent is five minus two. This is the quotient rule: when dividing powers with the same base, keep the base and subtract the exponent of the denominator from the exponent of the numerator. We can verify this by taking our result of two cubed, which is eight, multiplying it by the original divisor of two squared, which is four, to get thirty-two, which matches the original numerator of two to the fifth power.

This subtraction leads us naturally to zero and negative exponents. Suppose you divide two to the third power by two to the third power. Using our quotient rule, subtracting three minus three gives us two to the zero power. But we also know that any non-zero number divided by itself equals one. Therefore, any non-zero base raised to the power of zero must equal one. Following this logic further, what if we divide two to the second power by two to the fifth power? Subtracting two minus five gives us two to the negative third power. Expanded out, two squared over two to the fifth power leaves one over two cubed, or one over eight. A negative exponent does not make a number negative; it indicates a reciprocal, flipping the base from numerator to denominator.

Another essential structural rule is the power rule, which tells us what happens when a power is raised to another power, such as the quantity two cubed raised to the fourth power. That expression means multiplying two cubed by itself four times, which gives four groups of three twos, totaling twelve factors of two, or two to the twelfth power. The power rule states that when you raise a power to a power, you keep the base and multiply the exponents.

We use these exact rules in scientific notation, which expresses any number as a product of a number between one and ten and a power of ten. The mass of the Earth is roughly six trillion trillion kilograms, which we write compactly as five point nine seven times ten to the twenty-fourth power. We also use exponent rules to multiply and divide numbers in scientific notation. To multiply two numbers in scientific notation, you multiply their decimal parts together and add the exponents of ten. For example, three times ten squared multiplied by four times ten cubed gives us three times four, which is twelve, multiplied by ten to the two plus three power, which is ten to the fifth power. Because twelve is not between one and ten, we rewrite twelve as one point two times ten, so the answer in proper scientific notation is one point two times ten to the sixth power. To divide, you divide the decimal parts and subtract the exponent of the denominator from the exponent of the numerator. As we move forward into our next lesson, we will see how these numerical laws generalize when we introduce variables into algebraic expressions.

## Key ideas
- A whole-number exponent represents repeated multiplication, telling you how many times to use the base as a factor.
- The product rule states that multiplying two powers with the same base means keeping the base and adding the exponents together, while the power rule states that raising a power to a power means multiplying the exponents.
- Any non-zero number raised to the power of zero equals one, while a negative exponent indicates the reciprocal of that base raised to the positive opposite power.
- Scientific notation combines a decimal factor between one and ten with a power of ten to make very large and very small numbers easy to read and calculate.
- When multiplying numbers in scientific notation, you multiply their decimal parts and add the exponents of ten, and when dividing, you divide their decimal parts and subtract the exponent of the denominator from the exponent of the numerator.
