# Ionic and Molecular Compounds

Think about the salt you sprinkle on your dinner and the water you drink to wash it down. Even though both are simple, common substances, they behave in fundamentally different ways. Table salt is a crystalline solid that melts only at very high temperatures, while water is a liquid that flows easily at room temperature. This difference exists because these two substances represent the two main ways atoms organize themselves into compounds: ionic and molecular. In our last lesson, we explored how the periodic table is organized by electron behavior. Today, we will see how that behavior drives atoms to join together to form the vast array of materials that make up our world.

Atoms generally seek a more stable state by adjusting their electron count. Metals, located on the left side of the periodic table, tend to lose electrons, while nonmetals on the right side tend to gain them. When a metal atom transfers one or more electrons to a nonmetal, they become charged particles called ions. An atom that loses electrons becomes a positive ion, known as a cation, because it now has more protons than electrons. Conversely, an atom that gains electrons becomes a negative ion, known as an anion. You can predict these charges by looking at the group number. Group one metals almost always form ions with a charge of positive one. Group seventeen nonmetals, like chlorine, typically form ions with a charge of negative one.

Ionic compounds form when these oppositely charged ions attract one another. Because this attraction is strong and pulls in many directions, these compounds form repeating, three-dimensional lattice structures rather than small, discrete clusters. When we write the formula for an ionic compound, the goal is to ensure the total charge equals zero. For example, consider calcium and chlorine. Calcium, in group two, forms an ion with a charge of positive two. Chlorine forms an ion with a charge of negative one. To balance a positive two charge, we need two chloride ions, which are chlorine atoms that have each gained one electron and carry a charge of negative one. Therefore, the formula has one calcium ion for every two chloride ions.

Sometimes, a group of atoms stays bonded together and carries a collective charge. These are called polyatomic ions. Think of them as a single unit, like a package that cannot be taken apart. A common example is the carbonate ion, which consists of one carbon atom and three oxygen atoms, carrying a total charge of negative two. If carbonate pairs with calcium, which has a charge of positive two, they balance perfectly in a one to one ratio. The resulting compound is calcium carbonate, written as one calcium, one carbon and three oxygen atoms.

Molecular compounds are different. Instead of transferring electrons to create ions, the atoms in molecular compounds share electrons. This usually happens between two or more nonmetals. Because they share electrons rather than forming charged lattices, molecular compounds exist as individual, distinct units called molecules. Water is a perfect example. Two hydrogen atoms share their electrons with one oxygen atom to form a stable, discrete unit of water. Unlike ionic compounds, which are held together by the attraction of opposite charges, these molecular units are held together by the shared electrons within the molecule itself.

Distinguishing between the two is a matter of checking the periodic table. If a compound is composed of a metal and a nonmetal, it is almost certainly ionic. If it is composed entirely of nonmetals, it is likely molecular. Understanding this distinction is the first step toward mastering the language of chemistry. By looking at a formula, you can now predict whether you are dealing with a rigid, salt-like lattice or a collection of individual molecules. This classification sets the stage for how we communicate these formulas to other scientists, which is the vital skill we will master in our next session.

## Key ideas

- Ionic compounds are formed through the transfer of electrons between metals and nonmetals, resulting in a lattice of positive and negative ions.
- You can predict the charge of an ion by its group number on the periodic table, where metals typically lose electrons to become positive and nonmetals gain them to become negative.
- When writing ionic formulas, you must balance the total positive and negative charges so that the final compound is electrically neutral.
- Polyatomic ions are groups of atoms that remain bonded together and carry a collective charge, acting as a single unit in a chemical formula.
- Molecular compounds consist of nonmetal atoms that share electrons to form discrete, individual units, rather than the extended lattices found in ionic compounds.
