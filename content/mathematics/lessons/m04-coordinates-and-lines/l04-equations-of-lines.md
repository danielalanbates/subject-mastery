# Equations of Lines

Imagine you are standing at the top of a hiking trail, looking down a hillside that drops steadily by three feet for every ten feet you walk forward. In our last lesson, we saw that this constant rate of change is the slope of a line. But knowing how steep a hill is does not tell you exactly where it sits on the landscape. To place that hill on a map, or to write down a complete algebraic recipe for its path, you need more than just a slope. You need a starting point. Equations of lines are simply the mathematical rules that lock a slope and a point together, creating a permanent blueprint for every single pair of coordinates that makes up that straight path.

The most famous blueprint for a line is slope intercept form. Picture an equation written as y equals m times x plus b. Here, m represents the slope, or how steeply the line climbs or falls, and b represents the y intercept, which is the vertical height where the line crosses the y axis when x equals zero. This form works because it tracks the accumulated change: starting from the vertical height b, every time x increases by one unit, y increases by m units, so any point on the line satisfies this balance. Suppose a delivery service charges a flat fee of five dollars plus two dollars for every mile driven. If you let x be the number of miles and y be the total cost, the rule is y equals two x plus five. The slope is two, meaning every additional mile costs two dollars, and the y intercept is five, representing that initial five-dollar fee before you even drive a single mile. We can verify this equation by substituting a known point back into it. For instance, if you drive three miles, x is three, so y equals two times three plus five, which is six plus five, or eleven. The point three comma eleven satisfies the equation, confirming our rule.

Often, however, you do not know the y intercept. You might just know one point the line passes through and its slope. For that situation, mathematicians use point slope form. Imagine you are given a slope of three and a specific point on the line with coordinates four and seven. Point slope form writes this as y minus seven equals three times the quantity x minus four. Notice the structure. On the left, you subtract the y coordinate of your known point from y. On the right, you multiply the slope by the quantity of x minus the x coordinate of your known point. This formula works because it simply enforces the definition of slope for any arbitrary point x comma y on that same line, equating the change in y over the change in x to our fixed slope. To check this work, we can substitute our original point four comma seven into the equation, yielding seven minus seven equals three times the quantity four minus four, which simplifies to zero equals zero, confirming the point lies on the line.

What happens if you are given two points instead of a slope and a point? Suppose a line passes through the points two comma three and four comma seven. First, you calculate the slope by finding the change in y over the change in x. Seven minus three is four, and four minus two is two. Four divided by two gives a slope of two. Once you have that slope of two, you can pick either of your two points, plug them into the point slope form along with your slope, and simplify. If we use the point two comma three, we write y minus three equals two times the quantity x minus two. Distributing the two gives two x minus four, and adding three to both sides yields y equals two x minus one. To verify this result, we substitute our second point four comma seven into the final equation: seven equals two times four minus one, which is eight minus one, or seven, showing that the equation holds true for both starting points.

Some lines do not fit neatly into standard sloped formulas because they are perfectly flat or perfectly vertical. A horizontal line has a slope of zero, meaning y never changes no matter what x does. If a horizontal line crosses the y axis at four, its equation is simply y equals four. A vertical line, on the other hand, runs straight up and down. Because its x coordinate never changes while y wanders freely up and down, its slope is undefined, and its equation is simply x equals a constant, such as x equals negative three. Every horizontal line is perpendicular to every vertical line, but this cannot be read from their slopes, because a slope of zero has no reciprocal and a vertical line has no slope at all.

Finally, slopes tell us how two distinct lines relate to one another in the plane. Two distinct lines are parallel if and only if they have the exact same slope, meaning they tilt at the identical angle and will never intersect, much like railroad tracks. Coincident lines share the same slope as well, but because they lie directly on top of each other, they intersect at infinitely many points rather than being parallel. Two lines are perpendicular if their slopes are negative reciprocals of one another, meaning you flip the fraction and change its sign. If one line has a slope of two thirds, a line perpendicular to it will have a slope of negative three over two. When you multiply those two perpendicular slopes together, you always get negative one, ensuring they meet at a precise right angle. As we move forward, we will take these geometric rules for lines and apply them to build sophisticated linear models in context.

## Key ideas
- Slope intercept form expresses a line as y equals m times x plus b, using slope m and y intercept b, and any valid point can verify this equation by substitution.
- Point slope form relates any general point on a line to a known specific point and a known slope by enforcing the constant rate of change.
- Horizontal lines have a zero slope and equations of the form y equals a constant, while vertical lines have an undefined slope and equations of the form x equals a constant, creating a perpendicular relationship between them.
- Distinct parallel lines share identical slopes without intersecting, whereas perpendicular lines have slopes that are negative reciprocals of each other.
