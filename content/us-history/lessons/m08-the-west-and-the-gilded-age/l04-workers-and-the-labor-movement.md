# Workers and the Labor Movement

Imagine standing on a factory floor in the late eighteen-eighties. The air is thick with coal dust and the relentless roar of steam-powered machines. There is no ventilation, the lighting is dim, and your shift lasts twelve hours, six days a week. If you lose a finger to a gear, there is no insurance to pay your medical bills, and you will likely be fired for being too slow. This was the reality for millions of Americans as the industrial giants we discussed in our last session reshaped the national economy. While the titans of industry built empires, the workers who powered them began to realize that their only path toward dignity lay in collective action.

The industrial workplace was inherently dangerous. In mines, workers faced cave-ins and poisonous gases. In textile mills, children—some as young as ten—worked under spinning machines, risking limbs to clear jams. Because there were no federal safety regulations, employers generally viewed labor as a disposable commodity. When workers tried to voice complaints, they were often replaced by the steady stream of laborers arriving from abroad or moving from rural farms to the cities. To counter this, workers began to organize into unions, seeking shorter hours, better pay, and safer conditions.

One of the earliest and most ambitious organizations was the Knights of Labor. Founded in 1869, the Knights were unique because they welcomed almost everyone, including skilled and unskilled workers, women, and Black laborers. They envisioned a cooperative society where the producers of wealth, rather than the bosses, held the power. However, the Knights struggled to maintain unity, and their influence waned after the public began to associate them with radicalism following the Haymarket Riot in Chicago in 1886. During a protest for an eight-hour workday, a bomb was thrown at police, leading to a violent clash that turned public opinion against the labor movement.

As the Knights faded, a more pragmatic organization rose to prominence: the American Federation of Labor, or the AFL, led by Samuel Gompers. Unlike the Knights, the AFL focused almost exclusively on skilled craftsmen. They practiced what Gompers called bread and butter unionism, meaning they avoided broad social reform in favor of concrete goals like higher wages and shorter workdays. By organizing workers by trade, the AFL created a more stable and effective structure, though it often excluded women and minorities, leaving those groups to fend for themselves in an increasingly stratified industrial landscape.

The struggle between capital and labor often exploded into open warfare. The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 was the first major indicator of this boiling tension, as workers shut down rail lines across the country to protest wage cuts. Federal troops were eventually called in to break the strike, marking a recurring pattern where the government sided with corporate interests. This pattern repeated in the eighteen-nineties at the Homestead steel works in Pennsylvania, where a violent confrontation between strikers and armed guards ended in bloodshed and the defeat of the union.

Perhaps the most famous conflict was the Pullman Strike of 1894. Workers at the Pullman Palace Car Company, who lived in a company-owned town where their rent was deducted directly from their paychecks, went on strike to protest wage cuts. When the American Railway Union, led by Eugene Debs, joined the cause, they effectively paralyzed the nation’s rail traffic. President Grover Cleveland intervened, claiming the strike interfered with the delivery of the United States mail, and sent in the army to crush the protest. The defeat of the Pullman workers effectively stalled the momentum of national labor organizing for years to come.

These clashes reveal a fundamental conflict in the Gilded Age: the tension between the democratic ideal of individual liberty and the reality of corporate control. Labor unions were not just asking for a few more pennies an hour; they were asking for a voice in a system that seemed designed to silence them. While these early unions often failed to achieve their immediate goals, they laid the groundwork for the future of American labor, forcing the nation to confront the human cost of its rapid economic expansion. As the century drew to a close, the workforce became increasingly diverse, setting the stage for a new wave of urban growth and the rise of the immigrant city.

## Key ideas

- The industrial revolution of the late nineteenth century created dangerous, dehumanizing work environments that prompted workers to form labor unions to demand safety and fair treatment.
- The Knights of Labor attempted to organize all workers into a single movement, but their influence collapsed following the social unrest associated with the Haymarket Riot.
- The American Federation of Labor shifted the movement toward bread and butter unionism, focusing on specific gains for skilled workers through a more organized and exclusive structure.
- Major strikes like the railroad strike of 1877, the Homestead strike, and the Pullman strike were often suppressed by state or federal intervention, highlighting the government's tendency to prioritize industrial stability over workers' rights.
