# Gas Mixtures and Gas Stoichiometry

Imagine you are standing on a beach, feeling the wind against your skin. That breeze is not just a single substance moving through the air. It is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide, water vapor, and trace amounts of other gases. Because gas molecules are so far apart and move independently, they do not typically react with one another in the atmosphere. This independence allows us to treat each gas in a mixture as if it were the only one present. This is the heart of Dalton’s Law of Partial Pressures. John Dalton observed that in a mixture of non-reacting gases, the total pressure exerted is simply the sum of the pressures that each individual gas would exert if it occupied the container alone. We call these individual contributions partial pressures.

If you have a container holding nitrogen at a pressure of two atmospheres and oxygen at a pressure of one atmosphere, the total pressure is simply three atmospheres. It does not matter what the gases are, only how many moles of each are present. The partial pressure of any component gas is equal to its mole fraction multiplied by the total pressure. The mole fraction is the ratio of the moles of one gas to the total moles of all gases in the mixture. This principle is incredibly useful in the laboratory, particularly when we collect a gas produced by a chemical reaction. Often, we bubble a gas through water into an inverted collection bottle. As the gas displaces the water, it also picks up water vapor. The pressure inside the bottle is actually the sum of the pressure of the gas we collected and the vapor pressure of the water. To find the true pressure of our target gas, we must subtract the water’s vapor pressure, which depends entirely on the temperature of the water, from the total measured pressure.

Let’s walk through an example. Suppose you collect oxygen gas over water at twenty-five degrees Celsius, and the total pressure is seven hundred and fifty millimeters of mercury. At this temperature, water vapor exerts a pressure of approximately twenty-four millimeters of mercury. To find the partial pressure of the dry oxygen, we subtract twenty-four from seven hundred and fifty, giving us seven hundred and twenty-six millimeters of mercury. Now that we have the pressure of the pure oxygen, we can use the ideal gas law to calculate the moles of oxygen produced. We plug our pressure, volume, and temperature into the ideal gas equation and solve for the quantity of gas.

This brings us to the bridge between gases and stoichiometry. In our previous work with stoichiometry, we often dealt with masses or molarities. Now, we can include volumes of gases in our calculations. Because the ideal gas law relates pressure, volume, and temperature to the number of moles, we can convert any gas volume into a molar amount. Once we have moles, we follow the standard stoichiometry path using the mole ratios from our balanced chemical equation.

Consider the thermal decomposition of potassium chlorate, which releases oxygen gas and leaves behind solid potassium chloride. If we start with a specific mass of potassium chlorate, we first use its molar mass to find the number of moles. Then, we look at the balanced equation, which tells us that two moles of potassium chlorate produce three moles of oxygen gas. We use that two to three mole ratio to find the exact number of moles of oxygen produced. Finally, if we know the pressure and temperature of the lab, we can use the ideal gas law to calculate exactly what volume of oxygen gas we expect to collect.

We can also work backward. If you know the volume, temperature, and pressure of a gas produced in a reaction, you can calculate the moles of gas, then use the stoichiometric coefficients to find the moles of a reactant, and finally convert that into a mass in grams. This is how chemists verify the purity of samples or determine the amount of a substance produced in an industrial process. Whether you are analyzing the composition of the air or calculating the yield of a gas-producing reaction, the key is remembering that gas molecules are independent actors. By treating each gas as a separate entity and using the ideal gas law as a translator between volume and moles, you can solve almost any problem involving gaseous mixtures.

As we move forward, we will investigate the microscopic world to understand why these laws hold true and why real gases sometimes deviate from these ideal behaviors. We will explore the kinetic-molecular theory to see how molecular speed and volume influence the macroscopic properties of the air around us.

## Key ideas
- Dalton’s Law of Partial Pressures states that the total pressure of a gas mixture is the sum of the individual partial pressures of each component gas.
- When collecting a gas over water, the total pressure measured includes the water vapor pressure, which must be subtracted to find the pressure of the dry gas.
- Gas stoichiometry allows us to use the ideal gas law to convert between the volume of a gas and the number of moles involved in a chemical reaction.
- In any gas mixture, the partial pressure of a specific gas is directly proportional to its mole fraction, making it possible to determine the composition of the mixture based on pressure readings.
