# Systems of Two Linear Equations

Imagine you are managing a small community garden. You have a budget of sixty dollars to buy two types of plants: tomatoes and peppers. Each tomato plant costs three dollars, and each pepper plant costs two dollars. You also know that you need exactly twenty-five plants in total to fill your raised beds. How many of each should you buy? You could guess and check, but there is a more powerful way to find the answer. This is a system of linear equations, where we look for a single point that satisfies two different conditions simultaneously.

A system of two linear equations consists of two relationships between two variables, usually called x and y. When we think of these on a coordinate plane, each equation forms a straight line. The solution to the system is the point where those two lines intersect.

There are two primary algebraic methods for finding this point: substitution and elimination. Substitution works best when one variable is already easy to isolate. Let us look at our garden problem. We have two variables: x for the number of tomato plants and y for the number of pepper plants. The first condition is that the total number of plants is twenty-five, so x plus y equals twenty-five. The second condition is the cost, so three times x plus two times y equals sixty. To solve by substitution, we rearrange the first equation to say y equals twenty-five minus x. Now, we substitute this expression for y into the second equation. We get three times x plus two times the quantity twenty-five minus x equals sixty. Distributing the two gives us three times x plus fifty minus two times x equals sixty. Combining the x terms leaves us with x plus fifty equals sixty. Subtracting fifty from both sides, we find that x equals ten. Since y is twenty-five minus x, y must be fifteen. We check this by substituting these values back into both original equations. Ten plus fifteen is twenty-five, which matches the first equation. Three times ten plus two times fifteen is thirty plus thirty, which is sixty, matching the second equation.

Sometimes, substitution is messy because of fractions. In those cases, we use elimination. This method involves adding or subtracting the equations to cancel out one variable entirely. Sometimes you must multiply one or both equations by a constant to obtain opposite coefficients for one of the variables. Suppose we have the system two times x plus y equals eight and three times x minus y equals seven. If we add these two equations together, the positive y and the negative y cancel out perfectly. We are left with five times x equals fifteen, meaning x equals three. Plugging three back into the first equation, we get two times three plus y equals eight, which means six plus y equals eight, or y equals two. We check this by substituting three and two into the second equation: three times three minus two is nine minus two, which is seven. This confirms our solution is correct.

Not every system results in a clean, single point of intersection. Imagine two lines that are parallel and never touch. In this case, there is no solution because there is no point that satisfies both equations. Algebraically, this happens when you try to solve the system and end up with a false statement, like zero equals five. Conversely, if you have two equations that actually describe the same line, they lie directly on top of each other. Every point on that line is a solution, which we call infinitely many solutions. This results in a statement that is always true, such as zero equals zero.

Understanding these systems is essential because they model how different constraints interact in the real world. Whether you are balancing a budget, optimizing a flight path, or mixing chemical solutions, you are essentially finding the intersection of multiple requirements. While we focused on two equations today, the logic remains the same even as we add more variables.

## Key ideas

- A system of linear equations asks for the coordinate point where two lines intersect, representing a solution that satisfies both conditions at once.
- The substitution method involves solving one equation for one variable and plugging that expression into the other equation to isolate the remaining variable.
- The elimination method involves adding or subtracting equations, sometimes after multiplying them by constants to create opposite coefficients, to cancel out one variable.
- Always check a proposed solution by substituting the values back into both original equations to ensure they satisfy the system.
- A system may have one unique solution, no solution if the lines are parallel, or infinitely many solutions if the two equations represent the same line.
