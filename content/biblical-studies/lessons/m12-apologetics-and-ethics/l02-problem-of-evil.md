# The Problem of Evil

Imagine you are standing in a hospital waiting room, watching a loved one suffer from a terminal illness. Or perhaps you are reading the morning news, filled with reports of earthquakes and senseless violence. In these moments, a heavy, haunting question often surfaces: If God is all-powerful, all-knowing, and perfectly good, why does he allow such profound suffering? This is the core of the problem of evil. It is perhaps the most significant challenge to the Christian faith, often acting as a barrier for those considering the classical arguments for God’s existence.

To address this challenge, scholars distinguish between two types of suffering. Moral evil refers to the harm caused by the free choices of human beings, such as war or theft. Natural evil refers to suffering that results from natural processes, such as disease, tsunamis, or famine. This distinction is vital because different explanations, or theodicies, are often required to address each category.

The problem is usually divided into two philosophical approaches: the logical problem and the evidential problem. The logical problem, an argument often credited to the Greek philosopher Epicurus, suggests that the existence of evil and the existence of an all-good, all-powerful God are mutually exclusive. It argues that if God were willing to prevent evil but not able, he would not be omnipotent. If he were able but not willing, he would not be benevolent. Therefore, the presence of evil supposedly proves that God cannot exist. Many philosophers, including some atheists, now think Alvin Plantinga's free will defense, published in 1974, showed that God and evil are not strictly contradictory, so much of today's debate concerns the evidential problem.

The evidential problem is slightly different. It does not argue that God and evil are logically impossible to reconcile, but rather that the sheer amount and intensity of suffering in the world make the existence of a loving God highly improbable. When we see the scale of suffering—what scholars often call gratuitous or pointless suffering—it becomes difficult to see how a good Creator could permit it.

In response, Christian thinkers have developed various defenses. One of the most traditional is the free will defense. This view holds that for humans to experience genuine love and moral goodness, they must have the freedom to choose. A world where humans are forced to be good is a world without authentic moral life. Consequently, the possibility of choosing evil is a necessary condition for a world that contains the greater good of free creatures. In this view, moral evil is the tragic byproduct of human misuse of freedom.

However, the free will defense does not fully explain natural evil. For this, many look to the soul-making theodicy, rooted in the second-century bishop Irenaeus and developed by the philosopher John Hick in 1966. This approach suggests that the world is a place of challenge designed for spiritual growth. Just as a physical muscle requires resistance to grow, the human soul requires a world with real dangers and difficulties to develop virtues like courage, patience, and compassion. In this view, the world is a workshop for character, and natural suffering is part of the environment that allows us to move toward becoming the people God intends for us to be. Many traditions, following Augustine, also point toward the impact of a fallen, broken creation that is currently groaning under the weight of sin, awaiting ultimate restoration.

Yet, these philosophical arguments often feel cold when faced with the raw reality of a child’s pain. This is why many theologians point to the cross of Christ as the definitive Christian response. The central claim of the Gospel is not that God is a distant observer of our pain, but that he has entered into it. In the suffering and death of Jesus, God identifies with human agony. He does not provide a simple intellectual answer to why suffering exists, but he provides a personal presence in the midst of it. The cross demonstrates that God’s love is so deep that he was willing to endure the worst of human evil to eventually defeat it.

Christianity does not claim that we can fully understand the mind of God or see the complete tapestry of his providence from our limited perspective. Instead, it asserts that while we live in a world marked by both moral and natural evil, we do not suffer alone. The hope of the Christian life is that this current suffering is not the final word. As John writes in the book of Revelation, chapter twenty-one, verse four, in the King James Version: "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain."

This tension between the reality of pain and the promise of hope leads us directly to the bedrock of the Christian witness. Having examined the nature of suffering, we must now look at the historical evidence for the event that Christians believe inaugurated the ultimate victory over death and evil: the resurrection of Jesus.

## Key ideas

- The problem of evil is categorized into moral evil, stemming from human choices, and natural evil, arising from natural processes.
- The logical problem of evil claims God and evil are incompatible, while the evidential problem argues that the amount of suffering makes God's existence improbable.
- The free will defense posits that the capacity for genuine love requires the freedom to choose, which carries the risk of moral evil.
- The soul-making theodicy suggests that our world is a challenging environment where suffering provides the necessary resistance for the development of human character.
- The Christian doctrine of the cross asserts that God does not merely explain suffering but enters into it through the experience of Jesus, offering hope for ultimate restoration.
