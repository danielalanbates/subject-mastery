# Colligative Properties

Have you ever wondered why we sprinkle salt on icy sidewalks in the middle of winter, or why adding antifreeze to a car radiator keeps the engine from overheating? It is not because salt or antifreeze have some magical property that ignores the laws of thermodynamics. Instead, these substances change the physical behavior of water simply by being there. In our last lesson, we focused on molarity and how to measure the concentration of a solution. Today, we look at how the total number of dissolved particles—regardless of what they actually are—fundamentally alters the physical properties of a solvent. These effects are called colligative properties.

Colligative properties depend only on the ratio of the number of solute particles to the number of solvent particles. They do not care about the chemical identity of the solute. Whether you dissolve glucose or urea, if you have the same number of particles in the same amount of water, the effect on the freezing point or boiling point will be identical. However, not all solutes are created equal. This is where we must distinguish between nonelectrolytes and electrolytes. If you dissolve one mole of sugar in water, you get one mole of particles. But if you dissolve one mole of sodium chloride, it dissociates into one mole of sodium ions and one mole of chloride ions. Because salt splits into two particles, it has twice the impact on colligative properties as sugar. This is why electrolytes count for more than nonelectrolytes in these scenarios.

The first colligative property is vapor pressure lowering. When you dissolve a nonvolatile solute in a liquid, the solute particles occupy space at the surface of the liquid. This physically blocks some solvent molecules from escaping into the gas phase. Consequently, the vapor pressure of the solution is lower than that of the pure solvent. To calculate this, we often use the mole fraction, which is the ratio of the moles of one component to the total moles in the mixture. As you increase the mole fraction of the solute, the vapor pressure drops proportionally.

Next, we encounter boiling point elevation and freezing point depression. Because the solute lowers the vapor pressure, the solution must be heated to a higher temperature to reach a vapor pressure equal to the atmospheric pressure. This is boiling point elevation. Conversely, the presence of solute particles interferes with the solvent molecules as they try to organize into a solid crystal lattice. To force the solvent to freeze, you must remove even more energy, which lowers the freezing point. To measure these changes accurately, we prefer to use molality rather than molarity. Molality is defined as the moles of solute per one kilogram of solvent. Unlike molarity, which changes slightly with temperature due to liquid expansion, molality remains constant regardless of the temperature.

Let us walk through a quick calculation. Imagine we dissolve one mole of a nonelectrolyte in one kilogram of water. The concentration is one mole per kilogram of solvent. If the freezing point depression constant for water is one point eight six degrees Celsius per mole per kilogram of solvent, the water will freeze at negative one point eight six degrees Celsius instead of zero. If we used one mole of sodium chloride instead, it would dissociate into two moles of ions. Our effective concentration becomes two moles per kilogram of solvent. We multiply two by the constant of one point eight six to get a total depression of three point seven two degrees Celsius. The new freezing point would be negative three point seven two degrees Celsius.

Finally, we must consider osmotic pressure. This occurs when a solution is separated from a pure solvent by a semipermeable membrane, which allows solvent molecules to pass through but blocks solute particles. Nature tends toward equilibrium, so solvent molecules flow from the region of low solute concentration to the region of high concentration to dilute it. Osmotic pressure is the exact amount of pressure required to stop this net movement of solvent. This force is significant in biological systems, such as how water moves into plant cells or how your kidneys filter blood.

By understanding these properties, we gain a deeper insight into how solutions behave in the real world. We have seen how the number of particles dictates the physical state of a mixture. Now that we understand how substances interact in solution, we are ready to shift our focus toward how those substances transform over time. In our next lesson, we will begin exploring reaction rates and how we describe the speed at which chemical changes occur.

## Key ideas
- Colligative properties are physical changes in a solvent that depend solely on the concentration of solute particles rather than their chemical identity.
- Dissolved particles lower the vapor pressure of a solvent, which in turn elevates the boiling point and depresses the freezing point of the resulting solution.
- Electrolytes, such as salt, have a greater effect on colligative properties than nonelectrolytes, such as sugar, because they dissociate into multiple particles in solution.
- Molality, defined as moles of solute per kilogram of solvent, is the preferred unit of concentration for these calculations because it remains independent of temperature fluctuations.
- Osmotic pressure is the force required to prevent the flow of solvent through a semipermeable membrane into a more concentrated solution.
