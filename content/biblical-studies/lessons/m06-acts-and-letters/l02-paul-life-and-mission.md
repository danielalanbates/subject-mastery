# Paul: Life, Mission and Letters

Imagine a brilliant legal mind and a fierce religious zealot walking down a dusty Roman road, intent on hunting down a small, persecuted religious sect. Within a few years, that same man is sitting in a damp prison cell, writing passionate letters of comfort and correction to communities scattered across the Mediterranean. This is the staggering trajectory of Saul of Tarsus, known to history as the Apostle Paul. In our last lesson, we watched the church burst out of Jerusalem and cross cultural boundaries under the guidance of the Holy Spirit. Today, we turn to the most prolific missionary and theologian of the early Christian movement, whose life and correspondence reshaped the ancient world.

To understand Paul, we must recognize the unique intersection of three distinct worlds that shaped him: Jewish, Greek, and Roman. As he tells us in his letter to the Philippians, he was a Hebrew of the Hebrews, and according to Acts he was trained in Jerusalem under the renowned Pharisee teacher Gamaliel. This meant he possessed an exhaustive knowledge of the Hebrew scriptures and the strict Pharisaic traditions. Yet he was born in Tarsus, a major center of Hellenistic philosophy and education, which gave him fluency in the Greek language and a deep familiarity with Greco-Roman culture. Acts also reports that he was a Roman citizen by birth, a privilege that provided him with legal protections and a distinct mobility across the empire.

Paul’s dramatic conversion on the road to Damascus, detailed in the Book of Acts, transformed him from a violent opponent of the church into its most tireless advocate. Following this encounter with the risen Christ, Paul launched three extensive missionary journeys across the Mediterranean basin. Traveling by foot and by ship, he established Christian communities in key urban centers such as Philippi, Thessalonica, Corinth, and Ephesus. Paul typically began his ministry in local Jewish synagogues, reasoning from the scriptures that Jesus was the promised Messiah, before turning his focus to the wider Gentile populations. These journeys were marked by immense hardship, including shipwrecks, beatings, and constant danger, yet they successfully planted the gospel across the borders of the Roman world.

When Paul planted a church, his relationship with it did not end when he packed his bags. He stayed connected through letters, which served as pastoral substitutes for his personal presence. These letters are occasional documents, meaning they were written to address specific situations, crises, or theological questions in young congregations. For instance, when the church in Thessalonica faced intense persecution and confusion regarding the return of Christ, Paul wrote to encourage their perseverance and clarify Christian eschatology, which is the theological study of the end times. When the church in Corinth descended into bitter factionalism, moral failure, and confusion over spiritual gifts, Paul wrote a sweeping letter of correction, urging them toward unity and love.

In the ancient world, letters of this length were dictated to a trained secretary, known as an amanuensis, and delivered by a trusted courier who would read the letter aloud to the assembled congregation. Paul often added a personal greeting in his own handwriting at the close to authenticate the document. The letters were designed to be read aloud in community gatherings, functioning as living authoritative voices from the apostle himself. They combined rigorous theological instruction with practical ethical exhortation, grounding everyday Christian behavior in the profound reality of Christ's death and resurrection.

Scholars today engage in vigorous debates regarding the authorship of all thirteen letters attributed to Paul in the New Testament. Traditional views attribute every one of these letters directly to the apostle. Modern critical scholarship, however, often distinguishes between undisputed letters, generally considered to be Romans, First and Second Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, First Thessalonians, and Philemon, and disputed letters. Ephesians, Colossians and Second Thessalonians are debated, and most critical scholars think the Pastoral letters, First and Second Timothy and Titus, come from after Paul’s lifetime. They suggest these may have been written by close associates or later followers using Paul's name and theological framework in a common ancient practice known as pseudonymity. Regardless of these academic discussions, the letters remain foundational texts of Christian theology.

As we move forward, we will dive deeper into Paul's theological masterpieces, examining how his letters to the Romans and the Galatians unfold the doctrine of justification by faith.

## Key ideas
- Saul of Tarsus, transformed by his encounter with the risen Christ, became the apostle Paul and the most influential missionary of the early church.
- Paul's background combined Jewish Pharisaic training, Hellenistic education, and Roman citizenship, equipping him to bridge diverse cultures across the Mediterranean.
- Through his extensive missionary journeys, Paul planted urban Christian communities and maintained relationships with them through pastoral letters.
- Paul's letters were occasional documents written to address specific crises, theological questions, and practical matters in young congregations like those in Corinth and Thessalonica.
- The letters combine deep theological instruction with ethical guidance and were originally read aloud to gathered communities as authoritative apostolic teaching.
