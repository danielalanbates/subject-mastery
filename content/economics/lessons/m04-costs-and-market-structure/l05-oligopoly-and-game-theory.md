# Oligopoly and Game Theory

Imagine you run one of only two gas stations in a small desert town. If you lower your price by two cents, drivers will flock to your pumps, and your rival’s lot will empty out. But what happens by tomorrow morning? Your rival notices the lost business, slashes their price even further, and suddenly you are both making far less profit than before, while local drivers enjoy cheap fuel. In our last lesson, we looked at monopolistic competition, where many firms sell slightly different products. But when an entire industry is dominated by just a handful of massive firms, a very different dynamic takes over. This market structure is called an oligopoly.

In an oligopoly, firms do not operate in isolation. Because there are only a few competitors, every single pricing or output decision directly affects the others. Economists call this mutual interdependence. If cereal makers, commercial aircraft builders, or cellular network providers change their prices, their rivals react immediately. This creates a fascinating puzzle. Unlike a monopoly, where a firm ignores competitors, or perfect competition, where firms are too small to matter, oligopolists must constantly anticipate their rivals' next moves before making their own. To untangle this complex strategic dance, economists turn to game theory.

Game theory is the formal study of mathematical models involving strategic interaction among rational decision-makers. Think of it as chess played out in the marketplace. To see how it works, imagine two rival streaming services deciding whether to run a massive summer advertising campaign. If both run ads, they split the market, but the huge cost of advertising eats into their profits. If neither runs ads, they save money and split the market just the same, earning higher profits. But if one advertises while the other does not, the advertiser captures a massive market share and cleans up.

When you map out these potential payoffs, a striking pattern emerges. No matter what the other streaming service chooses, each firm does better by advertising. If the rival advertises, advertising keeps you from being out-marketed; if the rival stays quiet, advertising lets you steal the market. When one choice is best for a player whatever the opponent does, economists call it a dominant strategy. In our advertising example, both firms independently choose to run ads because of this dynamic, landing in a situation where they both spend heavily on marketing and end up with lower profits than if they had mutually agreed to stay quiet. This is the structure of the famous prisoner's dilemma.

This outcome is known as a Nash equilibrium, named after the brilliant mathematician John Nash. A Nash equilibrium is a state where no player has an incentive to unilaterally change their chosen strategy, given what their opponents are doing. Both firms are doing the absolute best they can, conditional on the other firm's behavior. Yet, notice the irony. The Nash equilibrium often leads to a suboptimal collective outcome. Even though both firms would be richer if they cooperated and called a truce on advertising, the temptation to cheat and steal market share drives them into a competitive trap.

This tension between cooperation and self-interest explains why oligopolistic firms are often tempted to form a cartel, which is a formal agreement among competing firms to fix prices and restrict output, acting collectively like a single monopoly. If our two streaming services could legally sit in a room and agree to ban expensive ads, they could split the high monopoly profits. However, cartels face an inherent instability. Once the cartel agrees to keep prices high, every individual firm has a massive financial incentive to secretly cheat, cut prices slightly, and capture the entire market. Because every firm knows the others face this temptation, cartels frequently collapse from within.

Government antitrust regulators watch these markets closely to prevent illegal price-fixing and collusion, forcing firms to compete even when game theory tempts them to cooperate. Understanding how these strategic interactions shape prices and output reveals why certain industries remain permanently locked in fierce corporate rivalries. As we shift our focus from private market structures to the wider society, we will next explore how private economic activities can spill over to affect innocent bystanders, beginning with negative externalities and pollution.

## Key ideas
- An oligopoly is a market structure dominated by a small number of firms whose decisions are bound together by mutual interdependence.
- Game theory provides the mathematical and strategic framework economists use to analyze how firms make decisions when facing intelligent rivals.
- A dominant strategy is a course of action that is best for a player regardless of what their competitors decide to do.
- A Nash equilibrium occurs when all players choose their optimal strategies simultaneously, and no single player can benefit by unilaterally changing their choice.
