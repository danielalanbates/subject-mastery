# Presidential and Congressional Reconstruction

Imagine a house torn apart by a violent storm. The roof is gone, the walls are crumbling, and the foundation is cracked. When the storm passes, neighbors argue about how to rebuild. Should they patch the holes and pretend nothing happened? Or should they tear down the dangerous parts, pour a new foundation, and build a house that will not collapse again? This was the dilemma facing the United States in the spring of 1865. The Civil War had ended, but the nation remained broken. The question of how to readmit the former Confederate states and what rights to grant the newly freed people triggered a bitter power struggle between the President and Congress.

Before the war even ended, Abraham Lincoln had proposed his Ten Percent Plan, which offered amnesty to Southerners who took an oath of loyalty to the Union. Once ten percent of a state’s voting population had taken this oath, they could form a new government. Many Congressional Republicans viewed this as far too lenient. They countered with the Wade-Davis Bill, which required a majority of white male citizens to swear an oath of past and future loyalty before a state could be readmitted. Lincoln pocket-vetoed the bill, setting the stage for future conflict.

Following Lincoln’s assassination, Andrew Johnson assumed the presidency. A Southerner from Tennessee who remained loyal to the Union, Johnson held deep-seated prejudices and believed in a limited federal government. His vision for Reconstruction was rapid and lenient. He offered pardons to most Southerners, allowing them to reorganize their state governments quickly. By late 1865, many of these governments were led by the same men who had previously led the Confederacy. They quickly passed laws known as Black Codes, designed to restrict the freedom of Black Americans, force them into exploitative labor contracts, and deny them basic rights like owning land or serving on juries.

To Republicans in Congress, Johnson’s approach felt like a betrayal. They watched with alarm as the South attempted to recreate a social order that resembled slavery. Congress responded by creating the Freedmen’s Bureau in 1865. This agency provided food, housing, and medical aid to freed people and poor whites, while also establishing schools. Women played a vital role here, as Northern teachers and missionaries traveled south to staff these schools and provide essential education. Meanwhile, the Civil Rights Act of 1866 was passed to override the Black Codes, declaring that all persons born in the United States were citizens with equal rights.

A faction known as the Radical Republicans insisted that the federal government must intervene to protect the formerly enslaved. They argued that because the Southern states had rebelled, they had forfeited their right to self-governance. This clash led to a series of constitutional changes. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in 1865, abolished slavery. The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, defined citizenship and guaranteed equal protection under the law. Finally, the Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in 1870, prohibited denying the vote based on race.

For decades, many historians promoted the Dunning School interpretation, which characterized Reconstruction as a tragic era of corruption caused by Black political participation and Northern interference. This view, often linked to the Lost Cause ideology, has been thoroughly abandoned by modern historians. We now recognize that these accounts were built on racist foundations and intentionally ignored the agency of Black Americans, who organized politically, built churches, and sought education. Reconstruction was also a period of profound economic change. While many Black families sought land ownership, they were often forced into sharecropping, a system where workers farmed land in exchange for a portion of the crop, often trapping them in cycles of debt.

President Johnson fought these measures, leading the House of Representatives to impeach him in 1868 for violating the Tenure of Office Act. He escaped removal by one vote in the Senate, but his influence was shattered. Congress took control, dividing the South into military districts to enforce the new laws. This period saw a surge in civic activity among diverse groups. Immigrants, particularly in urban centers, were beginning to organize into labor movements, while women activists, though often excluded from the language of the new amendments, pressed for their own rights, setting the stage for future suffrage movements.

## Key ideas
- Reconstruction was marked by a power struggle between President Andrew Johnson’s lenient approach and the Radical Republicans, who sought to protect the rights of formerly enslaved people through federal intervention.
- The Freedmen’s Bureau and the Civil Rights Act of 1866 were early legislative efforts to provide aid and legal protection to Black Americans in the post-war South.
- The Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments fundamentally reshaped the Constitution by abolishing slavery, establishing birthright citizenship, and protecting voting rights.
- Historians have abandoned the discredited Dunning School and Lost Cause interpretations, which ignored Black agency and promoted racist myths about the Reconstruction era.
- Reconstruction involved diverse actors, including women who staffed schools, workers navigating the sharecropping system, and Black citizens who exercised their new political rights.
