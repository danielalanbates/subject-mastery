# Balancing Chemical Equations

Think for a moment about a recipe for baking bread. If you have flour, water, and yeast, you cannot simply toss random amounts into a bowl and expect a perfect loaf. You need the right proportions. Chemistry works in much the same way. In our last lesson, we used the mole to calculate the mass of compounds based on their formulas. Now, we must look at how those compounds interact. A chemical equation is essentially a sentence that describes a chemical change, telling us which substances are starting the process and which ones are being created.

When we write a chemical equation, we represent the starting materials, called reactants, on the left side, and the substances produced, called products, on the right side. We connect them with an arrow that points from reactants to products, which we read as yields or produces. To make these sentences more descriptive, we often include the state of matter for each substance in parentheses. We use the letter S to represent a solid, the letter L for a liquid, the letter G for a gas, and the first two letters of the word aqueous for a substance that is aqueous, meaning it is dissolved in water. For example, solid carbon reacts with oxygen gas to yield carbon dioxide gas.

However, a chemical equation must obey a fundamental law of nature: the law of conservation of mass. Matter cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. This means the number of atoms of each element on the reactant side must exactly match the number of atoms of each element on the product side. To achieve this, we use coefficients. A coefficient is a whole number placed in front of a chemical formula. It tells us how many molecules or formula units of that substance are involved. It is vital to distinguish these from subscripts. A subscript, such as the two in H two O, is part of the identity of the molecule itself. You must never change a subscript to balance an equation, because doing so changes the substance entirely. You only adjust the coefficients, which act like multipliers for the entire formula that follows.

Let us look at a simple example: the reaction of hydrogen gas with oxygen gas to form water. If we write this as hydrogen gas plus oxygen gas yields water, we have two hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms on the left, but only two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom on the right. We have lost an oxygen atom. To fix this, we start by placing a coefficient of two in front of the water molecule. Now we have four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms on the right. To balance the left side, we place a coefficient of two in front of the hydrogen gas. Now, the equation reads: two molecules of hydrogen gas react with one molecule of oxygen gas to yield two molecules of water. We have four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms on both sides, and the mass is conserved.

When a polyatomic ion appears unchanged on both sides of the equation, you can balance it as a single unit rather than counting each individual atom within it. Furthermore, once you finish balancing, you must ensure that your coefficients are reduced to the smallest possible whole-number set. If you accidentally end up with a ratio like four to two, you should divide both coefficients by two to get the simplest two to one ratio.

Consider the combustion of methane, the main component of natural gas. Methane, which contains one carbon and four hydrogen atoms, reacts with oxygen gas to produce carbon dioxide and water. We start with one carbon on each side, which is already balanced. We have four hydrogens on the left, so we need a coefficient of two in front of the water molecule on the right to give us four hydrogens there as well. Now, count the oxygens. We have two in the carbon dioxide and two in the two water molecules, for a total of four on the right. To balance the left, we place a coefficient of two in front of the oxygen gas. The final balanced sentence is: one molecule of methane reacts with two molecules of oxygen gas to yield one molecule of carbon dioxide and two molecules of water.

By mastering these equations, we gain the ability to predict exactly how much of a product we can make from a given amount of starting material. Once we understand how to balance these chemical sentences, we can begin to explore how specific reactions behave in the laboratory.

## Key ideas

- The law of conservation of mass requires that the number of atoms for each element must be equal on both sides of the reaction arrow.
- Coefficients are multipliers used to balance equations, while subscripts are fixed parts of a chemical formula that must never be changed.
- When a polyatomic ion appears on both sides of a reaction, it can be balanced as a single unit to simplify the process.
- After balancing, coefficients must be reduced to the smallest possible set of whole numbers.
