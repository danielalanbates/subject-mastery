# Oxidation-Reduction Reactions

Imagine a rusted iron nail sitting in a damp garden. Over weeks, the silver-gray metal transforms into a brittle, reddish-brown crust. Or consider the steady, rhythmic beat of your heart, powered by the chemical breakdown of the food you eat. These two scenarios, as different as they seem, are driven by the same fundamental process: the transfer of electrons. While our previous lessons explored reactions where ions swap partners to form precipitates or neutralize acids and bases, oxidation-reduction reactions, or redox reactions, involve a deeper shift where electrons leave one atom and are captured by another.

The core of a redox reaction is the movement of electrons. We define oxidation as the loss of electrons from an atom or molecule. Conversely, reduction is the gain of electrons. It helps to remember the mnemonic oil rig, where oxidation involves loss and reduction involves gain. These two processes are inseparable. If one substance loses an electron, another must be standing by to receive it. Electrons do not simply vanish into the ether, nor do they appear from nothing. Therefore, oxidation and reduction always happen simultaneously.

Because these processes are linked, we identify the substances involved by their roles. The species that gains electrons is called the oxidizing agent, because it causes the other substance to lose them. The species that loses electrons is called the reducing agent, because it provides the electrons that cause the other substance to be reduced.

To track these electron movements in complex molecules, chemists use a bookkeeping system called oxidation numbers. An oxidation number is a hypothetical charge an atom would have if all its bonds were purely ionic. For an element in its pure, uncombined state, such as oxygen gas or solid copper, the oxidation number is always zero. When an atom exists as a monatomic ion, like a sodium ion with a charge of positive one, its oxidation number is simply that charge. In compounds, we follow a set of rules based on the periodic table. For instance, oxygen almost always has an oxidation number of negative two, and hydrogen usually carries a positive one when bonded to nonmetals. By assigning these values to every atom in a reaction, we can see exactly which element has become more positive, meaning it lost electrons, and which has become more negative, meaning it gained them.

Let us look at a simple example: the reaction between solid sodium and chlorine gas to form sodium chloride. At the start, both solid sodium and chlorine gas have an oxidation number of zero. During the reaction, each sodium atom loses one electron to become a sodium ion with a charge of positive one. Meanwhile, each chlorine atom gains an electron to become a chloride ion with a charge of negative one. Because sodium lost electrons, we say sodium was oxidized. Because chlorine gained electrons, we say chlorine was reduced. In this case, sodium acts as the reducing agent, and chlorine acts as the oxidizing agent.

Another common class of redox reactions is combustion. When you light a candle or burn natural gas in a stove, you are witnessing a rapid redox reaction between a fuel and oxygen. In the combustion of methane, carbon atoms in the methane molecule lose electrons to the oxygen atoms. This movement releases a significant amount of energy, which we perceive as heat and light. Similarly, when a piece of magnesium metal reacts with hydrochloric acid, the magnesium atoms lose electrons to the hydrogen ions in the acid. The magnesium becomes a magnesium ion, while the hydrogen ions are reduced to form bubbles of hydrogen gas. In these cases, the metal is the reducing agent that provides the electrons.

As you practice assigning these numbers, look for the change in value from the reactant side to the product side. If the oxidation number increases, that atom has been oxidized. If it decreases, it has been reduced. This mental framework allows us to identify the driving force behind a vast array of chemical transformations, from the industrial production of metals to the metabolic pathways within your own cells. Mastering this electron accounting is the final piece of the puzzle before we begin using these balanced equations to calculate exactly how much product we can expect to create in a laboratory setting.

## Key ideas

- Oxidation is defined as the loss of electrons, while reduction is the gain of electrons, and these two processes must always occur together.
- The species that gains electrons is the oxidizing agent, and the species that loses electrons is the reducing agent.
- Oxidation numbers act as a bookkeeping system to track the movement of electrons by assigning a hypothetical charge to each atom in a compound.
- A substance is oxidized if its oxidation number increases, and a substance is reduced if its oxidation number decreases during a reaction.
- Common redox processes include combustion, where fuel reacts with oxygen, and the reaction of metals with acids to produce hydrogen gas.
