# The Law of Supply

Imagine you manage a local bakery, and the market price for artisan sourdough bread suddenly doubles. What is your immediate instinct? You will likely call your bakers in early, buy an extra bag of flour, and fire up the ovens for a second daily batch. You do this because higher prices mean higher revenue per loaf, making it worthwhile to produce more, even if it requires paying overtime wages or burning extra electricity. This everyday business instinct captures the beating heart of microeconomics known as the law of supply. While our last lesson explored how consumers respond to shifting price tags, we now turn our attention to the other side of the marketplace to see how producers behave.

The law of supply states that, holding everything else constant, a rise in the price of a good or service leads to an increase in the quantity supplied, while a fall in price leads to a decrease. Notice the directional relationship here. It is a positive or direct correlation, moving in stark contrast to the inverse relationship we saw with consumer demand. Economists visualize this relationship on a graph as a supply curve, an upward-sloping line rising from left to right. The vertical axis measures the price, and the horizontal axis measures the total quantity that producers are willing and able to sell.

Why does this curve slope upward? The answer lies in the increasing marginal cost of production. Suppose our bakery can produce one hundred loaves a day using its standard equipment and regular staff. If we want to push production to one hundred fifty loaves, we have to crowd the kitchen and pay overtime. Each additional loaf becomes more expensive to bake than the one before it. Therefore, a producer will only agree to incur those higher extra costs if the market price is high enough to cover them and still yield a profit. As the market price climbs higher, it entices firms to scale up production and tempts new competitors to enter the industry.

We must make a careful distinction between moving along an existing supply curve and shifting the entire curve itself. A change in the price of the product causes movement along the curve. If the price of sourdough rises from four dollars to six dollars, we slide up along our current supply curve, resulting in a higher quantity supplied. But what happens if the price stays completely unchanged, yet our production costs drop because we purchase a high-speed industrial mixer? That is an entirely different economic event.

When outside factors change production conditions, the entire supply curve shifts to the right or to the left. Economists call these the non-price determinants of supply. The first major shifter is the cost of inputs, which includes raw materials, labor, and energy. If the price of wheat flour triples, baking bread becomes more expensive at every single price point, causing the supply curve to shift inward to the left, meaning producers now supply fewer loaves at any given price. Conversely, a drop in input costs shifts the supply curve outward to the right.

Technology represents the second great engine of supply shifts. Innovation allows producers to squeeze more output out of the exact same amount of labor and capital. When a bakery adopts automated dough kneaders, its overall productivity increases, shifting the supply curve to the right. The third shifter is the number of sellers in the market. If dozens of new bakeries open their doors in your neighborhood, the aggregate market supply for sourdough increases dramatically, shifting the total supply curve outward regardless of the current price level. Expectations about the future also play a role; if farmers expect wheat prices to soar next month, they may withhold some of their current crop from the market, shifting current supply inward.

Understanding these mechanics allows us to look past simple price tags and see the complex choreography of production costs, technology, and market competition at work. Knowing how producers react to incentives gives us half of the market puzzle. As we move to our next lesson, we will bring producers and consumers together to see how supply and demand intersect to discover the market price.

## Key ideas
- The law of supply states that a higher price leads to an increase in the quantity supplied, creating an upward-sloping supply curve.
- The upward slope of the supply curve reflects increasing marginal costs, meaning producers need higher prices to justify the extra expense of producing more units.
- A change in the price of a good causes movement along an existing supply curve, changing the quantity supplied.
- Non-price determinants such as input costs, technology, and the number of sellers shift the entire supply curve to the right or left.
