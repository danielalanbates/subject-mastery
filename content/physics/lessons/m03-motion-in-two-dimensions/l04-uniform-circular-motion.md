# Uniform Circular Motion

Imagine you are driving a car on a perfectly circular track, holding your steering wheel steady so the speedometer reads a constant sixty miles per hour. Your speed is not changing at all. Yet, if you close your eyes for just a moment, you feel a distinct pull pushing you outward against the door. How can you be experiencing a push or a pull when your speed is completely steady? The answer lies in the subtle difference between speed and velocity, and it introduces us to one of the most fascinating topics in kinematics. Even when an object moves in a circle at a constant speed, it is constantly accelerating.

To understand why, we recall that velocity is a vector quantity, possessing both a magnitude, which is our speed, and a direction. When you drive along a curved path, your car is constantly changing the direction it points. At one moment you are heading north, a moment later north-northeast, and then east. Because your direction is continually shifting, your velocity vector is continually changing. Acceleration is defined as any change in velocity over time. Since velocity includes direction as well as speed, changing your direction means you are accelerating, even if your speedometer never budges. Furthermore, centripetal acceleration is always perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity vector at any given point along the path. Because this acceleration acts at a right angle to the motion, it changes only the direction of the velocity, leaving the magnitude, which is the speed, entirely constant.

Where does this acceleration point? It points directly toward the center of the circle. Picture a heavy ball on the end of a string, whirling around your head in a horizontal circle. If you let go of the string, the ball flies off in a straight line tangent to the circle, following the direction it was moving the exact instant you released it. The string was exerting a continuous inward pull on the ball, bending its path away from a straight line and forcing it into a circle. That inward pull produces an inward acceleration, which physicists call centripetal acceleration.

An object cannot move in a circle without a net inward force acting upon it. In the physical world, various real forces can provide this essential centripetal pull. When a car rounds a curve, static friction between the tires and the road supplies the inward force. When the ball whirls on a string, the tension in the string provides it. When the moon orbits the earth, the force of gravity supplies it. Without one of these real forces acting toward the center, the object would simply continue moving in a straight line as Newton's laws dictate.

Sometimes people speak of a centrifugal force pushing outward in a circular path. In physics, however, centrifugal force is not a real force acting on the object at all. Instead, it is a fictitious force observed only when you try to analyze motion from a rotating reference frame, such as the inside of a turning car. From an inertial reference frame outside the car, no outward force exists; you feel pushed outward simply because your body tends to travel in a straight line while the car turns beneath you.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Suppose a small training aircraft flies in a horizontal circle with a radius of one hundred meters, completing one full circuit every twenty seconds. What is the centripetal acceleration of the airplane? First, we find the distance of one full trip around the circle, which is the circumference, calculated as two times pi times the radius. Using three point one four for pi, the circumference is two times three point one four times one hundred meters, giving six hundred twenty-eight meters. Dividing that distance by the twenty seconds it takes to complete the loop tells us the speed is thirty-one point four meters per second.

Next, we calculate the centripetal acceleration. The magnitude of this acceleration equals the square of the speed divided by the radius of the circle. We square our speed of thirty-one point four meters per second, which gives roughly nine hundred eighty-six. Then we divide that result by our radius of one hundred meters, yielding about nine point eight six meters per second squared. This acceleration is almost exactly equal to standard gravity near the surface of the earth. We can also express centripetal acceleration using angular speed, which measures the angle swept out per second. Angular speed squared times the radius gives the exact same acceleration magnitude, and angular speed itself relates directly to the period of motion or its frequency.

This center-seeking acceleration is the hidden engine behind countless everyday experiences, from rounding a highway curve in an automobile to the orbit of the moon around the earth. Recognizing that circular motion requires a continuous inward acceleration bridges the gap between how things move in straight lines and how they navigate curves. As we prepare to leave pure kinematics behind, we must ask what physical agent actually provides this inward pull. That inquiry leads us directly to the concept of inertia and the founding principles of dynamics.

## Key ideas
- An object moving in a circle at a constant speed is still accelerating because its direction of motion is continually changing.
- Centripetal acceleration always points inward toward the center of the path and remains perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity.
- A net inward force, provided by real forces such as tension, friction, or gravity, is strictly required to maintain circular motion.
- Centripetal acceleration can be calculated using speed squared divided by radius, or alternatively using angular speed squared times radius.
- Centrifugal force is not a real force acting on an object, but rather a fictitious force perceived from a rotating reference frame.
