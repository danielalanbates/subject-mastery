# Sum, Difference and Double-Angle Formulas

Imagine you are standing on a balcony, trying to calculate the exact angle of a beam of light hitting a surface, but you only know the basic angles of two separate light sources. You might know the sine of thirty degrees and the sine of forty-five degrees, but what if you need the sine of seventy-five degrees? In our previous lesson, we explored fundamental trigonometric identities that relate basic functions like sine and cosine to one another. Today, we expand those tools by discovering how to combine angles, which allows us to calculate the exact values of trigonometric functions for angles that are not on the standard unit circle.

The core of this topic lies in the sum and difference formulas. These rules tell us that the sine of the sum of two angles, let us call them theta and phi, is equal to the sine of theta times the cosine of phi plus the cosine of theta times the sine of phi. Similarly, the cosine of the sum of theta and phi is the cosine of theta times the cosine of phi minus the sine of theta times the sine of phi. Notice the pattern here: the sine formula mixes sine and cosine, while the cosine formula keeps the functions together. When we move to subtraction, the sine of the difference of theta and phi follows the same structure but with a minus sign, while the cosine of the difference of theta and phi uses a plus sign. These identities work because they reflect the underlying geometry of rotating coordinate systems.

Let us test this with an exact value. Suppose we want to find the sine of seventy-five degrees. We can think of seventy-five as the sum of forty-five degrees and thirty degrees. Using our formula, the sine of the sum of forty-five degrees and thirty degrees equals the sine of forty-five degrees times the cosine of thirty degrees plus the cosine of forty-five degrees times the sine of thirty degrees. We know the sine of forty-five degrees is the square root of two over two, and the cosine of thirty degrees is the square root of three over two. The cosine of forty-five degrees is the square root of two over two, and the sine of thirty degrees is one half. Multiplying the first pair gives us the square root of six over four. Multiplying the second pair gives us the square root of two over four. Adding these together, we get the quantity square root of six plus square root of two, all divided by four. This is an exact value, far more precise than a decimal approximation.

From these sum formulas, we derive the double-angle formulas. If we set theta and phi to be the same angle, let us call it omega, the sum formulas simplify beautifully. The sine of two times omega becomes two times the sine of omega times the cosine of omega. For cosine, the double-angle formula is the cosine of two times omega equals the cosine squared of omega minus the sine squared of omega. Because we know the Pythagorean identity, which states that sine squared of omega plus cosine squared of omega equals one, we can rewrite the cosine double-angle formula in two other useful ways. We can express it as two times the cosine squared of omega minus one, or as one minus two times the sine squared of omega.

These double-angle formulas are powerful tools for simplifying complex trigonometric expressions. For instance, if you encounter an expression like two times the sine of fifteen degrees times the cosine of fifteen degrees, you might be tempted to calculate each part. Instead, you can recognize this as the double-angle form of the sine function. Since two times fifteen is thirty, the entire expression is simply equal to the sine of thirty degrees, which is one half. Recognizing these patterns saves time and prevents errors during more complex manipulations.

As you practice these, remember that the signs are the most common source of mistakes. Always check whether you are adding or subtracting within the parentheses, as that determines which operator to use in your final expansion. Mastering these formulas allows you to break down complicated trigonometric expressions into manageable parts, turning what looks like an impossible calculation into a straightforward arithmetic problem. Now that we have the ability to manipulate and expand trigonometric expressions, we are ready to apply these skills to solve equations where the variable is locked inside a trigonometric function.

## Key ideas

- The sum and difference formulas for sine and cosine allow us to calculate exact trigonometric values for angles that are combinations of standard unit circle angles.
- The sine of a sum involves a product of mixed sine and cosine terms, while the cosine of a sum involves products of the same functions.
- Double-angle formulas are a special case of the sum formulas where both angles are equal, providing simplified expressions for sine and cosine of twice an angle.
- These identities are essential for simplifying complex trigonometric expressions and solving equations where multiple angles or functions are present.
