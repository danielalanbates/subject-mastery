# Mathematical Induction

Imagine standing before an endless line of upright dominoes stretching out into the distance, each placed just close enough to tip the next. If you push the first domino over, and if you know that any single domino falling will reliably strike its immediate neighbor, you can be certain that every single domino in the entire infinite line will eventually fall. In mathematics, this magnificent picture of unstoppable, sequential propagation is called mathematical induction. It is our primary tool for proving statements that must hold true for every natural number, an infinity of cases that we could never check one by one by hand. Before we begin, let us establish our convention: in this course, the natural numbers begin with one, so our sequence of numbers starts with one, two, three, and continues upward without end.

In our previous lesson, we explored proof by contradiction, where we assume a statement is false and chase that assumption until it shatters against an impossibility. Induction works differently. Instead of tearing down an alternative, it builds a staircase. To prove a statement for every natural number using induction, we must execute two distinct steps. First, we establish the base case, proving that the statement holds true for the very first number, which is one. This is our push of the first domino. Second, we prove the inductive step, demonstrating that if the statement holds for some arbitrary natural number, let us call it letter k, it must logically follow for the next number, which is the quantity letter k plus one.

Let us watch this engine run on a concrete mathematical object from our earlier work with sequences. Consider the sum of the first letter n odd numbers: one, plus three, plus five, all the way up to the odd number two times the quantity letter n minus one. If you add the first one, you get one. If you add the first two, one plus three, you get four. If you add the first three, one plus three plus five, you get nine. You might notice a striking pattern. One is one squared, four is two squared, nine is three squared. The sum of the first letter n odd numbers always equals letter n squared. But how can we be certain this holds for a billion, or for any natural number whatsoever?

We turn to mathematical induction. For our base case, we test letter n equals one. The sum of the first one odd number is just one, and one squared is also one. Both sides match, so our first domino has fallen. Now for the inductive step. We make a temporary assumption, known as the inductive hypothesis, that the formula works for some arbitrary natural number letter k. That means we assume the sum of the first letter k odd numbers, from one up to two times the quantity letter k minus one, equals letter k squared.

Our job is to show that, using this assumption, the formula must also work for the next number. The sum of the first (letter k plus one) odd numbers is our old sum of the first letter k odd numbers, plus the new, next odd number, which is two times the quantity letter k plus one, minus one. By our inductive hypothesis, we can replace that initial sum with letter k squared. We then simplify the new odd term: two times the quantity letter k plus one, minus one, which expands to two times letter k plus two minus one, giving us two times letter k plus one.

Putting those pieces together, our new total is letter k squared plus two times letter k, plus one. You might recognize this expression from our factoring work as a perfect square trinomial. It factors neatly into the quantity letter k plus one squared. Look at what we have just achieved. Assuming the formula worked for letter k, we proved it must work for the quantity letter k plus one. Because our base case checked out for one, the inductive step guarantees it works for two, which in turn guarantees it works for three, marching forward forever into infinity. Having mastered this method of building infinite bridges of truth, we are now ready to explore how sets and functions serve as the shared language of all modern mathematics.

## Key ideas
- Mathematical induction is a method of proof used to establish that a statement is true for every natural number by connecting an infinite chain of logical implications.
- This course follows the convention that the natural numbers begin with the number one.
- The base case proves that the statement holds for the starting number, typically one, serving as the push of the first domino.
- The inductive step assumes the statement is true for an arbitrary number letter k and proves it must then hold for the quantity letter k plus one.
- Combining a verified base case with a proven inductive step guarantees that the statement is true for all subsequent natural numbers without needing to check each one individually.
