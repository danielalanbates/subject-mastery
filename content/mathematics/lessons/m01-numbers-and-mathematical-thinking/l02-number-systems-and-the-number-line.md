# The Number Systems and the Number Line

Imagine holding a basket that can only hold whole items like apples and oranges. You cannot put half an apple in it, nor can you owe someone fruit. That simple limitation mirrors how human counting began thousands of years ago with the natural numbers. In the last lesson, we explored how mathematicians step back to observe patterns in the world. Today, we look at the specific ingredients we use to build those patterns: the expanding nested sets of numbers that make up the real number line.

We begin with the natural numbers, the counting numbers one, two, three, and so on. In some mathematical traditions, zero is included in this set, while in others it is not; in this course, we will follow the common convention of treating the natural numbers as starting at one. But humanity soon outgrew these basic tools. To measure debts or temperatures dropping below freezing, we need negative numbers. By combining the natural numbers, their negative opposites, and zero, we form the integers: negative three, negative two, negative one, zero, one, two, three. Notice that every natural number is already an integer. The natural numbers sit entirely inside the integers like a smaller box nestled within a larger one.

Yet, sharing a pizza or dividing a tract of land requires something more fluid. This leads to the rational numbers, which are any numbers that can be written as a fraction where the top and bottom are integers, and the bottom is not zero. Every integer is also a rational number because you can write any integer, such as four, as four over one. When you convert a rational number into a decimal, it either terminates, like zero point two five, or it falls into a repeating pattern, like zero point three three three repeating. Conversely, any decimal that terminates or repeats represents a rational number.

Long before modern algebra, ancient Greek geometry revealed that some lengths cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers. The diagonal of a square with sides of length one has a length that cannot be written as any fraction. These are the irrational numbers, whose decimal expansions go on forever without ever settling into a repeating cycle. Famous examples include the square root of two and the constant pi. The irrational numbers live side by side with the rational numbers, completely filling in all the microscopic gaps between them.

When we gather all the rational numbers and all the irrational numbers together into one massive collection, we arrive at the real numbers. Every single real number corresponds to one exact point on an unbroken, continuous line stretching infinitely in both directions. We call this the real number line. On this line, numbers are strictly ordered: a number is less than another if it lies to the left of it. For example, negative five sits to the left of negative two, so negative five is less than negative two.

Ordering numbers on a line brings us to a vital measurement tool: absolute value. The absolute value of a number is its distance from zero on the number line, regardless of direction. Distance is always a non-negative quantity. For instance, the absolute value of negative four is four, because negative four is four units away from zero. Similarly, the absolute value of four is also four. When we say the absolute value of the quantity x minus three equals five, we are asking which numbers sit exactly five units away from three on our number line.

As we move forward, we will take these number systems and examine the bedrock rules of arithmetic that govern how they interact under addition and multiplication.

## Key ideas
- The natural numbers, integers, rational numbers, and irrational numbers form nested sets that expand to solve new mathematical and practical problems.
- Every rational number can be expressed as a ratio of integers and produces a decimal that either terminates or repeats, and conversely, any terminating or repeating decimal is a rational number.
- Irrational numbers have non-terminating, non-repeating decimal expansions and cannot be written as simple fractions.
- The real numbers combine both rational and irrational numbers, filling every point on an unbroken number line.
- Absolute value measures the distance of a number from zero on the number line, meaning it is always a non-negative value.
