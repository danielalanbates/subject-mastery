# Antiderivatives and Riemann Sums

Imagine you are driving a car along a straight road, and your speedometer is broken. You cannot read your instantaneous speed, but you have a device that logs your acceleration every second. In our recent work on optimization and motion, we learned how to start with a position function and take derivatives to find velocity and acceleration. But now, we face the reverse journey. Can we rebuild our entire trip, figuring out the total distance we traveled, if all we have is a record of how our speed changed over time?

This reverse process is the heart of integral calculus. Just as subtraction undoes addition, and division undoes multiplication, integration undoes differentiation. In our previous studies, we mastered taking a function and finding its derivative, moving from a position formula down to a velocity formula. Now, we start with a velocity formula and ask what original function produced it. We call this reverse operation finding an antiderivative. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz introduced the elongated letter s for integration, while Isaac Newton marked integrals with a small vertical bar above a letter or a box drawn around it, though the modern notation we use most often stems from Leibniz and includes the integral sign, the function, and the differential.

Let us make this concrete with a simple example. Suppose a falling object has a velocity given by two times the variable t, measured in meters per second. We want to find a position function whose derivative is equal to two times the variable t. Thinking backward through our derivative rules, we know that the derivative of the variable t squared is equal to two times the variable t. Therefore, the function t squared is an antiderivative of two times the variable t. If we take the derivative of t squared with respect to t, we get our velocity back, which confirms our work is correct.

Yet, there is a catch. What if the original position function was t squared plus five, or t squared minus ten? When we take the derivative of any constant number, the result is zero. This means infinitely many functions share the exact same derivative. To account for this, whenever we find an antiderivative, we must add a constant of integration, which we usually call the constant c. We write this formally by saying that the indefinite integral of f of t with respect to t equals capital F of t plus the constant c, where capital F is any one antiderivative of f. So the most general antiderivative of two times the variable t is the quantity t squared plus the constant c. Sometimes we are given an initial condition, such as knowing that the position is ten when time is zero. We can substitute these values into our general antiderivative to solve directly for the constant c, locking down a single unique function for our specific physical situation.

While antiderivatives let us work backward algebraically, we also need a way to measure accumulation directly from a changing rate, such as finding the total area under a velocity curve. Think about approximating the area under the curve of our velocity function from time zero to time three. Since the curve is sloped, we cannot simply use a rectangle of a fixed height. Instead, we can slice the region under the curve into several vertical strips and top each strip with a flat rectangle.

We can estimate the total area by adding up the areas of these rectangles. Suppose we use three rectangles of equal width, with the right edge of each rectangle touching our velocity curve. The width of each time interval is one. The heights of our rectangles are given by evaluating our velocity function at the right endpoints, which are one, two, and three. Evaluating two times t at these points gives heights of two, four, and six. Multiplying each height by the width of one and adding them together, we get two plus four plus six, which totals twelve.

This technique of adding up the areas of slices is named a Riemann sum, honoring the nineteenth-century mathematician Bernhard Riemann. If three rectangles give us a rough approximation, what happens if we use six rectangles, or fifty, or a million? As we make the width of each rectangle thinner and thinner, the number of rectangles approaches infinity. The sum of their areas squeezes closer and closer to the exact net area under the curve. Taking the limit of a Riemann sum as the width of the rectangles approaches zero defines the definite integral. Evaluating our antiderivative at the interval endpoints gives this exact same net accumulation value, which is the foundational connection of calculus that bridges slopes and areas.

## Key ideas
- Antiderivatives reverse the process of differentiation, and we state the indefinite integral of f of x with respect to x equals capital F of x plus the constant c, where capital F is any antiderivative of f.
- Because the derivative of any constant number is zero, every indefinite integral must include a constant of integration, which can be determined if we know an initial condition.
- A Riemann sum approximates the total area under a curve by dividing the region into vertical strips and summing the areas of approximating rectangles.
- Taking the limit of a Riemann sum as the width of the rectangles approaches zero defines the definite integral, yielding the same value found by evaluating an antiderivative at the interval endpoints.
