# Exponential and Natural Logarithm Derivatives

Imagine a colony of bacteria whose population mirrors the natural constant e. It grows at a rate precisely equal to its current size. When you try to find the slope of this population curve using the definition of the derivative as a limit, you stumble upon a remarkable algebraic limit. It involves the quantity e to the power of h, minus one, all divided by h, as h approaches zero. Because the function describes a process where growth is proportional to value, its derivative turns out to be remarkably simple. Today, we unlock the calculus of growth itself by differentiating functions built from the natural constant e and the natural logarithm.

The natural constant e, approximately equal to two point seven one eight, is the unique base whose exponential function is its own derivative. If you define a function f of x as e to the power of x, its derivative, f prime of x, is simply e to the power of x. To understand why this happens without relying on a picture, imagine a curve whose vertical height at any point matches the steepness of its tangent line at that exact same coordinate. Growth and rate of change are identical.

Historically, the study of these rates sparked a fierce priority dispute between Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. Newton used dot notation, placing a small dot above a variable to represent its derivative with respect to time. Leibniz introduced the d notation we use today, writing expressions like d y over d x to show a ratio of infinitesimal changes.

When exponential functions involve more complex exponents, we lean on the chain rule. Suppose we want to find the derivative of e to the power of two times x. We treat the exponent two times x as an inner function. The outer derivative of e to the power of u is just e to the power of u, and the inner derivative of two times x with respect to x is two. Multiplying them together gives two times e to the power of two times x. In general, the derivative of e to the power of u, where u is any differentiable function of x, is e to the power of u multiplied by u prime.

This exponential rule extends to any positive base other than e. The derivative with respect to x of b to the power of x, where b is a positive constant not equal to one, equals b to the power of x multiplied by the natural logarithm of b. We multiply by the natural logarithm of b because changing the base introduces a scaling factor that measures how quickly that specific exponential base grows compared to base e.

Let us walk through a concrete example step by step using a product. Consider the function f of x defined as e to the power of negative three times x, multiplied by x squared. This function combines an exponential term with a power term, so we must use the product rule. The product rule tells us to take the derivative of the first factor multiplied by the second, plus the first factor multiplied by the derivative of the second. Our first factor is e to the power of negative three times x, and its derivative, using our exponential rule, is negative three times e to the power of negative three times x. Our second factor is x squared, and its derivative is two times x.

Now we assemble the pieces. We multiply the derivative of the first factor by the unmodified second factor. Then we add the unmodified first factor multiplied by the derivative of the second factor. Factoring out the common terms of e to the power of negative three times x and x, we get the final result: x times e to the power of negative three times x, multiplied by the quantity two minus three times x.

Just as the exponential function has a special derivative, its inverse, the natural logarithm, possesses an equally elegant rule. If f of x is the natural logarithm of x, its derivative, f prime of x, is one divided by x. This rule extends to the natural logarithm of absolute value. The derivative with respect to x of the natural logarithm of the absolute value of x is one divided by x, valid for any value of x not equal to zero. Absolute value bars ensure the logarithm receives only positive inputs while preserving the domain of the derivative for negative numbers as well.

If we wish to use other bases for logarithms, a change of base formula yields another useful rule. The derivative with respect to x of the logarithm base b of x is equal to one divided by the quantity x multiplied by the natural logarithm of b, where b is a positive constant not equal to one.

If we combine the natural logarithm with the chain rule, the derivative of the natural logarithm of u, where u is a positive differentiable function of x, becomes u prime divided by u. For instance, if f of x is the natural logarithm of the quantity three times x plus four, we differentiate the inner function three times x plus four to get three, and place it over the original inner function. The resulting derivative is three divided by the quantity three times x plus four. These rules for e and the natural logarithm open the door to analyzing complex compounding interest, radioactive decay, and logistic populations. Next, we will turn our attention to how these ideas extend to inverse functions and inverse trigonometric derivatives.

## Key ideas
- The derivative of e to the power of x is simply e to the power of x, meaning the function grows at a rate equal to its own value.
- The derivative of a general exponential b to the power of x equals b to the power of x multiplied by the natural logarithm of b.
- The derivative of the natural logarithm of the absolute value of x is one divided by x for x not equal to zero.
- The derivative of logarithms with other bases, such as the derivative of the logarithm base b of x, is one divided by the quantity x multiplied by the natural logarithm of b.
- Combining the natural logarithm with the chain rule gives the derivative of the natural logarithm of u as u prime divided by u.
