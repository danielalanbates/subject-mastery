# The Rule of Faith, Bishops and Canon

Imagine you are a traveler in the second century, moving from a house church in Rome to another in Ephesus, located in what is now western Turkey. You would notice something striking. Despite the miles between them, the basic message and the way these communities organized themselves felt remarkably similar. In our last lesson, we saw how Gnostics and leaders like Marcion tried to reshape the Christian message to fit their own philosophical agendas. To counter these threats, the early church developed three essential tools for stability: the rule of faith, the office of the bishop, and the formation of the New Testament canon.

The rule of faith was not a formal document like the later creeds we know today. Instead, it was a summary of the core Christian story, passed down orally. It acted like a compass for believers. Whenever a new teacher arrived with a strange or innovative idea, the community held it up against the rule of faith. If the teaching contradicted the story of God as creator, the incarnation of Jesus, and the promise of resurrection, it was rejected. It was a shared memory that kept the church’s identity intact even when it was scattered across the Roman Empire.

As the church grew, it also needed a way to handle leadership disputes. By the early second century, many churches began to move toward a model of a single, presiding leader in each city, known as the bishop. This structure was designed to provide a clear point of accountability. The bishop served as the guardian of the tradition. Because he was connected to the community’s history and often traced his succession back to the apostles, he provided a sense of continuity.

How we interpret this development depends on which tradition we ask. Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Christians generally view the emergence of the single bishop as a divinely guided evolution, essential for maintaining the unity and sacramental life of the church. They see the authority of the bishop as a continuation of apostolic leadership. On the other hand, many Protestant traditions tend to view this development with more caution. They often argue that while the office of bishop was a practical, perhaps even helpful, administrative response to the challenges of the age, it was not necessarily a requirement for the church’s validity. For them, the authority of the church rests more directly on the proclamation of the gospel and the faithful reading of the Bible.

While the rule of faith provided the message and the bishops provided the leadership, the church still needed to define its library. Which writings were truly authoritative? This was the process of forming the New Testament canon. It was not a sudden decision made by a single committee. It was a slow, centuries-long process of discernment. Churches across the Mediterranean began to circulate letters from Paul, the four Gospels, and other apostolic writings. They read these aloud in their gatherings, comparing them to the rule of faith they had received.

By the end of the second century, the core of the New Testament—the four Gospels and the letters of Paul—was widely accepted as scripture. Books that were considered Gnostic or that falsely claimed to be written by apostles were excluded because they did not match the message held by the majority of the churches. The canon, therefore, was not a list imposed from the top down. It was a collection of writings that the community had already found to be essential for their life, worship, and understanding of Jesus.

These three elements—the rule of faith, the authority of the bishop, and the canon—formed the framework of the church. They protected the gospel from being diluted by outside philosophies and held the diverse, growing church together during a time of intense pressure from the Roman state. These developments did not happen in a vacuum, however. They were the result of real, breathing human beings wrestling with what it meant to be the body of Christ. In our next lesson, we will meet some of the most influential thinkers who helped sharpen these tools, including Irenaeus, Tertullian, Origen, and Cyprian.

## Key ideas

- The rule of faith served as an oral summary of the Christian message, allowing early believers to identify and reject teachings that deviated from the apostolic witness.
- The office of the bishop emerged as a way to provide stability and accountability, though Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions interpret the theological necessity of this development differently.
- The New Testament canon took shape over several centuries as local churches discerned which writings were consistent with the gospel and truly representative of the apostolic tradition.
- These three tools—the rule of faith, the episcopacy, and the canon—collectively provided the structural foundation that allowed the church to maintain its identity during the era of Roman persecution.
