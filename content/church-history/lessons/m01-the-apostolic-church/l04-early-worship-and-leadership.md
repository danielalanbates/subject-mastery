# Early Worship and Leadership

Imagine walking into a house in Corinth, in what is now Greece, or Ephesus, in what is now Turkey, during the middle of the first century. You would not find a building designed like a temple or a sanctuary. Instead, you would enter a private home, moving through the courtyard into a dining area. The early Christians gathered in these intimate settings, reflecting a movement that was deeply communal. Following the rapid expansion of the faith, these small, house-based communities became the primary hubs for worship and instruction.

Worship in these early churches was shaped by Jewish synagogue traditions and the new reality of the resurrection of Jesus. They continued to read the Jewish scriptures, which they viewed as the foundation of their faith. However, a significant shift occurred as believers began to gather on the first day of the week, Sunday, which they called the Lord's Day. Acts describes believers meeting on the first day of the week, Paul asks the Corinthians to set money aside on that day, and the book of Revelation speaks of the Lord's Day. Many believers of Jewish background still kept the Sabbath as well, but over time Sunday became the main day of Christian worship.

Their gatherings were punctuated by two central rituals: baptism and the Lord’s Supper. Baptism served as the public entry point into the community. Early practice, as reflected in the Didache, an ancient manual of church instruction, often involved immersion in running water, accompanied by pre-baptismal fasting and the use of a trinitarian formula, invoking the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. The Lord’s Supper, or the breaking of bread, was a communal meal that recalled Jesus’s last night with his disciples. It was a profound experience of fellowship where the presence of the risen Christ was believed to be felt among the people.

Leadership in these early years was fluid and often charismatic. Alongside local, settled leaders, the church relied on itinerant ministers, including apostles, prophets, and teachers who traveled between communities to provide guidance. Within the local house churches, the New Testament identifies roles such as elders, or presbyters, who provided spiritual oversight, and deacons, who managed practical ministry and the care of the poor. The term bishop, or episkopos in Greek, also appeared, initially used interchangeably with elder to describe the person tasked with watching over the health of the community.

The role of women in these settings remains a subject of significant scholarly disagreement. Some scholars, often called egalitarians, point to figures like Phoebe, a deacon in Cenchreae, and Priscilla, who taught and hosted house churches, as evidence that women held positions of authority and teaching equal to men. Other scholars, known as complementarians, argue that passages such as the first letter to Timothy or the first letter to the Corinthians indicate that while women were essential coworkers and patrons, the formal roles of elder and teacher were reserved for men. Both sides acknowledge that women were foundational to the mission and daily operation of the church, even as they interpret the biblical instructions regarding leadership roles differently.

The Didache provides us with a window into how these communities tested their leaders, instructing the faithful to discern true prophets from those who might seek personal gain. This structure of local elders and deacons, supported by the visits of traveling ministers, provided a framework for the church to endure. It allowed the movement to maintain a consistent message while adapting to different cultural contexts.

As the number of believers increased and the original apostles passed away, the need for a more formal and settled structure became pressing. This growing organization happened alongside a cooling of the relationship between the Jesus movement and the broader Jewish community. The church was learning how to be a distinct body, balancing its Jewish heritage with its increasingly Gentile identity. As these structures solidified, the community faced the reality of its own unique existence apart from the local synagogue. The tension between the growing church and its ancestral roots would soon lead to a profound shift in how Christians understood their place in the world.

## Key ideas

- Early Christian worship centered on the reading of scripture, the ritual of baptism, and the shared meal known as the Lord's Supper, with a shift to Sunday as the primary day of gathering.
- Leadership was a blend of settled local roles like elders and deacons alongside charismatic, itinerant ministries including apostles, prophets, and teachers.
- The Didache serves as a vital historical source for understanding early baptismal practices, Eucharistic prayers, and the testing of church leaders.
- Scholars disagree on the nature of women's leadership in the early church, with egalitarian and complementarian views offering different readings of the New Testament evidence.
- These emerging structures and rituals allowed the early church to maintain its identity and mission during a period of rapid expansion across the Roman world.
