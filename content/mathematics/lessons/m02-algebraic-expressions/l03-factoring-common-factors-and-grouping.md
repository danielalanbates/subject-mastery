# Factoring: Common Factors and Grouping

Imagine you are standing before a large mosaic made of hundreds of tiny, colored tiles, and your task is to figure out which rectangular stamps were used to stamp out blocks of the pattern in a single press. In our previous lesson, we learned how to build polynomials by multiplying expressions together, distributing terms like a machine stamping out products. Factoring is simply that process run in reverse. If multiplication takes us from factors to a product, factoring takes us from a product right back to the original factors. It is a fundamental art in algebra, turning a sum of terms into a neat, compact product.

To understand factoring by the greatest common factor, let us recall the distributive property of arithmetic. If we take the number three and multiply it by the sum of four plus five, we get three times nine, which equals twenty-seven. By distributing the three, we get three times four plus three times five, which is twelve plus fifteen, also equalling twenty-seven. Factoring does the exact opposite. If we begin with twelve plus fifteen, we search for the largest number that divides evenly into both twelve and fifteen. That number is three. We pull that three out front, leaving us with three times the quantity four plus five.

We can do the exact same thing with algebraic expressions. Suppose we have the polynomial six x squared plus nine x. Look closely at the numerical coefficients, six and nine. The greatest common factor of six and nine is three. Now look at the variable parts, x squared and x. The highest power of x that divides both terms is simply x. That means our greatest common factor for the entire expression is three x. To find what remains inside the grouping, we divide each term of our original polynomial by three x. Dividing six x squared by three x leaves us with two x. Dividing nine x by three x leaves us with three. Putting it all together, our factored expression is three x times the quantity two x plus three.

Always begin by factoring out any overall greatest common factor before attempting any other method, including grouping. Sometimes the leading coefficient of that greatest common factor is negative. If our polynomial is negative six x squared minus nine x, the leading term is negative, so we factor out a negative three x. Dividing negative six x squared by negative three x leaves us with two x, and dividing negative nine x by negative three x leaves us with positive three. This gives us negative three x times the quantity two x plus three. Factoring out a negative greatest common factor requires careful attention to signs because dividing by a negative changes the sign of every remaining term inside the parentheses.

How can we be certain our answer is correct? We check our work by multiplying back. If we take our factored result, three x times the quantity two x plus three, and distribute the three x, we multiply three x by two x to get six x squared, and three x by three to get nine x. We return precisely to our starting polynomial, six x squared plus nine x. Checking by multiplication is your built-in safety net in algebra. Whenever you factor, multiply your result back out to confirm it matches the original expression.

Sometimes an expression has four terms, and no single greatest common factor divides all of them. When this happens, we turn to a strategy called factoring by grouping. Always check for an overall greatest common factor first, and then split the polynomial down the middle, grouping the first two terms together and the last two terms together. Imagine you have a polynomial like two x cubed plus four x squared plus three x plus six. There is no single factor common to all four terms. From the first pair, two x cubed plus four x squared, we pull out the greatest common factor, which is two x squared. That leaves us with two x squared times the quantity x plus two.

Now we look at the second pair of terms, three x plus six. The greatest common factor here is three. We pull out the positive three, leaving us with three times the quantity x plus two. Notice something wonderful has happened. Both of our groups now share an identical binomial factor, the quantity x plus two. Because that binomial is common to both big terms, we can pull it out just like any other common factor. We pull out the quantity x plus two, and what remains is two x squared plus three. So our fully factored expression is the quantity x plus two times the quantity two x squared plus three.

Sometimes grouping yields opposite binomials, such as having an x minus two in one group and a two minus x in the other group. When this happens, we factor out a negative one from one of the groups to obtain a matching binomial, transforming two minus x into negative one times the quantity x minus two so that the binomials align perfectly for factoring out. Other times, a four-term polynomial cannot be factored by grouping at all, no matter how you rearrange the terms or which signs you adjust. Recognizing when a polynomial is prime, meaning it cannot be factored further using integer coefficients, is an essential skill in algebra.

## Key ideas
- Factoring is the reverse process of multiplication, taking a polynomial product and breaking it back down into its constituent factors.
- Always factor out any overall greatest common factor, including a negative greatest common factor when the leading term is negative, before attempting grouping or other methods.
- When grouping yields opposite binomials, factoring out negative one from one group obtains a matching binomial, whereas a polynomial that yields no common binomial structure and cannot be factored is recognized as prime.
- Four-term polynomials can often be factored by grouping, splitting the expression into pairs, factoring each pair separately, and then extracting a common binomial factor.
- Every factoring result can and should be checked by multiplying the factors back together to verify they return the original expression.
