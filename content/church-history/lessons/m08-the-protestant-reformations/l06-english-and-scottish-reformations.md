# The English and Scottish Reformations

Imagine a kingdom where the church’s identity shifts like the tide, tied entirely to the person wearing the crown. While the reformations we have studied so far were often driven by theological conviction or grassroots dissent, the English Reformation began as a matter of statecraft and royal succession. In our last lesson, we saw how the Anabaptists sought to separate the church from the state entirely. In England, the two were bound together in a way that would define the nation for centuries.

The catalyst was King Henry the Eighth. In the late 1520s, Henry became obsessed with the need for a male heir to secure the Tudor dynasty. He sought an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, arguing that their union was invalid. When the Pope refused to grant the annulment, Henry took matters into his own hands. In 1534, through the Act of Supremacy, he declared himself the Supreme Head of the Church of England. This was not initially a theological break from Catholic doctrine; it was a jurisdictional one. Henry kept the mass, the sacraments, and the structures of the old church, but he replaced the Pope with the monarch. During his reign, Henry also oversaw the dissolution of the monasteries, a massive seizure of church property that enriched the crown and fundamentally altered the social and economic structure of the English church.

The character of the English Church changed dramatically under Henry’s son, Edward the Sixth. Thomas Cranmer, the Archbishop of Canterbury, became the primary architect of this transition. Cranmer moved the church toward a reformed theology, most notably through the Book of Common Prayer, which he revised to shift the focus of worship toward the proclamation of the Word in English. Cranmer’s work, including the Ten Articles and the Six Articles, reflected the volatile theological debates of the time. It was a period of rapid change, but it was cut short by Edward’s untimely death in 1553.

England then swung sharply back toward Rome under Queen Mary the First. Mary was a devout Catholic who viewed the reforms of her father and brother as a tragic error. She restored the authority of the Pope and reinstated the Latin mass. This period is remembered for the intense persecution of Protestants, leading to the execution of notable leaders like Thomas Cranmer. Mary’s reign was a reminder that the English Reformation was not a smooth march toward Protestantism, but a turbulent struggle for the soul of the nation.

Stability finally arrived with Elizabeth the First, who took the throne in 1558. She sought a middle way, often called the Elizabethan Settlement. This settlement rested on three pillars: the Act of Supremacy of 1559, which reaffirmed the monarch's authority; the Act of Uniformity of 1559, which mandated the use of the Book of Common Prayer; and the Thirty-Nine Articles of 1563, which defined the church's doctrinal position. Elizabeth established a church that was Protestant in its theology but preserved much of the traditional episcopal structure and aesthetic of the Catholic past. This created a unique identity that remains the foundation of Anglicanism today, though it left many who wanted more thorough reform—the Puritans—unsatisfied.

While England navigated this royal path, the Reformation in Scotland took a very different, more radical turn. The Scottish Reformation was led by John Knox, a preacher influenced by John Calvin. Knox returned to a Scotland dominated by powerful nobles known as the Lords of the Congregation. Following the Treaty of Edinburgh in 1560, which secured the withdrawal of French troops, the Scottish Parliament moved to reform the church. Unlike the English model, where the monarch controlled the church, Knox envisioned a church governed by elders, a system known as Presbyterianism. The First Book of Discipline in 1560 established this new polity, emphasizing kirk sessions for local governance and explicitly abolishing the office of the bishop.

These two nations demonstrate the diverse ways that the Reformation could take root. In England, the reform was a top-down administrative shift that retained bishops and evolved into a national church. In Scotland, it was a movement that asserted the independence of the church from royal control, emphasizing a democratic, grassroots structure. Both journeys highlight the complex interplay between faith, power, and the desire for a purer practice of the Christian life. As these national churches solidified their identities, the Roman Catholic Church began to organize its own internal response to the challenges of the age.

## Key ideas

- The English Reformation began as a political and jurisdictional break from Rome under Henry the Eighth, fueled by the desire for an annulment and the seizure of monastic wealth.
- Thomas Cranmer played a central role in shaping the English Reformation by drafting the Book of Common Prayer and steering the church toward reformed theology.
- The Elizabethan Settlement established the Church of England through the Act of Supremacy, the Act of Uniformity, and the Thirty-Nine Articles, creating a middle way between Catholicism and radical reform.
- The Scottish Reformation, led by John Knox and the Lords of the Congregation, established a Presbyterian system that abolished the office of the bishop in favor of local kirk sessions.
