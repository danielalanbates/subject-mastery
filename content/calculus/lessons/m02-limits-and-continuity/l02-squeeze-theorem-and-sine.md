# The Squeeze Theorem and Sine

Imagine you are walking down a narrow hallway with a friend on your left and another friend on your right. As the hallway narrows, your friends start to move closer together. Eventually, the hallway becomes so thin that your friends are almost touching. If both of your friends walk through a specific doorway at the end of the hall, you have no choice but to walk through that same doorway too. You are trapped between them, and their destination becomes yours. In calculus, we call this the Squeeze Theorem, and it is a powerful tool for finding limits when direct algebra fails us.

In our previous lesson, we used algebraic tricks like factoring to evaluate limits that looked like zero divided by zero. But some functions are not algebraic. Consider the function sine of x divided by x. If we try to find the limit as x approaches zero, we run into a familiar problem. Sine of zero is zero, and the denominator is also zero. This gives us the indeterminate form zero divided by zero. Unlike a polynomial, we cannot factor a sine function to cancel out the x in the bottom. We need a different strategy to see what happens as we get closer and closer to that point.

The Squeeze Theorem, sometimes called the Sandwich Theorem, provides that strategy. Formally, it states that if we have three functions—let's call them g of x, f of x, and h of x—and for all values of x near our target, g of x is less than or equal to f of x, which is in turn less than or equal to h of x, then we have a "sandwich." If the limit of the lower function g of x is some value L, and the limit of the upper function h of x is also that same value L, then the limit of the middle function f of x must also be L. The middle function is squeezed into a single point by the two functions surrounding it.

To find the limit of sine of x divided by x as x approaches zero, we use a classic geometric squeeze. Imagine a unit circle with an angle x measured in radians. By comparing the areas of a small triangle inside the circle, a wedge-shaped sector of the circle, and a larger triangle outside the circle, mathematicians have established a specific inequality. For values of x near zero, cosine of x is always less than sine of x divided by x, and sine of x divided by x is always less than the constant value one.

Now, let's apply the Squeeze Theorem to this inequality as x approaches zero. First, look at the upper bound, which is the constant function one. The limit of a constant is just the constant itself, so the limit of one as x approaches zero is simply one. Next, look at the lower bound, which is cosine of x. As x gets closer and closer to zero, cosine of x gets closer and closer to cosine of zero. We know from trigonometry that cosine of zero is exactly one.

Because the lower function approaches one and the upper function also approaches one, the function trapped in the middle has nowhere else to go. Therefore, the limit as x approaches zero of sine of x divided by x is exactly one. This is one of the most important limits in all of calculus. It tells us that for very small angles, the sine of the angle and the angle itself are almost identical in value. This result will be the foundation for finding the derivatives of all trigonometric functions later in this course.

Let's look at another example to see the Squeeze Theorem in action with a more "wobbly" function. Suppose we want to find the limit as x approaches zero of the quantity x squared times sine of the quantity one divided by x. This function is a bit of a nightmare to look at near zero. As x gets smaller, one divided by x gets larger and larger, causing the sine function to oscillate or wiggle between negative one and positive one faster and faster. However, we know a fundamental fact about the sine function: no matter how big the input is, the output of sine is always trapped between negative one and positive one.

We can use this fact to build our squeeze. Since sine of one divided by x is between negative one and one, if we multiply everything by x squared, we find that our original function is trapped between negative x squared and positive x squared. Now we check the limits of our "bread" functions. As x approaches zero, negative x squared approaches zero. Similarly, as x approaches zero, positive x squared also approaches zero. Since our complicated, wiggling function is squeezed between two parabolas that both meet at the origin, the limit of x squared times sine of one divided by x as x approaches zero must also be zero.

The Squeeze Theorem is a reminder that calculus is about more than just moving symbols around on a page. It is about understanding the behavior of functions by comparing them to things we already know. When a function is too complex to handle directly, we can often find its limit by finding two simpler functions that act as boundaries. Now that we have mastered the tools for calculating limits, we are ready to use those limits to define one of the most essential concepts in mathematics: the idea of a continuous process. In the next lesson, we will explore continuity and the Intermediate Value Theorem.

## Key ideas

- The Squeeze Theorem states that if a function is trapped between two other functions that approach the same limit, the middle function must also approach that same limit.
- The limit as x approaches zero of sine of x divided by x is equal to one, a fundamental result established by squeezing the function between cosine of x and the constant one.
- The Squeeze Theorem is particularly useful for functions involving oscillations, such as sine or cosine, where the oscillating part is bounded between negative one and one.
- To apply the theorem, you must identify a lower bound function and an upper bound function that are easier to evaluate and that meet at the same value at the limit point.
- This geometric approach allows us to solve limits for non-algebraic functions that would otherwise result in an indeterminate form like zero divided by zero.
