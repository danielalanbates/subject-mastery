# Attachment and Early Relationships

Imagine a baby monkey in a laboratory cage. It has two surrogate mothers made of wire and cloth. One wire mother has a nursing bottle, but the other is covered in soft, terry-cloth fabric. If you were to guess which one the infant monkey would prefer, you might assume it would choose the food. In the nineteen-fifties, psychologist Harry Harlow tested this exact scenario. His experimental evidence showed that the infant monkeys spent almost all their time clinging to the soft cloth mother, only reaching over to the wire mother briefly for nourishment. This work provided strong evidence that contact comfort—the physical, tactile reassurance of a soft, warm body—was a fundamental need for healthy development, rather than just a byproduct of feeding.

While Harlow was exploring the importance of touch, British psychiatrist John Bowlby was developing his own theory of attachment. Bowlby’s ideas were rooted in ethological observations of animal behavior and psychoanalytic theory. He argued that infants are biologically predisposed to seek proximity to a primary caregiver as an evolutionary survival mechanism. By keeping a protective adult nearby, the infant increases its chances of staying safe from hazards. Bowlby proposed that these early interactions create an internal working model. This is a cognitive framework that the child uses to understand themselves and their future relationships. If the caregiver is responsive and reliable, the child learns to trust that the world is a generally safe place.

To test these ideas more formally, Mary Ainsworth, a colleague of Bowlby, developed a laboratory procedure known as the Strange Situation. This observational study relies on standardized coding of a child’s behavior during a series of brief separations and reunions with their caregiver. The procedure has demonstrated high inter-rater reliability in identifying distinct attachment styles. Children who show secure attachment use their caregiver as a secure base, exploring the room but checking back frequently. They are visibly upset when the caregiver leaves but easily comforted upon their return.

Ainsworth originally categorized insecure attachment into avoidant and anxious-resistant styles. Later, researchers Mary Main and Judith Solomon identified a fourth category called disorganized or disoriented attachment. Children in this group often display contradictory behaviors, such as approaching the caregiver while looking away, or appearing fearful or frozen in the caregiver's presence. This style is often associated with environments where the caregiver is a source of both fear and comfort.

It is important to discuss what this research tells us about parenting and what it does not. The attachment literature is often simplified in popular media into a rigid script for how to raise children. However, researchers emphasize that attachment is a two-way street. It is not just about the parent’s behavior; it is also about the child’s temperament and the specific cultural context. A child’s temperament, or their innate, biologically based reactivity, plays a significant role in how they interact with their environment. Furthermore, attachment styles are not permanent blueprints. While early experiences provide a foundation, humans are highly adaptable, and later relationships can reshape our attachment security.

We must be careful not to use these findings to blame caregivers for every outcome. The correlation between early attachment and later social functioning is well-documented, but it is not a direct causal arrow. Many factors, including socioeconomic status, peer relationships, and individual personality traits, interact with early attachment to influence long-term development. The research highlights the value of responsive, consistent care, but it does not account for the vast diversity of family structures or the resilience of children. Science shows us that while the early years are an important, sensitive period for developing a sense of security, the story of human development continues to unfold across the entire lifespan.

## Key ideas

- Harry Harlow’s experimental work with rhesus monkeys provided strong evidence that contact comfort is a primary need for infants, contradicting the theory that attachment is driven solely by feeding.
- John Bowlby proposed that attachment is an evolutionary survival strategy that helps infants maintain proximity to caregivers, creating an internal working model for future relationships.
- Mary Ainsworth’s Strange Situation procedure, an observational method with high reliability, identified secure, avoidant, and anxious-resistant attachment styles.
- Researchers Mary Main and Judith Solomon later identified disorganized or disoriented attachment as a fourth category, often associated with environments where the caregiver is a source of both fear and comfort.
- Attachment research highlights the importance of responsive caregiving but should not be used to oversimplify the complex, multifaceted factors that shape human personality and development.
