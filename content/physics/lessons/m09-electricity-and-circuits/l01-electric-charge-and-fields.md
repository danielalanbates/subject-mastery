# Electric Charge and Fields

Have you ever pulled a wool sweater over your head on a dry winter night and heard a faint crackling sound, or felt your hair stand on end? That sudden, startling static cling is not magic. It is the direct manifestation of electric charge, a fundamental property of matter that shapes almost everything around us. In our last journey through oscillations and waves, we explored how energy travels through mechanical disturbances, from rippling strings to the notes of a guitar. Now we step into a different realm of physics, one where invisible, non-contact forces reach across empty space to push and pull particles, long before those particles ever touch.

At the heart of electrical phenomena lies electric charge, which comes in two distinct types that we call positive and negative. Protons carry a positive charge, while electrons carry a negative charge. Like charges repel one another, meaning two positive charges or two negative charges push apart. Opposite charges attract, pulling toward each other with a steady, persistent force. Charge is also strictly conserved, meaning charge cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one object to another. Furthermore, charge is quantized, which means all observable charges exist as integer multiples of a fundamental elementary charge, approximately one point six times ten to the negative nineteen coulombs, the charge carried by a single proton or electron. This behavior governs the structure of atoms, holding electrons in orbit around atomic nuclei and dictating how different materials interact when rubbed together. When you shuffle your feet across a carpet, you transfer electrons from the fabric to your shoes, building up a surplus of charge that tends to discharge through a spark.

To measure the strength of this interaction between stationary charges, we turn to Coulomb's law. Named after the eighteenth century French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb, the law states that the electrostatic force between two point charges acts along the straight line joining them, being attractive for opposite signs and repulsive for like signs. The magnitude of this force is directly proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. This simple point-charge approximation applies exactly to point charges or spherically symmetric charge distributions. In plain terms, if you double the amount of charge on one object, you double the force it exerts. But if you double the distance between two charges, the force drops to one quarter of its previous value because the distance is squared in the denominator.

Let us walk through a quick example to see how this works in numbers. Imagine two small spheres, each holding a charge of one microcoulomb, separated by a distance of one meter in empty space. Because both carry positive charges, they repel each other. To find the push between them, we multiply the two charge values together, multiply by the Coulomb constant of nine times ten to the nine newton meter squared per coulomb squared, and divide by the square of the separation distance. The resulting force is about nine millinewtons, which corresponds roughly to the weight of a standard one gram paperclip. Even though the force is small at that distance, it scales up dramatically when charges grow larger or move much closer together.

When more than two charges are present, the superposition principle states that the net electric force on any single charge is simply the vector sum of the individual forces exerted on it by each of the other charges acting alone. To explain how charges exert these forces across empty space without touching, physicists use the concept of a field. An electric field is a vector field created by any charged object that alters the space surrounding it, telling other charges what force they would experience if placed there. Imagine planting invisible arrows pointing outward in every direction from a positive charge, radiating away into space. If you bring a second positive charge into that neighborhood, it feels a push in the direction of those arrows. The strength of the electric field at any point is defined as the electric force experienced by a tiny positive test charge placed at that location, divided by the magnitude of that test charge. The unit for this field is newtons per coulomb.

Electric field lines help us map and visualize these invisible influences. By convention, field lines start on positive charges and terminate on negative charges, and they never cross one another. Where the lines bunch closely together, the field is strong and the forces are intense. Where the lines spread far apart, the field is weak and the forces taper off. This spatial mapping allows us to calculate complex interactions involving multiple charges by adding their individual fields as vectors, combining directions and magnitudes just as we did with forces in earlier mechanics modules.

Understanding how charges establish these fields around themselves gives us the foundation needed to store electrical energy. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will explore electric potential and voltage, examining how moving a charge against an electric field stores potential energy, much like lifting a heavy object against the pull of gravity.

## Key ideas
- Electric charge is a conserved and quantized fundamental property of matter, existing in positive and negative varieties where like charges repel and opposites attract along the line joining them.
- Coulomb's law dictates that the electrostatic force between two point charges scales with the product of the charges and drops off with the square of the distance between them.
- The superposition principle allows us to find the net force on any charge by calculating the vector sum of forces from all surrounding charges.
- An electric field surrounds every charge, occupying space and exerting forces on other charges without requiring physical contact.
