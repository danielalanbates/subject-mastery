# The Aggregate Demand and Supply Model

Imagine walking through a bustling city marketplace where hundreds of individual buyers and sellers negotiate prices for shoes, coffee, and bicycles. In our earlier study of supply and demand, we looked at how those specific markets work in isolation. But what happens when we zoom out to the entire economy? When we combine every single consumer, business, government agency, and foreign buyer into one massive purchasing force, and every single industry into one massive producing force, we enter the realm of macroeconomics. In our last lesson, we tracked how economies move through the peaks and troughs of the business cycle. Today, we look under the hood of those fluctuations to discover the engine that drives them.

To understand national output and price levels, economists use the aggregate demand and aggregate supply model. Think of aggregate demand as total spending in the entire economy at different overall price levels. This total spending is made up of consumer spending, business investment, government purchases, and net exports combined. Just like the demand for a single cup of coffee, aggregate demand slopes downward, though for different reasons. As the overall price level rises, the real purchasing power of wealth declines, higher interest rates tend to reduce borrowing for purchases and investments, and domestic goods become more expensive relative to foreign goods. Higher overall prices therefore reduce the total purchasing power of consumers, businesses, and governments.

On the other side of the ledger sits aggregate supply, which represents the total quantity of goods and services that all firms in the country are willing and able to produce at different price levels. The shape of the aggregate supply curve has been a subject of intense debate among economists, particularly when we compare the short run to the long run and contrast different economic schools of thought. In the neoclassical view, the long-run aggregate supply curve is vertical because an economy's productive capacity depends strictly on its available physical capital, its level of technology, and its labor force, rather than on the price level. The long-run aggregate supply curve represents the economy's potential or full-employment output, and long-run equilibrium occurs where aggregate demand intersects this vertical long-run supply curve.

In the short run, however, the aggregate supply curve slopes upward in the neoclassical framework because input costs like wages tend to be sticky and slow to adjust. When overall prices rise while wages temporarily lag behind, firms find it profitable to expand production. At the same time, the Keynesian perspective offers a different take on the short-run aggregate supply curve. Keynesian analysis suggests that the aggregate supply curve is relatively flat or horizontal at low levels of output where idle resources are abundant, meaning firms can produce more without raising prices. As the economy approaches its full capacity, bottlenecks appear, and the curve bends upward and eventually becomes steep.

Where these forces intersect, they determine the macroeconomic equilibrium. If we imagine tracking the overall price level on one axis and real gross domestic product representing total national output on the other axis, equilibrium sits at the crossing point of aggregate demand and aggregate supply. If aggregate demand suddenly surges, perhaps because consumer confidence spikes or the government increases purchases, the aggregate demand curve shifts outward. This pushes the economy to a higher price level and a higher level of short-run output, often sparking an economic boom.

Let us trace a simple, concrete example with round numbers. Suppose an economy is currently producing an aggregate output of ten trillion dollars at an overall price level of one hundred. Now imagine a wave of optimism sweeps through the financial markets, causing businesses to invest heavily in new technology. Total spending rises, and the aggregate demand curve shifts outward. In the new short-run equilibrium, real output might climb to ten point five trillion dollars, but because the sudden demand presses against the immediate limits of existing production capacity, the price level ticks up to one hundred and five. The economy experiences growth accompanied by inflation.

Economists continue to debate how quickly wages and prices adjust, leading to different views on how the economy responds when things go wrong. While mainstream economists generally agree on how the aggregate demand and supply framework maps out equilibrium, they divide sharply on whether governments should actively manage aggregate demand during downturns or step back and let market forces restore balance. Exploring how the Keynesian perspective interprets these demand and supply shifts in detail will be our next major step forward.

## Key ideas
- The aggregate demand and aggregate supply model brings together all spending and all production in the economy to determine national output and overall price levels.
- Aggregate demand slopes downward because higher overall price levels reduce the total purchasing power of consumers, businesses, and governments.
- The long-run aggregate supply curve represents the economy's potential or full-employment output, and long-run equilibrium occurs where aggregate demand intersects this long-run curve.
- Macroeconomic equilibrium occurs where the aggregate demand curve intersects the aggregate supply curve, setting the reigning price level and real gross domestic product.
