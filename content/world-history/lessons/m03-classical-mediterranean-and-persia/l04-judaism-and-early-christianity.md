# Judaism and the Rise of Christianity

In a bustling market of first-century Jerusalem, under the watchful gaze of Roman sentries, a merchant drops a heavy silver coin minted with the profile of Emperor Tiberius. That single piece of metal captures a profound historical friction. It marks a small province in the eastern Mediterranean where an ancient, fiercely monotheistic faith collided directly with the sprawling administrative machinery of the Roman Empire. Out of this turbulent intersection emerged a religious movement that would eventually transform the classical world from within.

The story begins centuries earlier in the southern Levant. Jewish and Christian tradition traces Israel’s faith to God’s covenants with Abraham and Moses; most historians think Israel’s religion developed over centuries, from the worship of Yahweh alongside other gods toward a strict, covenantal monotheism. Unlike the neighboring empires of Mesopotamia or Egypt, whose peoples worshipped large pantheons of gods tied to rivers and cities, the Israelites centered their devotion on a single deity, Yahweh. This relationship was understood as a covenant, a binding legal and moral agreement mediated through sacred law. Centuries before the Roman era, the Jewish population encountered the vast cultural reach of Hellenism following the conquests of Alexander the Great. When Seleucid rulers attempted to suppress traditional Jewish religious practices in the second century BCE, a successful rebellion led by the Maccabean family established an independent Hasmonean dynasty. This crisis and its aftermath deeply shaped Second Temple Judaism, cementing a portable textual identity rooted in ethical monotheism. Much of this heritage was preserved and shared through the Septuagint, a Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures produced during the Hellenistic period, which later allowed both Greek-speaking Jews and early Christians to engage these sacred texts across the Mediterranean.

By the first century BCE, Judea had fallen under Roman control, first as an allied kingdom and later as an occupied province administered by a Roman governor. This political reality deepened internal divisions among Jewish communities. Some groups, like the Sadducees, collaborated with the Roman authorities to preserve the temple cult, while others, like the Pharisees, emphasized meticulous daily adherence to oral and written law. Meanwhile, apocalyptic expectations surged. Many looked anxiously for a divine deliverer who would overthrow foreign oppression and restore sovereign independence to Israel.

It was within this charged atmosphere that Jesus of Galilee began his itinerant ministry. Preaching an urgent message of spiritual repentance, the imminent arrival of the kingdom of God, and radical love for neighbors and enemies alike, he gathered a dedicated following. His execution by Roman crucifixion, the standard imperial punishment for political insurrection, should have ended the movement just like dozens of other messianic uprisings. Instead, his followers experienced what they proclaimed as his resurrection from the dead. His followers proclaimed him the Messiah, in Greek the Christ, soon worshipped him as divine, and formed a vibrant new religious community.

Initially operating as a reform movement within Judaism, the early church quickly expanded under the tireless missionary work of figures like Paul of Tarsus. At a meeting in Jerusalem around 49 CE, described in the Book of Acts, early Christian leaders affirmed that Gentile believers did not need to observe the full Mosaic Law, such as circumcision, to enter the covenant. This theological pivot freed the movement to cross ethnic and linguistic boundaries. Traveling along the remarkably safe roads and shipping lanes of the Roman world, Christian missionaries preached in urban centers from Antioch to Corinth and eventually to Rome itself.

The spread of Christianity across the Roman Empire faced profound obstacles. Because Christians often refused to participate in civic rituals honoring the traditional Roman gods and the divine emperor, they were viewed with suspicion as antisocial and politically disloyal. This tension periodically erupted into violent persecutions, notably under Emperor Nero after 64 CE and later under Emperor Diocletian at the dawn of the fourth century. These trials tested the faithful and fostered a powerful cult of martyrdom. Yet, the faith continued to grow, drawing converts from all social strata through its emphasis on mutual charity, spiritual equality before God, and the promise of eternal life.

A monumental turning point arrived in 70 CE, when Roman legions besieged Jerusalem and destroyed the Second Temple in the wake of a massive Jewish revolt. The loss of the central sanctuary forced a profound reinvention of Jewish religious life, accelerating the shift toward rabbinic Judaism and local synagogue worship, while also compelling early Christians to articulate their identity apart from the ruined temple.

By the fourth century CE, the numerical and cultural weight of Christianity had become too significant for the imperial state to suppress. Following the conversion of Emperor Constantine and the Edict of Milan in 313 CE, the Roman government shifted from persecution to active patronage. Christianity was legalized, favored, and woven into the very fabric of imperial governance, though it did not become the official religion of the empire until the Edict of Thessalonica, issued by Emperor Theodosius in 380 CE. What had begun as a localized covenant in the hills of Judea now stood as a favored pillar of a global empire, providing the institutional glue for a radically new European and Mediterranean order.

## Key ideas
- Ancient Israelite religion developed into a strict ethical monotheism centered on a covenant and sacred texts, weathering foreign conquests, Hellenistic rule, and the Maccabean revolt.
- The translation of the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, known as the Septuagint, helped spread Jewish ideas throughout the Hellenistic world and later provided early Christians with their primary scriptural text.
- Under Roman occupation, Judea experienced acute political tension and apocalyptic expectation, culminating in the ministry, crucifixion, and reported resurrection of Jesus of Galilee.
- The destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE reshaped Jewish practice toward rabbinic traditions and synagogue worship, while early Christian leaders like Paul formalized the mission to Gentiles.
- Following periods of periodic persecution under emperors like Nero and Diocletian, Christianity gained imperial favor through Constantine in the fourth century and was established as the official religion under Theodosius.
