# Ellipses and Hyperbolas

Imagine swinging a weighted cord around two fixed pins on a wooden board. As the pencil tip moves while keeping the string taut, it traces a graceful, closed curve that looks like a stretched circle. In our last lesson, we explored circles and parabolas, seeing how equations in the coordinate plane translate into geometric shapes formed by equidistant points. Today, we turn to the ellipse and the hyperbola, two curves that arise not from a single center point, but from the combined distance to two distinct points called foci. These shapes govern the orbits of planets across the night sky and guide the precision instruments of modern navigation.

An ellipse is the set of all points in a plane where the sum of the distances from two fixed foci is a constant value, specifically equal to two times a, the length of the major axis. Picture placing two thumbtacks into a board, looping a piece of string around them, and pulling a pencil tight against the loop. As you move the pencil around the tacks, the total distance from the pencil tip to the first tack, plus the distance from the pencil tip to the second tack, always equals the fixed length of the string. In a horizontal ellipse centered at the origin, the standard equation is given by the square of x divided by the square of a number we can call m, plus the square of y divided by the square of a number we can call n, all equaling one. If the larger denominator is under y squared instead, the ellipse is vertically oriented, meaning its major axis runs up and down. The numbers m and n represent the lengths of the semi-axes, stretching the curve horizontally and vertically.

Consider a specific example of an ellipse centered at the origin. Suppose the square of x is divided by twenty-five, and the square of y is divided by nine, with the sum equaling one. Here, the number m is five, meaning the graph extends five units to the right and left of the origin, and the number n is three, meaning the graph extends three units up and down. To find the foci, we use the general formula for an ellipse where the square of the focal distance, which we can call c squared, equals the square of m minus the square of n. Subtracting nine from twenty-five gives sixteen, so the distance from the origin to each focus is the square root of sixteen, which is four. Thus, the foci sit at four comma zero and negative four comma zero on the horizontal axis.

A hyperbola shifts our perspective from a sum of distances to a difference of distances. It is defined as the set of all points where the absolute difference between the distances to two fixed foci remains constant, specifically equal to two times a, the length of the transverse axis. This curve consists of two separate, mirroring branches that open outward away from each other, curving around two diagonal dashed lines called asymptotes that guide the outer edges of the graph. For a horizontal hyperbola centered at the origin, the standard equation features the square of x divided by the square of m minus the square of y divided by the square of n, equaling one. Notice the minus sign, which distinguishes the hyperbola from the ellipse, and note that for a hyperbola with a positive y squared term, the transverse axis is vertical.

Let us trace a hyperbola where the square of x is divided by sixteen, and the square of y is divided by nine, equaling one. The number m is four, marking the vertices where the two branches turn inward toward the center, located at four comma zero and negative four comma zero. The asymptotes are straight lines passing through the origin with slopes equal to plus or minus three over four. As x grows very large in either the positive or negative direction, the points on the hyperbola get closer and closer to these diagonal asymptote lines without ever touching them. For hyperbolas, the foci lie farther out than the vertices, with the square of the focal distance, c squared, equaling the square of m plus the square of n. Adding sixteen and nine gives twenty-five, making the focal distance five, placing the foci at five comma zero and negative five comma zero.

These geometric forms appear naturally across the physical universe. Johannes Kepler discovered that planets travel in elliptical paths with the sun sitting at one focus, an insight that replaced ancient circular models of the heavens. In navigation systems, the time difference between radio signals arriving from two synchronized transmitter stations places a receiver on a hyperbola, and combining readings from multiple stations pinpoints a precise geographical location. As we move forward, we will see how these conic curves interact with other relations when we solve nonlinear systems and systems of inequalities.

## Key ideas
- An ellipse is defined as the set of all points where the sum of the distances to two fixed foci remains constant and equals two times a, while a hyperbola has a constant absolute difference of distances equal to two times a.
- The general relationship for the focal distance c involves subtraction for an ellipse where c squared equals a squared minus b squared, and addition for a hyperbola where c squared equals a squared plus b squared.
- Orientation for both curves depends on the relative sizes of the denominators or the placement of positive terms, placing the major or transverse axis vertically when the larger denominator or positive term is associated with y.
- Elliptic and hyperbolic geometry describe celestial orbits in astronomy and hyperbolic positioning in navigation systems.
