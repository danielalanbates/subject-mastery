# The Resurrection and History

Imagine standing before a courtroom judge. You have witnesses, physical evidence, and a compelling narrative, but the opposing counsel argues that your central piece of evidence—the return of a man from the dead—is physically impossible. How do you argue for a historical event that defies the normal patterns of human experience? Today, we turn to the cornerstone of the Christian faith: the resurrection of Jesus. Apologetics in this area is not about proving a miracle through a laboratory experiment, but about evaluating historical data to see if the resurrection provides the best explanation for the known facts.

Apologists such as Gary Habermas use a minimal-facts approach, which builds only on facts that most historians, including skeptical ones, accept. These facts include the death of Jesus by crucifixion, the subsequent conviction of his followers that they had seen him alive, and the rapid emergence of the early church.

One of the most important pieces of data is the creedal summary in the first letter to the Corinthians, chapter fifteen, verses three through eight. In the King James Version it begins: “For I delivered unto you first of all that which I also received, how that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures; And that he was buried, and that he rose again the third day according to the scriptures.” Many scholars, including skeptical ones, date the tradition Paul received to within a few years of Jesus’ death. This indicates that the belief in the resurrection was not a legend that developed over centuries, but a core conviction held by the earliest community of believers.

The empty tomb is another central point of discussion. The Gospel accounts emphasize that the tomb was found empty by women. This is historically significant because, in the cultural context of the first century, the testimony of women was often undervalued in legal settings. If the early Christians were inventing a story, they likely would have chosen more prominent male witnesses. Furthermore, Matthew's Gospel, and later Justin Martyr in the second century, report that Jewish opponents claimed the disciples stole the body. If so, even opponents assumed the tomb was empty. Apologists call this enemy attestation, though skeptics note that the report reaches us only through Christian writers, and scholars are more divided on the empty tomb than on the appearances.

Critics often search for alternative explanations. Some suggest the swoon theory, which posits that Jesus did not actually die but merely fainted and was later revived by the cool air of the tomb. However, most historians reject this as medically implausible given the severity of Roman scourging and crucifixion. Another common response is the hallucination theory, which suggests the disciples experienced grief-induced visions. This theory struggles to account for the physical evidence of the empty tomb or the fact that the appearances occurred to diverse groups of people in different settings.

Apologists point to the radical transformation of the disciples to argue for the reality of these experiences. Before the resurrection, the disciples were hiding in fear; after these events, they became bold witnesses. While historical evidence for the specific manner of death for each of the twelve apostles is varied and often based on later tradition, it is historically clear that the early leaders of the church were willing to undergo severe hardship and social ostracization for their testimony. It is difficult to explain why a group of people would suffer for a claim they knew to be a deliberate lie.

Skeptics often raise the principle of analogy, associated with the German theologian Ernst Troeltsch, which suggests that because we do not observe people rising from the dead today, we cannot accept it as having happened in the past. Apologists reply that this principle can quietly assume naturalism, the view that the physical world is all there is. They add that if a Creator exists, then miracles are not logically impossible. If God is the author of nature, then God is not bound by the patterns of nature. Therefore, the resurrection is not a violation of the laws of nature, but an act of the lawgiver intervening within the history he created.

Ultimately, historians deal in probabilities. They ask which hypothesis best explains the combination of the empty tomb, the early creedal evidence, and the sudden, explosive growth of the early church. As we weigh these claims, we see that the resurrection is not merely a theological doctrine to be accepted by faith alone; it is an event presented within the framework of history. For Christians, it serves as the primary validation of Jesus’ teachings and the foundation upon which Christian life is built.

## Key ideas
- The minimal-facts approach used by apologists evaluates the resurrection by focusing on historical data accepted by the vast majority of historians.
- The creed in first Corinthians fifteen is a vital historical datum because many scholars date it to within a few years of Jesus’ death.
- The criterion of enemy attestation, based on reported Jewish claims that the body was stolen, is used to support the empty tomb, though scholars remain divided on it.
- The radical transformation of the disciples and their willingness to suffer for their testimony provides strong circumstantial evidence for the resurrection.
