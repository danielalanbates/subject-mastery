# Exponential Growth and Decay

Imagine you have a single bacterium sitting in a glass dish, and every single hour, that tiny organism splits cleanly into two. After one hour passes, you have two bacteria swimming about. After two hours, those two split to become four. After three hours, you have eight, and soon the population is sixteen, thirty-two, and sixty-four. In our last module, we looked at rational functions that flattened out gently or blew up suddenly at vertical walls. Today, we step into a completely different kind of change, one where the variable lives in the exponent rather than the base. This is the fascinating world of exponential functions, where quantities do not merely add a fixed amount over time, but instead multiply by a constant factor with every tick of the clock.

The parent exponential function is written as f of x equals b to the power of x, where the base b is a positive constant other than one, and the exponent x is our variable representing time or another changing dimension. To capture starting amounts other than one, we use the general form of an exponential function, which is f of x equals p times b to the power of x, where the letter p represents the initial value at the starting point where x equals zero.

When the base is strictly greater than one, we encounter exponential growth. Think back to our bacterial dish. If you start with five bacteria and the population triples every single hour, the amount after x hours is five times three to the power of x. Let us test this with actual arithmetic. After one hour, three to the first power is three, and five times three gives fifteen bacteria. After two hours, three squared is nine, and five times nine gives forty-five. After three hours, three cubed is twenty-seven, and five times twenty-seven yields one hundred thirty-five.

Notice how aggressive this multiplication is when compared to linear growth, which just adds the exact same amount at every single step. Linear growth climbs like a steady staircase, stepping up by five or ten units each year regardless of how large the building gets. Exponential growth climbs like a rocket taking off into the night sky. At first, the curve creeps along so slowly that an unsuspecting observer might think it is completely flat. But once the exponent grows large enough, the numbers explode upward, quickly leaving any linear function far behind in the dust. No matter how steep a linear slope you choose for your comparison, an exponential function with a growth factor greater than one will eventually outrun it entirely.

Growth factors and percent rates are two sides of the same mathematical coin. If a growing town expands by eight percent each year, its population is not merely adding eight percent of the original starting number; it is multiplying by one plus zero point zero eight, which gives a multiplier of one point zero eight. That multiplier is our growth factor. Conversely, if a radioactive substance decays by losing twenty percent of its mass every hour, what remains behind is eighty percent of what was there before. The decay factor is zero point eight, and our function takes the form of a starting amount multiplied by zero point eight to the power of x.

Let us picture the behavior of these exponential graphs in your mind. Every general exponential function possesses a smooth, sweeping curve that never breaks or jumps. It always crosses the vertical axis at a height exactly equal to the starting coefficient p, because any nonzero base raised to the power of zero equals one. For exponential growth, where the base is greater than one, the graph flattens out to the left, drawing closer and closer to the horizontal axis without ever touching it. For exponential decay, where the base is a fraction between zero and one, the graph flattens out to the right instead. That invisible floor is known as a horizontal asymptote. It tells us that the value can shrink toward nothingness, but in pure mathematics, it never quite reaches absolute zero.

## Key ideas
- Exponential functions feature a variable in the exponent, causing repeated multiplication by a constant factor rather than steady addition.
- The general exponential function is written as f of x equals p times b to the power of x, where p is the initial value and b is the base.
- Exponential growth outruns any linear growth over time because its curve accelerates upward at an increasing rate.
- The growth factor equals one plus the decimal growth rate, while the decay factor equals one minus the decimal decay rate.
- The graph of an exponential function features a smooth curve, a vertical intercept at the initial value, and a horizontal asymptote approached as x moves toward negative infinity for growth or positive infinity for decay.
