# The Derivative as a Limit

Imagine you are driving a car along a winding country road. If you look at your dashboard odometer at the start of a trip and again an hour later, you can easily calculate your average speed for that entire hour. But your speedometer tells a completely different story. It reveals how fast you are moving at one exact microsecond in time. How can math capture that instantaneous speed when a single frozen moment has no duration at all, and therefore covers zero distance in zero time? This riddle is the beating heart of differential calculus.

To solve this riddle, the mathematical world needed a new language. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently forged this breakthrough in the seventeenth century, though their invention sparked a bitter priority dispute that lasted for decades. Newton introduced dot notation, placing a small dot above a variable to indicate its rate of change. Leibniz introduced the notation of d y over d x, treating the derivative as a ratio of tiny differentials. Today, we use elements from both traditions to study how quantities change.

Think back to finding the slope of a straight line. You pick two points on the line, take the change in your vertical coordinates, and divide by the change in your horizontal coordinates. But a curved graph does not have a constant slope. Its steepness changes at every single step. To find the slope of a curve at a single point, we can start by approximating it with a secant line. A secant line connects our target point on the curve to a second point a little ways down the road. The slope of that secant line is just an average rate of change over an interval. It is the change in the function values divided by the change in the independent variable. Naturally, this average rate is only a rough guess for the instantaneous rate at our first point, because the curve bends away from the straight secant line.

Here is the brilliant leap of calculus. What happens if we slide that second point closer and closer along the curve toward our original point? As the distance between the two points shrinks toward zero, the secant line pivots. It morphs into a tangent line that just touches the curve at our point, running in the same direction as the curve there. The slope of this tangent line is the instantaneous rate of change. For the derivative to exist at a point, this limit must exist from both the left and the right, meaning the secant slopes must approach the same value whether we slide toward our target from below or from above.

Because we cannot let the distance between our two points shrink to an absolute zero without dividing by zero, we use a limit. Formally, the derivative is defined as the limit as h approaches zero of f of the quantity x plus h, minus f of x, with that whole difference divided by h. We denote this resulting derivative function as f prime of x. When we want the derivative at a specific point, we simply evaluate this derivative function at that number.

Let us watch this process unfold with a concrete example. Suppose we have the function where f of x equals x squared, and we want to find the exact slope of the curve when x is equal to the number three. We pick a nearby point where x is equal to three plus h, where h represents a tiny horizontal nudge. The function value at our starting point is three squared, which is nine. The function value at our second point is the quantity three plus h, all squared. Expanding that binomial gives us nine plus six h plus h squared.

Now, we set up our average rate of change, which is the difference quotient. We subtract the starting function value, nine, from our new function value, leaving six h plus h squared, and we divide that entire numerator by the horizontal width, which is h. Because h is approaching zero but is not yet zero, we can factor an h out of the numerator. We cancel that h with the h in the denominator, leaving the simplified expression six plus h.

Finally, we take the limit as h approaches zero. As h vanishes, the expression six plus h simply becomes the number six. The instantaneous rate of change of x squared when x is three is exactly six, which means f prime of three is equal to six. This number is the slope of the tangent line to the curve at that point. By letting the interval shrink infinitely through a limit, we conquered the impossible problem of motion and change.

## Key ideas
- The derivative measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function, corresponding geometrically to the slope of the tangent line at a single point.
- The derivative is formally defined as the limit as h approaches zero of f of the quantity x plus h, minus f of x, all divided by h, and this limit must exist from both the left and the right.
- Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently developed calculus, introducing dot notation and d over d x notation respectively, which sparked a bitter priority dispute.
- The resulting derivative function is denoted as f prime of x, and the derivative at a specific point is simply the value of that derivative function evaluated at the point.
