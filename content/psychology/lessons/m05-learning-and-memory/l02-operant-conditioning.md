# Operant Conditioning

Think about the last time you trained a pet or encouraged a friend to try a new hobby. You likely offered a treat or a word of praise when they did what you wanted. This simple intuition—that behaviors followed by favorable outcomes are likely to be repeated—is the foundation of operant conditioning. While our previous lesson focused on classical conditioning, where an organism learns to associate two involuntary stimuli, operant conditioning focuses on how we learn to associate our own voluntary actions with the consequences that follow them.

This process is governed by the three-term contingency. This model consists of the antecedent, which is the environment or context in which a behavior occurs, the behavior itself, and the consequence that follows. By analyzing these three parts, researchers can predict and modify behavior with high reliability across many species.

The roots of this field lie in the work of Edward Thorndike. At the turn of the twentieth century, Thorndike studied how cats escaped from puzzle boxes. He observed that when a cat pressed a lever to open a door and receive food, it became faster at hitting that lever on subsequent trials. He called this the law of effect. This principle is supported by decades of experimental evidence showing that consequences are powerful drivers of behavior. It states that behaviors followed by satisfying consequences are strengthened, while behaviors followed by unpleasant consequences are weakened.

B. F. Skinner later expanded on this idea. He argued that psychology should focus on observable behavior rather than internal mental states. He developed the operant chamber, often called a Skinner box. This allowed him to precisely control the environment and measure how subjects learned to press a bar or peck a disk to receive a reward.

In Skinner’s framework, consequences are categorized by whether they increase or decrease the likelihood of a behavior. Reinforcement is any consequence that increases the frequency of a behavior. Positive reinforcement happens when you add something desirable, like giving a student a gold star for a completed assignment. Negative reinforcement occurs when you remove something unpleasant to encourage a behavior. For example, if you take an aspirin to stop a headache, the removal of the pain reinforces the act of taking medicine.

Punishment is designed to decrease a behavior. Positive punishment involves adding an unpleasant stimulus, such as receiving a ticket for speeding. Negative punishment involves taking away something desirable, such as losing phone privileges. Research consistently indicates that reinforcement is generally more effective at shaping long-term behavior than punishment. Punishment can be useful in specific contexts, but it often leads to resentment or avoidance rather than the desired change.

To achieve complex behaviors, we use a process called shaping. This involves reinforcing successive approximations of a desired behavior. If you are teaching a dog to roll over, you might first reward them for lying down, then for leaning to the side, and finally for the full rotation.

The timing of these consequences, known as schedules of reinforcement, is crucial. Continuous reinforcement rewards a behavior every time it occurs, which is ideal for teaching a new skill. Once a behavior is learned, intermittent reinforcement makes the action resistant to extinction. There are four main schedules. A fixed-ratio schedule provides a reward after a set number of responses, leading to high response rates. A variable-ratio schedule provides rewards after an unpredictable number of responses, which creates very high and steady rates of behavior that are difficult to extinguish. A fixed-interval schedule rewards the first response after a specific amount of time has passed. Finally, a variable-interval schedule rewards the first response after an unpredictable amount of time. Variable schedules generally produce more consistent behavior than fixed schedules.

Operant conditioning influences our daily lives, from how we work to how we interact with social media notifications. By understanding these principles, we can better appreciate how our environment shapes our choices. While these behavioral foundations are powerful, they are only part of the story. Our ability to learn extends beyond direct experience, as we can also learn by watching others. We will explore those processes in our next lesson.

## Key ideas

- The three-term contingency consists of the antecedent, the behavior, and the consequence, which together form the basis of operant conditioning.
- The law of effect, supported by extensive experimental evidence, states that behaviors followed by satisfying consequences are strengthened.
- Reinforcement increases the frequency of a behavior, while punishment is designed to decrease it, though reinforcement is generally more effective for long-term change.
- Shaping uses the reinforcement of successive approximations to teach complex behaviors that an organism would not typically perform on its own.
- Reinforcement schedules, including fixed and variable ratios or intervals, determine how quickly a behavior is learned and how resistant it is to extinction.
