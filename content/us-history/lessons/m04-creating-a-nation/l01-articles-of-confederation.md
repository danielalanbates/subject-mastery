# The Articles of Confederation

Imagine you have just finished a long, exhausting fight to evict a landlord who tried to rule your house with an iron fist. When you finally win your freedom, the last thing you want is a new master. You want to be the sovereign of your own domain. This was the mindset of the American revolutionaries. Having spent years protesting what they saw as British tyranny, they were deeply suspicious of centralized power. When they sat down to build a national government in the late 1770s, they designed a system that was intentionally weak. They wanted a loose alliance of sovereign states, not a consolidated empire.

The result of this thinking was the Articles of Confederation. Ratified in 1781, this document created a firm league of friendship rather than a true national government. It established a unicameral Congress in which each state held exactly one vote, regardless of its population. This meant that small states like Delaware had the same influence as large states like Virginia. Passing most laws required the approval of nine of the thirteen states, and amending the Articles required the unanimous consent of all thirteen. This high threshold made meaningful change nearly impossible.

Under the Articles, there was no executive branch to enforce laws and no national court system to settle disputes between states. Most importantly, the central government lacked the power to tax. If the national government needed money to pay off war debts or fund an army, it had to politely ask the states for contributions. The states often refused. Furthermore, the national government lacked the power to regulate interstate and foreign commerce. This allowed states to impose tariffs on one another, which hindered trade and turned neighbors into economic rivals. The government also struggled to enforce treaties. After the Treaty of Paris in 1783, Britain continued to hold forts in the Northwest Territory, violating the peace agreement, yet the American government had no leverage to force their departure.

Despite these structural flaws, the Articles did manage a few significant successes. The most enduring achievement was the management of the western territories. As states surrendered their conflicting land claims in the region north of the Ohio River, Congress passed the Northwest Ordinance of 1787. This landmark law provided a blueprint for how new territories could eventually become states on equal footing with the original thirteen. It also prohibited slavery in that specific region, setting a precedent that would influence the future of the nation’s expansion. However, this ordinance also signaled the beginning of aggressive federal expansion into lands inhabited by Native nations. It ignored the sovereignty of Indigenous peoples, setting the stage for decades of conflict as the United States pushed westward.

By the mid-1780s, the government’s inability to address economic distress exposed its fatal weaknesses. Following the war, the new nation faced a severe economic downturn. Farmers, particularly in western Massachusetts, were hit especially hard. They were saddled with heavy debt, high taxes, and a lack of paper currency. When courts began seizing their land to satisfy creditors, the farmers felt the same sense of betrayal they had once felt toward the British Crown. In 1786, a Revolutionary War veteran named Daniel Shays led an armed uprising. Shays's Rebellion saw protesters shut down courthouses to prevent the seizure of property and the imprisonment of debtors.

The rebellion sent a shockwave through the country’s political elite. They realized that the national government was far too weak to maintain order. When the Massachusetts governor asked for federal assistance to put down the uprising, the national government had neither the money to raise an army nor the authority to intervene effectively. Eventually, a private militia funded by wealthy merchants had to restore order. The message was clear: if the new republic could not keep the peace, it was in danger of collapsing.

Historians often debate the motivations of the men who sought to replace the Articles. Some scholars emphasize that the framers were motivated by a genuine desire to create a stable, democratic republic that could protect the liberties won in the Revolution. Others argue that these men were primarily concerned with protecting the interests of the wealthy, fearing that the excess of democracy at the state level would lead to the redistribution of property. Regardless of their specific motivations, there was a growing consensus that the Articles were insufficient. By 1787, leaders gathered in Philadelphia, intending to fix the Articles, but ultimately deciding to scrap them entirely to build a more energetic government.

## Key ideas
- The Articles of Confederation established a weak, unicameral Congress where each state had one vote and major changes required unanimous consent.
- The national government lacked the power to tax or regulate commerce, which crippled the economy and prevented the enforcement of international treaties.
- The Northwest Ordinance of 1787 created a process for statehood and banned slavery in the Northwest Territory, while simultaneously disregarding the sovereignty of Native nations.
- Shays's Rebellion exposed the inability of the central government to maintain order, convincing many leaders that a stronger national structure was essential for the republic's survival.
