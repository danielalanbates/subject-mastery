# International Financial Crises

Imagine standing in a bustling bank lobby where the line of people waiting to withdraw their life savings snakes out the door and down the street. Now imagine that panic multiplying across an entire continent, jumping instantly through digital networks from one national stock exchange to another. In our last lesson, we looked at how globalization links economies together through trade and culture. Today, we turn to the darker side of those deep connections by exploring how currency fluctuations and debt defaults trigger international financial crises. These events show how quickly confidence can evaporate in the global marketplace.

At the heart of many international financial crises lies the foreign exchange market and the problem of foreign debt. When businesses or governments borrow money, they often do so in a stable foreign currency like the United States dollar or the euro, often because foreign lenders will not lend in the local currency, or only at much higher interest rates. But suppose a developing nation experiences economic trouble, causing its domestic currency to depreciate sharply. Suddenly, local companies and the government itself need vastly more of their own local money to buy the dollars required to pay back their debts. This mismatch between local earnings and foreign obligations can push solvent institutions into sudden insolvency.

To make this concrete, imagine a fictional country called Veridia, whose central bank promises to keep its currency pegged at a fixed rate of two Veridian units to one United States dollar. To maintain this peg, the central bank must hold large reserves of foreign currency to buy up its own money whenever people try to sell it. If foreign investors lose confidence because of rising inflation or political instability, they rush to pull their money out of Veridia. They sell Veridian currency and demand dollars. The central bank's foreign reserves rapidly dwindle. Eventually, the central bank runs out of dollars, the fixed exchange rate collapses, and the currency plummets in value.

This sudden loss of confidence often triggers what economists call capital flight, an abrupt and massive outflow of financial investments from a country. When foreign investors panic, they do not just stop buying new assets; they desperately liquidate existing ones, selling local stocks, bonds, and real estate all at once. This massive sell-off depresses domestic asset prices and squeezes domestic banks, which suddenly find themselves starved of liquidity. A domestic credit crunch follows, halting construction, manufacturing, and trade. The crisis then spills across borders as investors, nursing heavy losses in Veridia, panic and pull their money out of neighboring countries with similar economic profiles, a phenomenon known as contagion. Real episodes follow this pattern: Mexico in 1994; Thailand, Indonesia and South Korea in 1997, after Thailand's dollar peg broke that July and panic spread across East Asia; and Argentina in 2001. The International Monetary Fund lent heavily during the Asian crisis, and its conditions of budget cuts and high interest rates drew sharp criticism, including from the economist Joseph Stiglitz.

When these international financial emergencies strike, economic schools of thought offer sharply contrasting diagnoses and policy prescriptions. Some free-market economists stress moral hazard, arguing that bailouts encourage reckless lending and that insolvent institutions should be allowed to fail. Monetarists focus closely on the money supply, warning that central banks must prevent sudden collapses in the circulating money while avoiding excessive currency printing that fuels inflation. Keynesian economists argue that during a severe financial panic, private demand collapses, so governments and international bodies must step in with emergency lending and fiscal stimulus to support aggregate demand and prevent a deep depression. Austrian economists trace the root cause back to prior artificial credit expansion by central banks, arguing that government intervention or international bailouts only delay necessary market liquidation and sow the seeds of future crises. Behavioral economists focus on psychological factors like herding behavior and irrational panic, explaining how market sentiment can detach from underlying economic fundamentals. There is also sharp disagreement over whether governments should impose capital controls, which are legal restrictions on the movement of money out of the country, to stem the bleeding during a panic.

Understanding how these financial shockwaves propagate is essential for grasping the fragile architecture of the modern global economy. Currency collapses and sovereign debt defaults are not isolated accidents of history; they are recurring symptoms of how interconnected financial markets react to shifting expectations and debt burdens. As we move from our exploration of international trade and finance into our next major module, we will step back to examine the major schools of economic thought that attempt to make sense of these sweeping macroeconomic dramas.

## Key ideas
- International financial crises occur when sudden currency depreciations and massive debt defaults destroy investor confidence across borders.
- Borrowing heavily in foreign currencies creates severe vulnerability if a nation's domestic currency loses value.
- Capital flight happens when panicked investors rapidly pull their money out of a country, causing domestic credit crunches and asset price crashes.
- Contagion spreads financial panics from one troubled economy to neighboring nations with similar economic structures.
- Free-market, monetarist, Keynesian, Austrian, and behavioral schools offer sharply different explanations and policy responses for international financial panics.
