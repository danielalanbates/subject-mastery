# Wave Properties and Behavior

Imagine standing on a wooden dock, watching ripples spread across a still lake after a small pebble drops into the water. The water molecules themselves do not travel all the way across the lake, yet a distinct disturbance moves steadily outward from the point of impact. In our previous look at simple harmonic motion, we saw how a single physical object can oscillate back and forth around an equilibrium position. Now, we expand that foundational idea outward into space and time. When a disturbance travels through a continuous medium, carrying energy forward without permanently displacing the matter of that medium, we have created a mechanical wave. Understanding how these traveling pulses behave requires us to look closely at their core geometry, their measurements, and their motion.

Waves generally arrive in two primary mechanical flavors, which are classified by how the individual particles of the medium move relative to the direction of the energy transfer. A transverse wave makes the particles vibrate perpendicularly to the path of the wave. Think of a large stadium crowd performing a traditional wave, where individual people stand up and sit down vertically while the visible wave travels horizontally around the arena bowl. Light waves and ripples traveling along a plucked guitar string behave this exact way. In contrast, a longitudinal wave forces the particles of the medium to oscillate back and forth parallel to the direction of travel. Sound traveling through the air is a classic everyday example of this behavior, where air molecules bunch together and then spread apart in a line matching the sound's journey.

To measure and describe these traveling disturbances, physicists rely on several dependable geometric properties. The highest point of a transverse wave is called its crest, and the lowest point is known as its trough. For longitudinal waves, the squeezed-together regions are called compressions, and the stretched-out regions are called rarefactions. The maximum displacement of any single particle from its resting equilibrium position is the amplitude, which directly reflects the amount of energy the wave carries. A larger amplitude always means a more vigorous, energetic disturbance. Meanwhile, the distance from one crest to the next identical point on a wave is the wavelength, typically measured in meters.

Another vital feature is frequency, which counts how many complete wave cycles pass a fixed point each second, measured in units of hertz. If you stand on a dock and count three wave crests passing a piling every single second, the frequency of those waves is three hertz. The time it takes for just one complete wave cycle to pass a given point is the period, measured in seconds. Frequency and period are strict mathematical inverses of each other; as one goes up, the other goes down. The speed of the wave tells us how fast the energy actually travels through space, measured in meters per second. Importantly, wave speed depends entirely on the mechanical properties of the medium, such as its stiffness and density, rather than on the wave's amplitude or its frequency.

A universal mathematical relationship ties all these core concepts together into a single equation. The speed of a wave always equals the product of its wavelength multiplied by its frequency. If a wave has a wavelength of two meters and a frequency of four hertz, the wave travels at eight meters per second. Let us trace another quick example to make this concrete. Suppose an ocean wave has a crest-to-crest distance of ten meters, and you observe one crest striking the rocky shore every two and a half seconds. That is a frequency of zero point four hertz. Multiplying the wavelength of ten meters by the frequency of zero point four hertz gives a wave speed of four meters per second. If the medium changes, perhaps transitioning from deep water to shallow water, the wavelength and speed may shift, but the frequency, which is strictly dictated by the original source of the vibration, remains constant.

As these traveling pulses encounter physical boundaries, obstacles, or other waves, they do not simply stop or vanish. They reflect off hard surfaces, bend around sharp corners, and pass directly through one another without losing their identity. When two waves meet in the same space at the same time, they temporarily combine their displacements before continuing on their separate journeys.

## Key ideas
- Mechanical waves transfer energy through a medium without permanently transporting the bulk matter of the medium itself.
- Transverse waves feature particle motion perpendicular to the wave direction, while longitudinal waves feature parallel oscillations.
- Amplitude measures the maximum displacement of a wave, and the energy carried by the disturbance grows with the square of the amplitude.
- Wave speed equals the product of wavelength and frequency, and depends strictly on the physical properties of the medium.
