# Christianity in Africa

Imagine a map of the world’s religious landscape in the year 1900. If you looked at Africa, you would see a continent where the Christian faith was largely confined to small coastal enclaves and historical outposts, such as the ancient Coptic Church in Egypt or the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. Yet, if you look at that same map today, you see a transformation that historians often call one of the most significant religious shifts in human history. In just over a century, the number of Christians in sub-Saharan Africa has grown from a few million to an estimated six hundred million or more. This is not merely a story of Western missionary expansion; it is a story of African agency, innovation, and a profound re-centering of the global church.

While our last lesson explored how the Lausanne Movement helped unify global evangelicals, the story in Africa often moved beyond those established institutional boundaries. Early in the twentieth century, many African Christians began to feel that foreign-led missions did not fully address their cultural context or spiritual needs. This dissatisfaction gave rise to what we call African-initiated churches. These movements were founded and led by Africans for Africans. They often combined traditional Christian liturgy with indigenous music, dance, and a deep focus on healing and prophecy. Figures like William Wade Harris in West Africa or Simon Kimbangu in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo drew thousands to the faith by preaching a message that felt both authentically Christian and deeply rooted in the African experience.

These independent churches represented a major shift in power. They asserted that the Holy Spirit was not the property of European or North American denominations. By emphasizing the power of prayer and the immediacy of the Spirit, these movements anticipated the global surge of Pentecostalism that would later sweep across the entire continent. Today, Africa is home to some of the largest congregations in the world, many of which are characterized by vibrant worship and a strong social witness. These churches have become central to the lives of millions, providing education, healthcare, and a sense of identity in rapidly changing urban centers like Lagos, Nairobi, and Kinshasa.

However, the history of Christianity in Africa is also a history of deep struggle, particularly regarding the role of the church in the face of systemic injustice. In South Africa, the church was tragically divided by the policy of apartheid, a system of institutionalized racial segregation. Some church leaders and denominations used theological justifications to support the status quo, claiming that the separation of races was divinely ordained. Conversely, many other Christians, including figures like Archbishop Desmond Tutu and the Reverend Beyers Naudé, stood in firm opposition. They argued that the gospel demanded the dignity of all human beings regardless of race. The church in South Africa became a vital battleground where the meaning of the cross was tested against the realities of oppression and reconciliation.

This era of struggle helped forge a new kind of African theological voice. African theologians began to write works that interrogated how the faith could speak to post-colonial realities, poverty, and political corruption. They moved away from imitating Western models to creating a theology that addressed the specific wounds and hopes of their own nations. This intellectual maturation meant that African leaders were no longer just recipients of theology; they were becoming its primary architects.

Today, this influence is felt in every corner of the global church. Whether in the halls of the Vatican, the synods of the Anglican Communion, or the gatherings of world Pentecostals, African bishops, scholars, and pastors occupy positions of profound influence. They are often the ones challenging the global North to return to the basics of biblical hospitality and communal living. They have turned the narrative of the church on its head, moving from being a mission field to being a mission force that sends evangelists back to the very countries that once sent missionaries to them.

As we look at the modern map of the faith, it is clear that the center of gravity for Christianity has shifted toward the Global South. The vitality of the African church serves as a constant reminder that the gospel is not a static relic of the past, but a living, breathing force that constantly adapts and grows in new soil. This energy is matched by similar, though distinct, movements of faith emerging across the Atlantic, where the church is navigating its own unique challenges in the diverse and complex societies of Latin America.

## Key ideas

- The twentieth century saw a massive demographic shift as sub-Saharan Africa became one of the most vibrant centers of Christianity in the world.
- African-initiated churches played a crucial role in indigenizing the faith, blending Christian teachings with local cultural forms and a focus on spiritual power.
- During the era of apartheid in South Africa, the church was a deeply divided institution, serving both as a pillar of the system and as a leading voice for justice and reconciliation.
- African Christians have moved from the periphery to the center of global church leadership, increasingly shaping the theological and mission priorities of the worldwide body of believers.
