# Creation and Providence

When you drop a stone into a still pond, the ripples spread outward, touching every reed and pebble along the shore. Yet the person who threw the stone has not left the pond to itself. In classical Christian theology, that image helps us move from the doctrine of the Trinity we explored last time—where God exists eternally in loving communion as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—to the foundational question of how this triune God relates to everything outside Himself. Why is there a universe at all, and how does God keep it from spinning into chaos?

The first pillar of this relationship is the doctrine of creation ex nihilo, a Latin phrase meaning creation out of nothing. Historic orthodoxy insists that God did not fashion the cosmos out of some pre-existent slime, chaos, or raw material floating around in eternity. Had He done so, that raw material would be co-eternal with God, limiting His absolute sovereignty. Instead, Christian theology affirms that God spoke, and reality came into being from utter nothingness. As the opening verse of the Bible declares, in the King James Version of Genesis chapter one, verse one: “In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.” Because creation has no source other than the free, loving will of God, the entire universe is completely dependent on Him for its very existence at every single second.

Yet creating the world was not merely a distant, once-for-all event like winding a giant clock and walking away. That philosophical view, known as deism, became popular during the Enlightenment, but historic Christian tradition rejects it entirely. The Bible portrays a God who is intimately involved with His universe through the doctrine of providence. Christian theologians often distinguish between general providence, which is God’s sustaining of all creation and natural laws, and special providence, which is God’s specific direction of particular events toward His redemptive purposes. This ongoing preservation of the cosmos is sometimes called creatio continua, meaning continuous creation, expressing the truth that if God were to withdraw His sustaining power for even a single moment, existence would simply vanish.

Christian traditions have wrestled deeply with how this divine governance works alongside human freedom and the ordinary laws of nature. Reformed theology, drawing heavily on the insights of Saint Augustine and John Calvin, emphasizes God's meticulous sovereignty. In this view, God ordains whatsoever comes to pass, working even through human choices and natural disasters to accomplish His sovereign will, while still holding human beings morally responsible for their actions.

By contrast, the Arminian and Wesleyan traditions teach that God, by his grace, gives creatures a genuine freedom to respond, and Eastern Orthodox theology speaks of synergy, the cooperation of divine grace and human freedom. Catholic theologians have long debated these questions too, for example between the followers of Thomas Aquinas and of the Jesuit Luis de Molina. In these cooperative frameworks, God governs the world while granting genuine freedom to His creatures, ensuring that His redemptive goals are ultimately met without overriding human accountability.

Catholics, Orthodox, and Protestants all agree that God uses secondary causes—such as physics, biology, and human decision-making—to run the day-to-day operations of the world. When a farmer plants a seed, rain falls through meteorological processes, and the seed sprouts. A secular observer sees only nature at work. A theologian trained in the doctrine of providence sees both genuine natural processes and God working concurrently through those natural means. God does not need to break the laws of physics to act; as some theologians put it, the laws of nature are the steady habits of God's faithfulness.

Understanding how God sustains and governs the physical and historical world naturally leads us to focus on the crown of that creation. Next, we will explore what it means to be made in the very image of God and how historic theology defines human nature.

## Key ideas

- The doctrine of creation ex nihilo teaches that God brought the entire universe into existence out of nothing, free from any pre-existing raw materials.
- Divine providence encompasses general providence, God’s sustaining of all natural processes, and special providence, His direction of particular events toward His purposes.
- The theological term creatio continua describes God’s continual preservation of the universe at every single moment of its existence.
- Christian traditions balance divine sovereignty with human responsibility, with Reformed theology emphasizing meticulous divine decree and Arminian, Wesleyan, and Orthodox traditions emphasizing synergism.
- Christian theology views natural processes and human choices as secondary causes through which God routinely works to sustain the world.
