# The Psalms and Hebrew Poetry

Imagine walking up a long, winding stone path under the Mediterranean sun, surrounded by thousands of voices singing in unison. The air vibrates with bronze cymbals, lyres, and the deep, resonant notes of ram horns. You are not listening to an abstract theological lecture or reading a legal code. You are breathing in the poetry of ancient Israel. Now, we turn from historical narrative to the lyric heart of Israel's spiritual life. We enter the world of Hebrew poetry and the book of Psalms.

When we read biblical poetry today, we often look for the rhyming sounds and strict meters we learned in school. But Hebrew poetry operates on a completely different architecture. Instead of rhyming sounds, it uses a technique scholars call parallelism, a term first systematically analyzed by the eighteenth-century bishop Robert Lowth. In Hebrew parallelism, the poet places two thoughts side by side, and the second line answers, amplifies, or contrasts with the first. Think of it as a creative echo.

For instance, consider the opening of Psalm nineteen in the King James Version: “The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork.” Here, the second line does not rhyme with the first, but it deepens the image. The heavens are the expanse of the sky, and God's glory is matched by the intricate craftsmanship of his hands. This is synonymous parallelism, where the second line reinforces the first. Other times, Hebrew poets use antithetical parallelism, where the second line contrasts with the first, such as the proverb that a wise son maketh a glad father, but a foolish son is the heaviness of his mother. There is also synthetic parallelism, where the second line builds upon or completes the thought, carrying the listener forward. Beyond basic parallelism, Hebrew poets also employed formal structures such as alphabetic acrostics, found in passages like Psalms nine and ten, twenty-five, thirty-four, thirty-seven, one hundred eleven, one hundred twelve, one hundred nineteen, and one hundred forty-five, where successive lines or stanzas begin with consecutive letters of the Hebrew alphabet.

Beyond how these poems are structured, we have to ask how they actually functioned in the daily life of ancient Israel. The book of Psalms, known in Hebrew as Tehillim, which means praises, was essentially the hymnal and prayer book of the temple and the wider community. Tradition often associated the collection with King David, and superscriptions attribute seventy-three Psalms to him. Modern scholarship shows that the collection was gathered over centuries, reflecting the joys of royal coronations, the agony of the Babylonian exile, and the quiet devotion of ordinary pilgrims. These superscriptions also include technical musical and liturgical terms whose precise meanings remain debated by scholars, such as Selah, which may indicate a musical interlude or pause, Maschil, suggesting a contemplative song, and Michtam, a term of uncertain origin possibly denoting a golden poem or inscription.

The five-fold division of the entire Psalter mirrors the five books of the Torah, a deliberate arrangement that may present the Psalms as a new, responsive law or instruction from the people back to God. Within this five-part collection, specific liturgical groupings served distinct functions in ancient worship. For example, the Songs of Ascents, spanning Psalms one hundred twenty through one hundred thirty-four, were sung by pilgrims traveling upward to Jerusalem for the major annual festivals. Another important grouping is the Hallel, Psalms one hundred thirteen through one hundred eighteen, which families and the community chanted during festivals such as Passover.

To understand the Psalms, scholars categorize them into distinct literary types based on their function in worship. The first major category is hymns of praise, which invite the worshipper to celebrate God as creator and king simply for who He is. A second category is communal and individual laments. In fact, laments make up the single largest group of Psalms in the collection. These are raw, honest prayers of distress, sickness, or political oppression where the speaker cries out to God, asking why He seems silent. Far from showing a lack of faith, the inclusion of lament in sacred scripture teaches that honest emotional expression is a vital part of a life of faith.

A third important category is thanksgiving Psalms, where individuals or the community return to the temple to offer gratitude after being delivered from trouble. You also have royal Psalms, which celebrate the reign of Davidic kings and often point forward, in both Jewish and Christian interpretation, to an ultimate, ideal king. Finally, wisdom and liturgical Psalms provided moral instruction or guided priests and worshippers through the ritual gates of the temple.

These texts were sung, chanted, and prayed by generations of Israelites, shaping their identity in times of peace and national catastrophe. Christians and Jews alike have continued to use the Psalms as a primary vocabulary for addressing God, finding in them words for every human emotion from deep despair to ecstatic joy.

## Key ideas
- Hebrew poetry relies on parallelism and echoing thoughts rather than modern rhyming meters, and sometimes employs alphabetic acrostics for mnemonic or artistic effect.
- The entire book of Psalms is arranged in a five-part division that may symbolically mirror the five books of the Pentateuch.
- Superscriptions preserve ancient musical and liturgical terms, such as Selah, Maschil, and Michtam, alongside specific functional collections like the Songs of Ascents and the Hallel.
- Scholars categorize the Psalms into types including hymns, laments, thanksgiving songs, royal texts, and wisdom poems, with laments forming the largest group to voice human struggle within faith.
