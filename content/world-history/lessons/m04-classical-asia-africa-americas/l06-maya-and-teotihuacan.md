# The Maya and Teotihuacan

Picture a sprawling metropolis rising from a high mountain valley in central Mexico, filled with towering pyramids, a broad ceremonial avenue, and some two thousand walled apartment compounds. This was Teotihuacan at its height, a city that did not merely house an empire, but functioned as a colossal social and economic engine for the entire region. While kingdoms like Aksum were flourishing across the Atlantic in East Africa, Mesoamerica witnessed the rise of complex urban civilizations that reshaped their physical and intellectual landscapes. Teotihuacan stood as a titan of central architecture and trade, while to the south, across the tropical lowlands of the Yucatán peninsula and modern Guatemala, the Maya developed a brilliant network of independent city-states connected by shared culture, trade, and science.

To understand the scope of these classical Mesoamerican societies, we must look closely at their urban complexity. Teotihuacan, which reached its peak between about 100 CE and 650 CE, was meticulously planned on a grid system. Its central axis, the Avenue of the Dead, was anchored by the massive Pyramid of the Sun and the Pyramid of the Moon. These were not merely architectural marvels; they were sacred mountains carved in stone, symbolizing the cosmos and the origins of life. Scholars estimate that Teotihuacan housed over one hundred thousand residents, making it one of the largest cities in the ancient world. People lived in walled neighborhood compounds organized by craft specialization, from obsidian toolmakers to potters, revealing a highly stratified and coordinated urban society.

Meanwhile, the Maya civilization developed a vastly different urban pattern. Rather than a single centralized empire ruled from one capital, the Maya world consisted of dozens of powerful, competing city-states like Tikal, Palenque, and Calakmul. These cities featured towering limestone step pyramids, intricate ball courts for ritual sport, and vast plazas. Maya kings, known as ajaw, claimed divine descent and legitimized their rule through elaborate ceremonies, public architecture, and warfare. Despite political fragmentation, these city-states shared a profound cultural unity anchored by a common writing system, religious pantheon, and deep calendar traditions.

The intellectual and mathematical achievements of the classical Maya remain among the most sophisticated in human history. Long before many other global cultures, Maya astronomers and mathematicians utilized a base-twenty numerical system that included a fully functional concept of zero. This mathematical mastery allowed them to track celestial movements with astonishing precision. The Maya developed interlocking calendar systems, including the three-hundred-and-sixty-five-day solar year and the sacred two-hundred-and-sixty-day ritual round, which meshed together in a fifty-two-year cycle known as the Calendar Round. They recorded these calculations, along with royal genealogies and mythological narratives, in hieroglyphic texts carved on stone stelae and painted on folding bark books known as codices.

The relationship between Teotihuacan and the Maya world was deep and complex, driven primarily by long-distance trade and diplomacy. Archaeological evidence shows that Teotihuacan merchants traveled deep into Maya territory, exchanging ceramics and prized green obsidian blades for tropical goods like jade, feathers, and cacao. At cities like Tikal, inscriptions record direct political interventions by Teotihuacan emissaries, reshaping local dynasties and military alliances in the fourth century CE. This interaction demonstrates that classical Mesoamerica was an interconnected zone of exchange rather than a collection of isolated cultures.

Around 550 CE, Teotihuacan’s central temples were burned, probably in internal upheaval, and the city declined over the following century. The classic Maya experienced their own profound transformation some centuries later, in the ninth century CE, as prolonged droughts and intense warfare led to the abandonment of the southern lowland cities in what historians call the classic Maya collapse. Yet neither civilization vanished entirely; their descendants adapted, migrated, and carried their linguistic, agricultural, and cultural traditions forward into later eras. As these great Mesoamerican centers shifted and transformed, across the Mediterranean and the Near East, a different kind of monumental shift was taking place as the Roman world gave way to the rising power of the Byzantine Empire.

## Key ideas
- Teotihuacan was a massive, grid-planned metropolis in central Mexico that housed over one hundred thousand residents and exerted immense economic and political influence across Mesoamerica.
- The Maya civilization was organized into a network of independent, culturally unified city-states rather than a single centralized empire.
- Maya mathematicians developed a base-twenty numerical system incorporating zero, enabling precise astronomical observations and sophisticated interlocking calendars.
- Extensive trade networks connected Teotihuacan and Maya city-states, facilitating the exchange of luxury goods, raw materials, and political ideas.
