# Molecular Orbital Theory

Imagine you are holding a strong magnet near a stream of liquid oxygen. You might expect nothing to happen, because oxygen gas is a stable molecule with a simple structure. Yet, as the liquid flows past the magnet, it is visibly pulled toward the poles. This observation is a classic puzzle in chemistry. If you draw the Lewis structure for a diatomic oxygen molecule, you see a clean double bond with every electron paired up. According to that model, oxygen should be diamagnetic, meaning it should be slightly repelled by a magnetic field. But the reality is that oxygen is paramagnetic, meaning it is attracted to magnets because it contains unpaired electrons.

In our last lesson, we explored valence bond theory and hybridization. That approach is excellent for describing the geometry of molecules, like why methane forms a tetrahedron. However, valence bond theory treats electrons as if they stay localized between two specific atoms. To solve the mystery of oxygen, we need a broader perspective. This is where molecular orbital theory comes in. Instead of viewing electrons as belonging to one bond or one atom, this theory treats them as belonging to the entire molecule. It suggests that when atomic orbitals overlap, they merge to form new, larger orbitals that spread across the whole structure.

When two atomic orbitals combine, they form two types of molecular orbitals. The first is a bonding orbital. This orbital has lower energy than the original atomic orbitals, which makes the molecule more stable. The second is an antibonding orbital. This one has higher energy than the original atomic orbitals. If electrons occupy an antibonding orbital, they actually work against the bond, destabilizing the interaction. You can think of this like a tug of war. Bonding orbitals pull the nuclei together, while antibonding orbitals push them apart.

We can determine the strength of a bond using a value called the bond order. To calculate it, you subtract the number of electrons in antibonding orbitals from the number of electrons in bonding orbitals, and then divide that total by two. A higher bond order generally means a stronger, shorter bond. If the bond order is zero, the molecule is not stable and will not form. This calculation is a powerful tool because it allows us to predict whether a molecule can exist based purely on the energy levels of its electrons.

Let us return to our oxygen mystery. When two oxygen atoms come together, their atomic orbitals combine to fill these molecular energy levels. If you fill these levels from the bottom up, you find that the final two electrons end up in two separate antibonding orbitals. Because they are in separate orbitals, they remain unpaired. Those two lonely, unpaired electrons are exactly what make oxygen paramagnetic. The Lewis structure failed us because it forced the electrons into pairs, but molecular orbital theory correctly accounts for their behavior by looking at the energy of the whole system.

This theory also confirms what simpler models predict for the diatomic nitrogen molecule. A nitrogen molecule has ten valence electrons, five from each atom. Eight of them sit in bonding orbitals and only two in an antibonding orbital. Eight minus two is six, and six divided by two gives a bond order of three, matching its very strong triple bond and explaining why nitrogen gas is so incredibly inert and stable. Whether we are looking at simple gases or complex materials, molecular orbital theory provides the most accurate map of where electrons live and how they hold matter together.

While these theories of bonding describe the internal architecture of molecules, we must also consider how these particles behave in the physical world. Once we understand how atoms hold onto their electrons and shape themselves into molecules, we can start to investigate how those molecules interact with their environment, such as the pressure they exert when confined in a container.

## Key ideas

- Molecular orbital theory describes electrons as occupying orbitals that spread across the entire molecule rather than being localized between two specific atoms.
- Bonding orbitals are lower in energy and stabilize the molecule, while antibonding orbitals are higher in energy and destabilize the bond.
- Bond order is calculated by taking the number of bonding electrons minus the number of antibonding electrons, divided by two, to predict bond strength and stability.
- The theory explains the paramagnetism of oxygen by showing that the molecule contains two unpaired electrons in its higher-energy antibonding orbitals.
