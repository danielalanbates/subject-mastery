# Evangelicals and the Lausanne Movement

Imagine a massive stadium filled to capacity. The air is electric, not with the sound of a sporting event, but with the collective singing of thousands of voices. In the middle of the twentieth century, this scene became a hallmark of a resurgent movement. Following the Second World War, a distinct branch of Protestantism known as evangelicalism began to assert a powerful, international influence. While the previous lesson explored how the Catholic Church sought to renew itself through the Second Vatican Council, many Protestants were simultaneously seeking their own path toward global unity and mission.

Historians often define evangelicalism through four key marks. First is conversionism, the belief that lives need to be changed through a personal encounter with Christ. Second is biblicism, a high regard for the Bible as the primary authority for faith. Third is crucicentrism, a focus on the sacrifice of Jesus on the cross as the heart of the gospel. Finally, there is activism, the drive to share this message and put faith into practice. In the United States, these commitments found institutional expression in the nineteen forty-two formation of the National Association of Evangelicals, which sought to unite believers across denominational lines.

At the center of the movement’s public face was an American preacher whose name became synonymous with the era: Billy Graham. Graham was a global communicator. Through his crusades, which traveled to cities across the globe, he emphasized personal conversion and biblical authority. This message resonated with millions who felt alienated by the rising tide of secularism or the theological shifts in their home denominations. By the nineteen-sixties and seventies, this movement had grown into a sophisticated, organized force that was beginning to look far beyond its North American and European roots.

As evangelicalism expanded, a tension emerged. Many leaders realized that the movement was often perceived as a Western export, tied too closely to the political and economic interests of the United States and Great Britain. They needed a forum to address this. This desire for a global conversation led to the International Congress on World Evangelization, held in Lausanne, Switzerland, in 1974. It was a watershed moment. Unlike previous gatherings that were dominated by Western voices, Lausanne brought together thousands of leaders from over one hundred and fifty nations.

The Lausanne Congress is famous for producing the Lausanne Covenant. This document did more than just affirm traditional evangelical beliefs; it challenged the movement to rethink its mission. One of the most significant shifts was the adoption of the concept of integral mission. This term describes the belief that the church’s work includes both the proclamation of the gospel and a commitment to social justice. Proponents argued that if Christians were to take the Bible seriously, they could not ignore the hungry, the oppressed, or the victims of systemic injustice. Faith, they insisted, must show itself in tangible care for the physical and social well-being of others.

This shift was driven largely by the growing influence of leaders from the global South. These delegates from Africa, Asia, and Latin America brought a perspective forged in contexts of poverty, political instability, and post-colonial struggle. They pushed back against the idea that mission was a one-way street from the West to the rest of the world. Instead, they argued that the church is a global body where every member has a voice and a role to play. They reminded their Western counterparts that the gospel is not a cultural product to be exported, but a living faith that takes root in every culture.

The impact of this shift was profound. The Lausanne movement helped to redefine evangelicalism as a truly international fellowship. It moved the conversation away from narrow debates about American cultural politics and toward a broader focus on discipleship, theological education, and the practical needs of the poor. It also fostered a new era of cooperation, as evangelicals began to engage more deeply with the challenges of global poverty and human rights. The movement recognized that while the church had failed in many ways—often remaining silent in the face of colonial exploitation or social inequality—it had a divine mandate to be a light in every corner of the earth.

By the end of the twentieth century, the center of gravity for the evangelical movement had undeniably shifted toward the global South. The voices that once occupied the margins were now the ones shaping the agenda for the future. This transition was not always smooth, and it required a great deal of humility from Western churches to listen and learn from their brothers and sisters in other regions. Yet, it was this very process of listening that allowed the movement to survive and flourish in a rapidly changing world.

## Key ideas

- Evangelicalism is defined by four marks: conversionism, biblicism, crucicentrism, and activism.
- The nineteen forty-two formation of the National Association of Evangelicals provided a key institutional structure for the movement in the post-war era.
- The nineteen seventy-four Lausanne Congress served as a turning point, moving the evangelical movement toward greater international cooperation and away from Western dominance.
- The concept of integral mission emerged as a vital framework, emphasizing that the church’s mandate includes both preaching the gospel and actively addressing social injustice.
- Leaders from Africa, Asia, and Latin America became essential voices in the global church, challenging the movement to reflect the diversity and needs of the majority world.
