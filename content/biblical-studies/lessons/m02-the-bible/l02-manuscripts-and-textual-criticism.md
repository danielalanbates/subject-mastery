# Manuscript Transmission and Textual Criticism

Imagine you are holding a handwritten letter from a friend. If you were to copy that letter by hand, the chances of making a small error are quite high. You might accidentally skip a word, misspell a name, or transpose two letters. Now, imagine that your copy is used to create ten more copies, and those ten copies are used to create a hundred more. Over centuries, these small errors would multiply. This is the challenge scholars face when studying the biblical text. Today, we turn to the physical reality of those books: how they were copied, preserved, and reconstructed.

Before the invention of the printing press in the fifteenth century, every copy of the Bible was written by hand. Scribes worked on materials like papyrus, a paper-like reed product, or parchment, which is treated animal skin. These manuscripts were fragile. Because we do not possess the original documents, known as the autographs, we rely on thousands of manuscript copies.

Textual criticism is the scholarly discipline of analyzing these copies to determine the most likely wording of the original text. Scholars categorize manuscripts into families based on shared characteristics. The three primary New Testament families are the Alexandrian, the Western, and the Byzantine. Key exemplars, or representative manuscripts, include the fourth-century Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, which are among the most important early witnesses.

To evaluate these texts, scholars use two types of evidence. External criteria involve the age, geographical distribution, and genealogical relationships of the manuscripts. Internal criteria focus on the text itself. Scholars ask which reading is more likely to have been changed by a scribe, known as transcriptional probability, and which reading best fits the author's style and context, known as intrinsic probability. Early translations also serve as vital witnesses. These include the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament; the Vulgate, a Latin version; the Peshitta, a Syriac translation; and the Targums, which are Aramaic paraphrases of the Hebrew scriptures.

When a scribe copied a text, they were generally careful, but they were human. Most variations, or variants, are simple spelling differences or word order changes that do not affect the meaning. For example, if two manuscripts agree on a reading while one differs, scholars investigate why. Did the scribe misread a letter? Did they try to correct a perceived grammatical error? By applying critical principles, scholars work to reconstruct the text. This work results in standard critical editions, such as the Nestle-Aland Greek New Testament and the Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia, which serve as the foundation for modern biblical scholarship.

In the New Testament, we have an embarrassment of riches. We possess thousands of Greek manuscripts, far more than for any other work of ancient literature. In the Old Testament, the discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls in the twentieth century provided a massive leap forward. These scrolls, copied between about the third century BCE and the first century CE, are roughly a thousand years older than the earliest complete Hebrew manuscripts we had before. Many of them match the later Masoretic Text very closely, while others agree with the Septuagint, showing that more than one form of the text circulated in that period.

Scholars approach this work with different philosophies. Some prioritize the oldest and most reliable manuscripts, while others consider the majority of manuscripts. Most textual critics, including skeptics such as Bart Ehrman, agree that the great majority of variants are minor, though they disagree about how much the remaining ones matter. The invention of the printing press in the fifteenth century significantly reduced the introduction of new copying errors, helping to stabilize the text for future generations. The goal of the textual critic is to peel back the layers of human error to get as close as possible to the original authors. This process is a testament to the dedication of generations who viewed these texts as sacred and worth preserving at any cost.

## Key ideas

- Textual criticism is the scholarly process of comparing ancient manuscript copies to reconstruct the original wording of the biblical text.
- Scholars categorize manuscripts into families, such as the Alexandrian, Western, and Byzantine, using both external evidence and internal probability to determine the most accurate readings.
- Early translations like the Septuagint and the Vulgate serve as essential witnesses alongside the Greek and Hebrew manuscripts.
- The vast number of existing manuscripts and the stabilizing influence of the printing press provide scholars with a high degree of confidence that the original message has been preserved.
