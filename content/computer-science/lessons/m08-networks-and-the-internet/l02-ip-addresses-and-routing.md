# IP Addresses and Routing

Imagine you want to send a handwritten letter to a friend living on the other side of the world. You do not need to know the exact path the mail carrier takes. You simply write a specific address on the envelope, and the postal system handles the rest. Each local post office looks at your letter, checks the destination, and passes it along to a larger hub. Eventually, it reaches its final city and is delivered to your friend’s door. The global internet works on a strikingly similar principle, replacing street addresses with digital identifiers known as Internet Protocol addresses.

In our previous lesson, we learned that data travels across networks in small, manageable chunks called packets. But how does a packet know where it is going, and how does it find its way through the vast web of global cables? Every device connected to the internet, whether it is a smartphone, a laptop, or a web server, is assigned a unique number. This is the Internet Protocol address. You can think of it like a house number on a global street map. Version four of the internet protocol, which is a common format, uses four sets of numbers separated by periods.

An Internet Protocol address is divided into two distinct parts: the network portion and the host portion. This hierarchy is essential for routing. To distinguish these two parts, devices use a subnet mask or a notation called classless inter domain routing. These tools tell the computer which bits of the address identify the specific network and which bits identify the individual machine within that network. By grouping addresses into networks, the internet avoids the need for every router to know the location of every single device on earth. Instead, routers only need to know how to reach the network where a device resides.

When a router receives a packet, it must decide where to send it next. It does this by consulting a routing table. This table contains a list of known network destinations and the corresponding path to reach them. To make a decision, the router applies the longest prefix match rule. It compares the destination Internet Protocol address against the entries in its table to find the most specific network match. If multiple entries could technically fit, the router chooses the one that covers the most bits of the address, ensuring the packet is sent toward the most accurate destination.

This process is known as hop by hop routing. A packet does not have a pre-written map for its entire journey. Instead, at every single router along the path, the packet is handed off to the next device. It is a series of short, local decisions. The router asks itself which path will get the packet closer to its destination while avoiding congestion or broken links. This happens in fractions of a second, thousands of times over, as your data makes its way across the planet.

This design choice was intentional and revolutionary. During the Cold War, the engineer Paul Baran at the Rand Corporation proposed a communication network that could survive the loss of its hubs by routing messages around the damage. Historians note that the Advanced Research Projects Agency Network, or ARPANET, the forerunner of the internet, was built mainly to let research computers share resources, but it adopted the same idea of many paths. By building the internet as a web of many paths, the engineers ensured that the network would be resilient. If a submarine cable under the Atlantic ocean is cut, or if a specific router in a city goes offline, the remaining routers simply update their tables. They identify that the usual path is blocked and immediately start sending packets along a different, secondary route.

Because the path is dynamic, your data might take a different route every time you load a webpage. One packet might travel through a cable under the sea, while the next takes a path through satellite links. This is why the internet is so reliable. It does not depend on a single, fragile connection. It assumes that links will fail, and it treats that failure as a routine event rather than a disaster. The system is constantly sensing the environment and adapting to ensure that the packets eventually reach their destination.

Of course, the internet is a chaotic environment. Packets might arrive out of order, or they might be lost entirely if a link fails while the data is in transit. We have seen how packets get from one address to another, but we still need a way to ensure that the message arrives intact and in the correct order. Since the network itself focuses only on moving individual packets, we need a higher-level set of rules to manage the conversation, which is where we turn next to the concept of reliable delivery.

## Key ideas

- Internet Protocol addresses are divided into network and host portions to create a hierarchy that makes global routing scalable.
- Routers use subnet masks and classless inter domain routing to determine which part of an address belongs to a network and which to a specific device.
- When choosing a path, routers apply the longest prefix match rule to identify the most specific network destination in their routing table.
- The hop by hop routing method allows the network to be highly resilient by dynamically adjusting paths when specific links or routers fail.
- The internet was built with many redundant paths to ensure that communication remains possible even if large portions of the network are damaged or disconnected.
