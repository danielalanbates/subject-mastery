# Money and How Banks Create It

Imagine walking into a bakery with a heavy sack of wheat, hoping to trade it for a cake, only to discover the baker already has more wheat than she can use. That awkward moment highlights the fundamental friction of a barter economy. In our previous lessons, we explored how aggregate demand and supply pulse through the macroeconomy, moving output and the price level. But those transactions rely on a lubricator so pervasive we rarely think about it. We are stepping into a new module on money, banking, and monetary policy, and our journey begins with a deceptively simple question: what actually is money, and where does it come from?

To answer that, we have to look past the physical pieces of paper in your wallet or the digital numbers on your banking app. Economists define money by its functions, not its material. First, money serves as a medium of exchange, eliminating the need for a double coincidence of wants by acting as an accepted intermediary. Second, it provides a unit of account, a universal yardstick that lets us compare the value of a sleek sports car, a cup of coffee, and an hour of labor. Third, it functions as a store of value, allowing you to save purchasing power from today and spend it next year, assuming inflation does not erode it too aggressively.

Throughout history, societies have used various commodities for these functions, from cowrie shells to gold coins. Commodity money has intrinsic value, while modern fiat money has no intrinsic value and is accepted simply because a government decrees it and society trusts it. That trust is managed through two main categories in the money supply. M-one includes currency in circulation and checkable deposits, which are highly liquid assets you can spend immediately. M-two includes M-one plus savings deposits, time deposits, and money market funds, which require a bit more effort to convert into spendable cash.

Yet, understanding currency and deposits only tells half the story. The truly fascinating machinery of modern macroeconomics involves the monetary base and how commercial banks operate. The monetary base consists of currency in circulation plus bank reserves held at the central bank or in vault cash. When a bank extends a loan, it actually creates money. Rather than merely lending out existing deposits, a bank creates a new checking deposit of equal size for the borrower when the loan is approved.

Regulations and prudent management require banks to hold only a fraction of their deposits in reserve, say ten percent, to meet daily withdrawals. The bank can take the rest of your deposit and lend it out to someone who wants to buy equipment or start a small business. That borrower takes the loan and pays a supplier, who promptly deposits that money into a bank account. Now, that second bank holds a new deposit, keeps ten percent in reserve, and lends out the remainder. This cycle repeats, cascading through the banking system in what mainstream economists call the money multiplier.

Different schools of thought view this process through distinct lenses. Neoclassical and Keynesian economists generally accept the fractional reserve model and the central bank's ability to steer the money supply by altering the monetary base. By contrast, Post-Keynesian economists argue that money is endogenous, meaning commercial banks make loans first to creditworthy borrowers and find reserves later, so the multiplier gets the causation backward. Central banks themselves increasingly describe it this way; the Bank of England did so in a widely read 2014 paper. In the United States, reserve requirements have been zero since March 2020, so the ten percent example below is a teaching model rather than current law. Meanwhile, critics in the Austrian school argue that fractional reserve banking is inherently unstable and inflationary because it expands the supply of credit beyond real savings.

Despite these theoretical debates, the standard money multiplier is calculated as the inverse of the reserve requirement. If the required reserve ratio is ten percent, or zero point one, the multiplier is one divided by zero point one, which equals ten. In theory, an initial injection into the monetary base could expand the broader money supply by a factor of ten, turning a modest cash injection into a much larger pool of spending power.

Of course, the real world is slightly messier than our clean theoretical formula. If banks choose to hold extra cash as excess reserves beyond what is required, or if everyday citizens prefer to hold more physical currency in their wallets rather than depositing it, the actual multiplier shrinks. During times of severe financial panic, these behavioral shifts can cause the money creation process to stall entirely, a breakdown we examine more closely when looking at how central banks step in to stabilize the system.

For now, remember that banks are not merely passive safety deposit boxes holding your savings. They are active creators of liquidity, transforming a handful of physical reserves into a robust network of credit that oils the wheels of commerce.

## Key ideas
- Money is defined by its three functions: acting as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value.
- The monetary base consists of currency in circulation plus bank reserves, and central bank actions that alter this base affect the broader money supply.
- Commercial banks create money when they extend loans, generating a new deposit of equal size rather than simply lending out pre-existing funds.
- Mainstream models use the money multiplier to show how fractional reserve banking expands deposits, while other schools like Post-Keynesian and Austrian economics offer alternative views on endogenous money and credit stability.
