# The Vietnam War

In the mid-twentieth century, Southeast Asia became a central theater of the Cold War. For many American policymakers, the region was a series of dominos. They argued that if one nation fell to communism, its neighbors would inevitably follow. This domino theory was a primary justification for United States involvement. However, historians offer several interpretations for why the United States committed to the conflict. Some emphasize the policy of containment, arguing that stopping the spread of communism was a global necessity. Others point to the need to maintain American credibility, fearing that failing to support an ally would embolden adversaries elsewhere. A third school of thought describes the war as a quagmire, where a series of small, incremental miscalculations by successive administrations trapped the United States in an unwinnable conflict.

Vietnam had been a French colony for decades before Japanese occupation during the Second World War. After the war, a nationalist movement led by Ho Chi Minh sought independence. When the French attempted to reclaim their colony, they were defeated in 1954. A peace agreement divided the country into a communist north and a pro-Western south. The United States stepped in to support the South Vietnamese government, viewing Ho Chi Minh’s vision of a unified, communist Vietnam as a threat to the global balance of power.

By the mid-1960s, the conflict had escalated into a major American military commitment. Following the Gulf of Tonkin incident in 1964, where North Vietnamese patrol boats allegedly fired on American destroyers, Congress passed a resolution giving President Lyndon Johnson broad authority to escalate the war. American forces relied on superior air power and search-and-destroy missions to combat the Viet Cong, a communist guerrilla force in the south. The enemy proved difficult to identify, as they blended into the civilian population and used complex tunnel networks to survive relentless bombing.

The war’s human cost and moral implications sparked intense domestic opposition. In 1969, the government began choosing draftees by lottery, which was meant to make the draft fairer but did little to calm public anger. The antiwar movement gained momentum as Americans grappled with reports of atrocities, such as the 1968 My Lai massacre, where American soldiers killed hundreds of unarmed civilians. In 1970, the killing of four students by the National Guard at Kent State University during a protest further polarized the nation. Alongside the protests, a youth counterculture rejected mainstream values, embracing rock music, long hair, communal living, drug use and freer attitudes toward sex, most famously at the Woodstock music festival in 1969. Disillusionment deepened in 1971 when the New York Times published the Pentagon Papers, a secret government study that revealed how multiple administrations had misled the public about the scope and progress of the war.

The turning point occurred in 1968 with the Tet Offensive. During this lunar new year holiday, communist forces launched a massive, coordinated attack across South Vietnam. While the United States military repelled the offensive, the images of fighting shattered the public’s belief that victory was near. Support for the war plummeted, and President Johnson announced he would not seek reelection. In response to the perceived overreach of executive power, Congress passed the War Powers Act in 1973, requiring the president to consult with Congress before committing troops to long-term conflicts. Additionally, the Twenty-sixth Amendment was ratified in 1971, lowering the voting age to eighteen, partly in response to the argument that young men old enough to be drafted should have a voice in the democratic process.

When Richard Nixon took office in 1969, he promised to achieve peace with honor. His strategy, known as Vietnamization, involved gradually withdrawing American troops while training the South Vietnamese army to take over the fighting. Despite this, Nixon expanded the war by authorizing secret bombings in neighboring Cambodia. By 1973, the Paris Peace Accords were signed, leading to the withdrawal of the last American combat troops.

The war concluded in 1975. Without American support, the South Vietnamese government collapsed. The fall of Saigon marked the unification of the country under communist rule. The conflict resulted in millions of Vietnamese deaths and the loss of about fifty-eight thousand American lives. It left a profound scar on the American psyche, creating deep skepticism toward government intervention that would shape political debates for decades.

## Key ideas

- The domino theory, the need for containment, and the desire to maintain international credibility drove United States involvement in Vietnam, though some historians argue the conflict resulted from a series of strategic miscalculations.
- The war was defined by grueling guerrilla combat, which led to significant domestic unrest, particularly after revelations like the My Lai massacre and the Pentagon Papers.
- The draft lottery, the Kent State shootings, and the expansion of the war into Cambodia fueled a powerful antiwar movement that led to the Twenty-sixth Amendment and the War Powers Act.
- President Nixon’s policy of Vietnamization sought to exit the war while maintaining South Vietnamese sovereignty, but the conflict ended in 1975 with the total collapse of the South.
