# The Fundamentalist-Modernist Controversy

Imagine walking into an American church in the early twentieth century. You might expect to hear a message focused on the saving power of the cross. Yet, in many pulpits during this era, you were just as likely to hear a sermon centered on social reform, the latest findings in biology, or the idea that the Bible was a collection of noble myths rather than literal history. This shift in focus created a deep, painful fracture in American Protestantism, setting the stage for what we now call the Fundamentalist-Modernist controversy.

In our last lesson, we explored the rise of Pentecostalism, which emphasized the direct, experiential power of the Holy Spirit. While Pentecostals were seeking a new outpouring of spiritual gifts, many established Protestant denominations were grappling with different pressures. As the nineteenth century gave way to the twentieth, new ideas about human history, biblical criticism, and science challenged traditional interpretations of the Christian faith. Modernists, or those who embraced these new perspectives, argued that the church needed to adapt to the modern world. They often prioritized human progress and the Social Gospel, a movement that sought to apply Christian ethics to social problems like poverty, labor rights, and urban decay.

This shift deeply concerned many conservative believers. They feared that if the church abandoned the core doctrines of the faith, it would cease to be Christian. To define their position, conservative leaders identified five key fundamentals: the inerrancy of Scripture, the virgin birth of Christ, the substitutionary atonement, the physical resurrection of Jesus, and the reality of biblical miracles. Between 1910 and 1915, these leaders published a series of twelve small books titled The Fundamentals. These booklets were distributed widely to pastors and laypeople. They defended the traditional, orthodox view of the Bible against the influence of modern biblical criticism, which analyzed the Bible using the same historical methods one might use to study any other ancient text.

The tension between these two camps reached a fever pitch in the mid-1920s. The debate was not just about theology; it was about the identity of the American church. The most famous flashpoint occurred in Dayton, Tennessee, during the summer of 1925. A high school teacher named John Scopes was brought to trial for teaching the theory of evolution, which violated a state law at the time. The trial became a national spectacle, pitting the politician William Jennings Bryan, who helped the prosecution, against the defense lawyer Clarence Darrow in a courtroom drama that captured the attention of the world. While the trial was about a legal statute, it was symbolically about whether modern science and traditional religion could coexist. The media portrayed it as a battle between enlightened modernity and narrow-minded tradition, though the reality was far more nuanced on both sides.

The aftermath of this era left a lasting mark on American Christianity. The controversy moved into the very heart of the church. Seminaries, which trained the next generation of pastors, were split down the middle. This led to significant institutional ruptures. For instance, in the Presbyterian Church, the divide became so severe that conservative scholars left existing institutions to form new ones, such as Westminster Theological Seminary in 1929. This eventually led to the formation of the Orthodox Presbyterian Church in 1936, as conservatives sought a space where their theological convictions would be upheld.

Denominations faced internal crises as they struggled to maintain unity while their leaders disagreed on the most fundamental questions of faith. Many churches eventually divided, leading to the creation of new denominations, schools, and mission agencies that were intentionally conservative or intentionally progressive. This period solidified the divide between those who identified as Fundamentalists—who insisted on a strict adherence to these traditional doctrines—and those who identified as Modernists. Yet, it is important to remember that these labels were fluid. Many people found themselves caught in the middle, wanting to hold onto the historic faith while also engaging honestly with the challenges of the modern era.

Ultimately, this period of intense internal conflict left the American church fragmented but also more self-aware. As the 1930s approached, the landscape of Christianity in the United States had been permanently altered. The focus shifted away from the broad, unified vision of the nineteenth century toward a more polarized environment. As we move forward in our study, we will see how these same churches, having just navigated their own domestic upheavals, were suddenly forced to confront the rise of totalitarian regimes in Europe that would challenge the very survival of the faith.

## Key ideas

- The Fundamentalist-Modernist controversy was a major conflict within American Protestantism concerning how the church should respond to modern science, biblical criticism, and social issues.
- Fundamentalists defined their movement through five core doctrines: the inerrancy of Scripture, the virgin birth, substitutionary atonement, the resurrection, and the reality of miracles.
- The 1925 Scopes trial became a national symbol of the struggle between evolutionary theory and traditional religious beliefs, highlighting the divide between modern secular thought and conservative Christianity.
- The controversy caused lasting divisions within denominations and seminaries, leading to the formation of separate institutions like Westminster Theological Seminary and the Orthodox Presbyterian Church.
