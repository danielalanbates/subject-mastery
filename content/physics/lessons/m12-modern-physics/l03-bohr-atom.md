# The Bohr Atom

When you heat a tube of hydrogen gas until it glows and look at the light through a prism, you do not see a smooth rainbow of colors. Instead, you see a few sharp, distinct lines of bright color, like glowing bars against a dark background. Classical physics could not explain why this happens. According to the laws of electricity and magnetism, an electron orbiting a positive nucleus should continuously spiral inward while radiating energy, meaning atoms should collapse in a fraction of a second and emit a continuous smear of light rather than sharp lines. Solving this puzzle required a radical new image of the atom, one built on the quantum ideas you encountered in our last lesson.

In nineteen thirteen, the Danish physicist Niels Bohr proposed a breakthrough model that saved the atom from collapse by introducing the concept of quantization. Bohr suggested that electrons do not spiral haphazardly around the nucleus. Instead, they are restricted to specific, circular orbits, which he called stationary states. He postulated that the orbital angular momentum of the electron is quantized, meaning it can only take on integer multiples of a fundamental constant, which restricts the electron to specific allowed radii and energies. While an electron stays in one of these allowed orbits, it does not radiate energy at all. It maintains a constant energy level. Each orbit corresponds to a specific, fixed amount of energy. The energy of each allowed level in hydrogen is given by a formula where the energy equals negative thirteen point six electron volts divided by the square of the principal quantum number. The electron can only jump between these permitted levels, rather than drifting through the spaces in between.

To picture this, imagine a ladder where the rungs are spaced at very specific heights, but the gaps between the rungs are strictly forbidden zones. A person can stand on the first rung, or the second rung, but never floating in the empty air between them. In the Bohr atom, the nucleus sits at the center, and the electron orbits are the steps outward. The lowest energy orbit sits closest to the nucleus, known as the ground state. Higher orbits sit further out, representing excited states with higher energy values.

When an electron jumps down from a higher energy level to a lower one, it gives up the exact difference in energy by spitting out a single particle of light, which we call a photon. The frequency of that emitted light depends strictly on the energy gap between the two orbits. Because the allowed orbits have fixed, unchanging sizes and energies, the energy gaps are also fixed. This means only specific frequencies of light can ever be emitted or absorbed by a given element. That explains why hydrogen displays those sharp, isolated lines through a prism rather than a continuous rainbow. Each bright line corresponds to an electron falling between specific quantum rungs.

Let us walk through a quick example of how energy changes in this system. Suppose an electron in a hydrogen atom drops from a higher orbit with an energy of negative zero point eight five electron volts down to a lower orbit with an energy of negative three point four electron volts. To find the energy of the emitted photon, we subtract the lower energy value from the higher energy value. The difference gives us two point five five electron volts of energy. That specific packet of energy corresponds to blue-green visible light in the Balmer series of hydrogen, which is a sensible result for an electronic transition in this atom.

While the Bohr model achieved stunning success in predicting the exact spectral lines of hydrogen, it had clear limits. It worked brilliantly for single electron systems like hydrogen or ionized helium, but it failed completely when applied to atoms with multiple electrons, where mutual electrical repulsions complicate the orbits. Physics would soon need a more abstract, wave-based picture of nature to describe those larger atoms accurately. Even so, Bohr provided the crucial stepping stone, showing how quantum rules govern the internal architecture of matter.

## Key ideas
- The Bohr model solved the classical paradox of atomic collapse by proposing that electrons orbit only in specific, quantized energy levels governed by quantized angular momentum.
- Electrons in stationary states do not radiate energy, and they can only change energy by jumping entirely from one allowed orbit to another.
- The energy levels of hydrogen are defined by a formula scaling inversely with the square of the principal quantum number.
- The emission of a photon occurs when an electron drops from a higher energy level to a lower one, producing specific spectral lines such as those in the Balmer series.
- While exceptionally successful for hydrogen, the Bohr model is limited and fails to accurately predict the behavior of multi electron atoms.
