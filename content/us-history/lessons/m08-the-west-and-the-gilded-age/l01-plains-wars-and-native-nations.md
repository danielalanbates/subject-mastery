# The Plains Wars and Native Nations

Imagine standing on the vast, golden expanse of the Great Plains in the mid-nineteenth century. You see a horizon that stretches endlessly in every direction, interrupted only by the massive, shifting herds of bison that sustained the life, culture, and spirituality of the Lakota, Cheyenne, Comanche, and other nations. For these peoples, the land was not a commodity to be owned, but a living relative. Yet, as the United States emerged from the trauma of the Civil War, this landscape became the stage for a brutal, transformative conflict. The push to settle the West brought the federal government into direct, often violent confrontation with the indigenous nations whose sovereignty had been recognized in earlier treaties but was now increasingly viewed as an obstacle to national expansion.

The Plains Wars were not a single, unified conflict, but a series of campaigns and battles that spanned from the 1860s through the 1890s. As the United States military expanded its reach, it sought to force Native nations onto reservations, which were smaller, government-designated tracts of land. This policy was intended to clear the way for miners, ranchers, and farmers. Resistance was fierce and strategically brilliant. Leaders like Red Cloud of the Lakota and Sitting Bull of the Hunkpapa Lakota organized successful defenses of their territories. The Battle of the Little Bighorn in 1876, where a coalition of Lakota and Northern Cheyenne fighters defeated Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer’s Seventh Cavalry, stands as the most famous example of indigenous military success. Yet, the overwhelming industrial capacity of the United States, combined with the systematic destruction of the bison herds by commercial hunters and government policy, eventually made sustained armed resistance impossible.

By the late 1880s, the federal government shifted its strategy from direct military conquest to a policy of forced assimilation. Policymakers argued that the only way for Native Americans to survive was to abandon their traditional cultures and adopt the values and lifestyles of white Americans. This shift was codified in the Dawes Act of 1887. The law aimed to dismantle communal tribal structures by breaking up reservations into individual plots of land to be distributed to Native families. The stated goal was to turn indigenous people into independent farmers. In practice, the Dawes Act was a catastrophe for Native landholdings. It opened up millions of acres of surplus land to white settlement, leaving many Native families with plots too small or too arid to support themselves.

Alongside the theft of land, the government launched an assault on indigenous identity through the boarding school system. The Carlisle Indian Industrial School, founded in 1879, served as the model for this effort. Children were forcibly removed from their families and sent to schools where they were forbidden to speak their native languages, wear traditional clothing, or practice their religions. The motto of the school’s founder, Richard Henry Pratt, was "Kill the Indian, Save the Man," a phrase he used in his 1892 speech at the National Conference of Charities and Correction. These schools were often characterized by harsh discipline, manual labor, and the systematic erasure of cultural heritage. While the schools were designed to force assimilation, they also inadvertently created a new generation of leaders who used the English language and Western education to advocate for Native rights and tribal sovereignty in the twentieth century.

Despite these immense pressures, Native nations did not simply disappear or remain passive victims. They practiced cultural survival through the secret preservation of ceremonies, the maintenance of oral traditions, and the adaptation of new political strategies. The Ghost Dance movement of the late 1880s, a spiritual revival that swept across many Plains nations, represented a profound hope for the restoration of the world before the arrival of white settlers. The tragic massacre at Wounded Knee in 1890, where soldiers killed about 250 to 300 Lakota people, many of them women and children, marked a grim turning point. It effectively ended the era of large-scale military resistance, yet the spirit of resistance evolved into a persistent struggle for legal and political recognition.

The conquest of the Great Plains was a fundamental reconfiguration of the American map and identity. By the end of the century, the reservation system and the policies of the Dawes Act had left most Native nations impoverished and dispossessed of their ancestral territories. However, the survival of these nations against such overwhelming odds remains a testament to their resilience. As the federal government tightened its grip on the interior of the continent, the stage was set for a massive transformation of the landscape.

## Key ideas

- The Plains Wars resulted from the collision between the United States' desire for western expansion and the sovereign rights of Native nations to their ancestral lands and resources.
- The Dawes Act of 1887 sought to destroy tribal identity by forcing the transition from communal land ownership to individual farming, which led to the massive loss of Native territory.
- Federal boarding schools were designed to forcibly assimilate Native children by stripping them of their languages, religions, and cultural connections to their families.
- Despite military defeat and aggressive assimilation policies, Native nations employed diverse strategies of resistance and cultural preservation to maintain their identity and sovereignty.
