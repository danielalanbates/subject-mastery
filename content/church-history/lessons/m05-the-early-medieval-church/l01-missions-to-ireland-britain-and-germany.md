# Missions to Ireland, Britain and the Germans

Imagine a map of Europe in the year 450 CE. The Roman Empire, which had provided a common language, law, and structure for the spread of Christianity, was crumbling in the West. As the previous lesson explored, the collapse of Roman authority left a power vacuum, but it also created a frontier. Beyond the old borders of the empire lay vast territories where the Christian faith had not yet taken root. In this lesson, we follow the monks and missionaries who ventured into these lands beyond the empire, transforming the religious map of the world.

Our story begins in Ireland, a land that had never been part of the Roman Empire. The most famous figure in its conversion was Patrick, a man born in Roman Britain who had been kidnapped by Irish raiders as a teenager. After escaping and later returning as a bishop in the fifth century, Patrick traveled throughout the island. He integrated the existing social structures into the life of the church. Because Ireland lacked the dense urban centers of the Mediterranean, the church there developed around monasteries rather than the city-based bishoprics common in the old empire.

These Irish monasteries became centers of intense learning and missionary zeal. Irish monks were instrumental in preserving Latin texts and producing artistic treasures like the Book of Kells. Figures like Columbanus traveled to the continent, establishing influential monasteries such as Luxeuil in what is now France. From the island of Iona, off the coast of Scotland, monks like Columba fanned out across Britain. They were known for their strict asceticism and their deep commitment to the study of the Bible.

While the Irish were moving eastward, the Roman papacy began looking northward. In 597 CE, a monk named Augustine, sent by Pope Gregory the Great, arrived in the kingdom of Kent in what is now England. Augustine of Canterbury successfully baptized King Ethelbert and established a base in Canterbury. This mission brought the English church into direct alignment with the customs and authority of Rome. This created tension with the existing Celtic traditions. The two groups differed on key issues, such as the method for calculating the date of Easter and the proper style of the monastic tonsure, or haircut. These disagreements were formally addressed at the Synod of Whitby in 664 CE. The king of Northumbria decided to adopt the Roman practices, which helped unify the church in Britain under the leadership of Rome.

As the seventh and eighth centuries progressed, the focus shifted toward the Germanic tribes living in what is now Germany and the Netherlands. The most pivotal figure was Boniface, an English monk who felt a divine call to preach to these peoples. Boniface was a master of organization. He established dioceses and held councils to reform the clergy. His work was often marked by the destruction of pagan sacred sites, which served as a clear, sometimes violent, rejection of local religious practices. While such actions were seen by the church as triumphs over idolatry, they also reflect the often coercive and confrontational nature of these missionary encounters.

Boniface is famously remembered for chopping down a tree known as the Oak of Thor. When he was not harmed by the local deities, many people concluded that the Christian God held true power. Boniface’s lasting contribution was the creation of a stable, hierarchical church structure in the region, centered on the Archbishopric of Mainz. His work ended in 754 CE, when he was killed by a pagan group in Frisia, in what is now the Netherlands. His martyrdom solidified his legacy as the apostle to the Germans.

The work of these men was not merely about spreading a new set of beliefs. It was about creating a new European identity. By the year 800 CE, the church had become the primary institution holding together the disparate peoples of the North and West. Yet, this success brought new challenges. As the church gained political power and became deeply entwined with kings and emperors, it often struggled with the tension between its spiritual mission and its reliance on secular authority. These developments set the stage for the rise of a new imperial partnership that would reshape the continent once again.

## Key ideas

- The Synod of Whitby in 664 CE resolved critical differences between Celtic and Roman traditions, such as the dating of Easter, favoring the Roman model for the sake of church unity.
- Irish monasticism played a vital role in preserving classical and Christian literacy, while missionaries like Columbanus expanded the church's reach into continental Europe.
- Boniface established a lasting ecclesiastical structure in Germany, culminating in his martyrdom in 754 CE and the founding of the Archbishopric of Mainz.
- Missionary expansion involved complex and sometimes violent encounters with pagan cultures, including the destruction of sacred sites and the use of royal authority to secure the church's position.
