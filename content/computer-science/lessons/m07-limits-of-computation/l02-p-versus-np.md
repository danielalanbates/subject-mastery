# P Versus NP

Imagine you are organizing a large dinner party. You have a list of twenty guests, but they have complicated seating preferences. Some people refuse to sit next to one another, while others insist on being near their best friends. Your task is to find a seating arrangement that satisfies everyone. If I give you a specific seating chart, you can quickly check it against your list of rules. You simply look at each pair of neighbors and confirm they are happy. This is easy to verify. However, finding that perfect arrangement from scratch is a different matter. You might have to try millions of combinations before finding one that works. This simple scenario sits at the heart of one of the deepest mysteries in computer science: the relationship between the class P and the class NP.

In our previous lesson, we discussed tractable problems, which are those we can solve in a reasonable amount of time, and intractable problems, which take far too long to compute. The class P, which stands for polynomial time, represents the set of all problems that a computer can solve quickly. If a problem is in P, the time it takes to find an answer grows at a manageable rate as the input size increases. For example, sorting a list of names or searching for a specific value in a database falls into this category. These are the problems where computers excel, providing answers almost instantly.

Then there is the class NP, which stands for nondeterministic polynomial time. Do not let the name confuse you; it does not mean not polynomial. Instead, think of NP as the class of problems where, if someone gives you a potential solution, you can verify its correctness in polynomial time. Returning to our dinner party, finding the perfect seating chart is an NP problem. It might be incredibly hard to find the solution, but once you have a candidate arrangement in your hand, checking it is fast. Every problem in P is also in NP, because if you can solve a problem quickly, you can certainly verify a solution quickly by just solving it yourself.

The million-dollar question in our field is whether P equals NP. In other words, if we can quickly verify a solution to a problem, does that imply we can also quickly find that solution? Most computer scientists suspect that the answer is no. They believe that there are problems in NP that are fundamentally harder than those in P. If someone were to prove that P equals NP, it would change everything. It would mean that many problems we currently think are difficult, such as breaking complex encryption codes or finding optimal logistical routes, could actually be solved with ease.

Within the class NP, there exists a special subset of problems known as NP-complete. These are the hardest problems in NP. They are the gatekeepers of computational complexity. The defining characteristic of an NP-complete problem is that if you could find a fast, polynomial-time algorithm for just one of them, you would automatically have a fast solution for every single problem in NP. They are all linked together. If you solve one, you solve them all. Examples include the traveling salesperson problem, where one must find the shortest route through a series of cities, and the boolean satisfiability problem, which asks if there is an assignment of true or false values that makes a complex logical expression true.

As of the mid-twenty-twenties, no one has proven that P equals NP, nor has anyone proven that they are different. It remains one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics and computer science. We have spent decades trying to find a fast algorithm for an NP-complete problem, and we have failed. We have also spent decades trying to prove that such an algorithm cannot exist, and we have failed there, too. This suggests that the boundary between easy-to-check and easy-to-solve is incredibly subtle and possibly impenetrable.

This uncertainty forces us to be clever when we encounter these hard problems in the real world. Since we cannot wait billions of years for a perfect answer, we often have to settle for something else. We look for ways to get close enough, or we accept that for some tasks, a good answer today is better than a perfect answer in the distant future. We will explore those practical strategies in our next lesson, where we look at how to navigate these impossible boundaries using heuristics and approximation.

## Key ideas

- The class P contains problems that computers can solve efficiently, while the class NP contains problems where a given solution can be verified efficiently.
- NP-complete problems are the most difficult problems in NP, and they share the property that a fast solution for one would imply a fast solution for all of them.
- Most researchers believe that P does not equal NP, meaning that verifying a solution is fundamentally easier than discovering one from scratch.
- The question of whether P equals NP remains one of the most significant unsolved problems in all of science as of the mid-twenty-twenties.
