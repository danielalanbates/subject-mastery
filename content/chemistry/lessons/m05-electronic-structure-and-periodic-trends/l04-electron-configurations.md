# Electron Configurations

Imagine you are trying to park cars in a multi-story garage where each level has specific parking spots. The lowest levels are the most convenient, so drivers always fill those up first before heading to the higher, less accessible floors. Electrons in an atom follow a similar logic. They occupy the lowest available energy levels first, a process we call the Aufbau principle, which comes from the German word for building up. As we move through the periodic table, we add one electron at a time, filling orbitals in order of increasing energy, starting from the one s orbital and moving outward.

This orderly filling is guided by two fundamental rules. First, the Pauli exclusion principle tells us that each individual orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons, and those two must have opposite spins. Think of this like a bunk bed; you cannot have two people in the same bed unless they are arranged in a specific, compatible way. Second, Hund’s rule governs how electrons fill orbitals of the same energy, such as the three p orbitals. Electrons will occupy these empty orbitals singly with parallel spins before they begin to pair up. It is like people choosing to sit in separate empty seats on a bus before forced to share a row.

To describe this arrangement, we use electron configuration notation. You say this by naming the principal energy level, the orbital type, and the number of electrons in that orbital. For example, the configuration for nitrogen is one s two, two s two, two p three. You read the superscript numbers as the quantity of electrons. When we reach the outermost shell of an atom, we call those electrons valence electrons. These are the most important players in chemistry because they are the ones that participate in bonding and determine how an element reacts with its neighbors.

Let us look at a slightly larger atom, like sodium. Sodium has eleven electrons. Following the energy sequence, we fill the one s orbital with two, the two s orbital with two, and the three p orbitals with six. That accounts for ten electrons. The final electron must go into the next available level, which is the three s orbital. So, the configuration is one s two, two s two, two p six, three s one. Because there is only one electron in that outermost three s orbital, sodium has one valence electron, which explains why it is so eager to lose that electron when it forms chemical bonds.

While this pattern works for most elements, nature occasionally surprises us. Some atoms achieve extra stability by shifting an electron from a lower orbital to a higher one to create a half-filled or fully filled subshell. The transition metals chromium and copper are the classic examples here. If you followed the standard rules for chromium, you would expect four electrons in the three d orbital and two in the four s orbital. Instead, chromium moves one of those s electrons into the d orbital. This leaves the four s orbital half-full and the three d orbital half-full, which is a more stable, lower-energy state for the atom. Copper does something similar, opting to fully fill its d orbital rather than leaving it with nine electrons.

Learning to write these configurations allows you to map out the geography of an atom. It turns a list of elements into a predictable map of behavior. When you know how many electrons occupy the outer shell and which orbitals they inhabit, you can predict how an atom will behave long before you ever see it react in a laboratory. These patterns are not just abstract rules written in a textbook; they are the physical reality that dictates the structure of everything from the air we breathe to the metals in our electronics.

Now that we understand how electrons are arranged within their shells and subshells, we can begin to see why the periodic table is shaped the way it is. The recurring patterns in these configurations are the direct cause of the chemical trends we observe across the rows and down the columns of the table. In our next lesson, we will explore how these electronic structures influence the size, energy, and reactivity of atoms as we move across the periodic table.

## Key ideas
- The Aufbau principle dictates that electrons fill the lowest energy orbitals first, while the Pauli exclusion principle limits each orbital to two electrons with opposite spins.
- Hund’s rule states that electrons will fill degenerate orbitals singly before pairing up to maintain the lowest possible energy state.
- Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost shell, and they serve as the primary drivers of chemical reactivity and bonding behavior.
- Elements like chromium and copper exhibit exceptions to standard filling rules because half-filled or fully filled d orbitals provide additional stability to the atom.
