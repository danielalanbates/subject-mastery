# Intermolecular Forces and Liquids

Have you ever watched a water strider skitter across the surface of a pond without sinking, or wondered why a drop of water beads up on a waxed car hood instead of spreading out flat? These common sights reveal a world of invisible connections occurring between individual molecules. In our last lesson, we explored the kinetic molecular theory, which describes gases as collections of particles moving rapidly and independently in empty space. However, when those particles lose enough energy, they slow down and begin to feel the pull of their neighbors. This pull is what keeps a liquid together, and it is the subject of our lesson today.

We call these attractive forces between molecules intermolecular forces. It is important to distinguish these from the strong intramolecular bonds, like covalent or ionic bonds, that hold a single molecule together. Intermolecular forces are generally much weaker, but they are strong enough to dictate whether a substance is a gas, a liquid, or a solid at a given temperature.

The weakest of these forces are dispersion forces, sometimes called London dispersion forces. These occur in all molecules, even nonpolar ones. At any given moment, the electrons in an atom or molecule might shift to one side, creating a temporary, fleeting dipole where one end becomes slightly negative and the other slightly positive. This tiny charge imbalance can induce a similar shift in a neighboring molecule, leading to a momentary attraction. While these forces are weak, they grow stronger as molecules get larger and have more electrons that can move around.

Next, we have dipole-dipole attractions. These occur in polar molecules, which have a permanent separation of charge due to differences in electronegativity. Because these molecules have a positive end and a negative end, they naturally orient themselves to align opposite charges, much like tiny magnets. This permanent attraction makes the molecules stick together more firmly than those relying only on temporary dispersion forces.

The strongest of these common interactions is hydrogen bonding. This is a special, powerful type of dipole-dipole attraction that occurs specifically when a hydrogen atom is covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom, namely nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine. Because these atoms pull electrons so strongly, the hydrogen atom is left with a significant partial positive charge, allowing it to be strongly attracted to the lone pair of electrons on a neighboring molecule. This is why water, despite its small size, remains a liquid at room temperature and possesses such unique properties.

These forces directly explain the physical characteristics of liquids. Consider boiling point. The stronger the intermolecular forces, the more energy is required to pull the molecules away from each other to enter the gas phase. Therefore, liquids with strong hydrogen bonding, like water, have much higher boiling points than similar-sized nonpolar molecules. Viscosity, which is a liquid's resistance to flowing, is also determined by these forces. Think of honey or motor oil. Their molecules are large and have complex intermolecular attractions, creating a tangled, drag-filled environment that makes them pour slowly.

Surface tension is another fascinating result of these forces. Molecules at the surface of a liquid are pulled inward by their neighbors below and to the sides, but they have no neighbors above them to pull back. This creates a sort of skin, or tension, that resists being broken. Finally, we look at vapor pressure, which is the pressure exerted by gas molecules that have escaped from the surface of a liquid. If the intermolecular forces are strong, fewer molecules have enough energy to break free, resulting in a lower vapor pressure. As temperature increases, more molecules gain the kinetic energy necessary to overcome these attractions, causing the vapor pressure to rise.

To see this in action, compare water and acetone. Water has strong hydrogen bonds, leading to a high boiling point and low vapor pressure. Acetone, while polar, lacks the ability to form hydrogen bonds, so it evaporates quickly and has a much higher vapor pressure. Understanding how these forces balance against the kinetic energy of molecules provides the foundation for predicting how substances will behave as they transition between physical states. As we move forward, we will see how these same forces determine the structure of solids and the specific energy requirements for phase changes.

## Key ideas

- Intermolecular forces are the attractive forces between molecules, which are distinct from the stronger covalent or ionic bonds that hold individual molecules together.
- Dispersion forces are temporary, weak attractions present in all molecules, while dipole-dipole attractions and hydrogen bonding are stronger interactions caused by permanent charge imbalances.
- Stronger intermolecular forces lead to higher boiling points, higher viscosity, higher surface tension, and lower vapor pressure for a liquid.
- The physical state of a substance is a result of the competition between the kinetic energy of its particles and the strength of the intermolecular forces holding them together.
