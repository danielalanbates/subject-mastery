# Large Language Models

Imagine you are playing a game where you must guess the next word in a sentence. If I say, the cat sat on the, you would almost certainly choose mat. You rely on your knowledge of the world, grammar, and common phrases to make that prediction. Large language models, or artificial intelligence systems designed to process and generate human language, operate on a similar principle. They are not thinking in the way a human does, nor are they consulting a dictionary of facts. Instead, they are sophisticated statistical engines that excel at predicting the most likely next piece of text based on the patterns they have observed during their training.

Before a model can process text, it must convert raw words into numerical representations. It breaks text into smaller units called tokens, which might be whole words, parts of words, or even individual characters. A common method for this is byte pair encoding, which iteratively merges the most frequent pairs of characters into single tokens. This allows the model to handle a vast vocabulary efficiently. Once tokenized, these units are processed by an architecture known as the transformer. The core innovation of the transformer is the self-attention mechanism. This allows the model to weigh the importance of different words in a sequence simultaneously, regardless of how far apart they are. For example, in a long sentence, the model can relate a pronoun back to a specific noun mentioned paragraphs earlier by calculating the strength of the connection between those tokens.

To build a large language model, engineers feed the system vast amounts of text gathered from the internet. The performance of these models is governed by scaling laws, which describe how increasing the number of parameters, the size of the training data, and the amount of computational power leads to more capable systems. During training, the model adjusts its internal parameters to minimize prediction error. By the end, it has developed a complex mathematical map of how tokens relate to one another. Most chat assistants then go through a second stage of training on examples of helpful answers and on human ratings of their replies, which shapes them into assistants that follow instructions.

When you ask a model a question, it treats your prompt as the start of a sequence. It calculates the probability of every token in its vocabulary appearing next. It chooses a token, adds it to your prompt, and repeats the process. Because of this architecture, these models exhibit impressive few-shot or zero-shot learning capabilities. This means that if you provide a well-crafted prompt with one or two examples of a task, the model can often perform that new task correctly without any additional training.

However, these models lack a grounding in reality. They do not understand the physical laws of the universe. They only know that certain tokens frequently appear near others. Because they predict based on patterns rather than truth, they can produce hallucinations. A hallucination occurs when the model confidently states a fact that is false, often because it has encountered a pattern that looks like a factual statement but lacks a basis in actual evidence.

As of the mid-twenty-twenties, these models are powerful tools for productivity. Yet, their capabilities have boundaries. Researchers disagree about how far they genuinely reason or understand cause and effect, and they cannot reliably verify their own output. They are mirrors of the data they were fed. Because they are trained on human-generated text, they inevitably absorb the prejudices present in those sources. If the training data contains biased viewpoints, the model will likely replicate those patterns. Understanding how these models generate text is the first step toward recognizing that their output is not a neutral reflection of reality, but a reflection of the data provided to them.

## Key ideas

- Large language models use the transformer architecture and self-attention mechanisms to weigh relationships between distant tokens in a sequence.
- Raw text is converted into numerical tokens through processes like byte pair encoding, which allows the model to process language efficiently.
- Scaling laws demonstrate that increasing model size, data volume, and computational resources consistently improves the performance and capabilities of these systems.
- Through few-shot and zero-shot learning, models can perform new tasks by following instructions in a prompt without needing explicit retraining.
- Because these systems rely on statistical patterns rather than factual truth, they can produce convincing but false information known as hallucinations.
