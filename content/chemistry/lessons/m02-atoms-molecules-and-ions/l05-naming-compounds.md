# Naming Compounds

Imagine you are walking through a laboratory, and you see hundreds of jars filled with white powders. Each one looks identical, but one might be table salt, while another could be a substance used for etching glass. Chemistry relies on a universal language to distinguish these substances. This ensures that when a chemist writes a formula, everyone around the world knows exactly what is in the container. In our last lesson, we explored how atoms combine to form ionic and molecular compounds. Today, we learn the systematic rules for naming those compounds so we can communicate our findings with precision.

Naming ionic compounds is our first task. We start with the cation, which is the positively charged ion, and then name the anion, the negatively charged ion. For a simple ionic compound like sodium chloride, we use the name of the metal, sodium, followed by the name of the nonmetal with its ending changed to the suffix ide, giving us chloride. However, some transition metals can exist with different charges. Think of iron, which can form an ion with a charge of positive two or positive three. To clarify which one we mean, we place a Roman numeral in parentheses right after the metal name. Iron with a charge of positive two is iron two, and iron with a charge of positive three is iron three.

Many compounds also contain polyatomic ions, which are groups of atoms bonded together that carry an overall charge. You might recognize names like sulfate or nitrate. While many polyatomic ions end in ate or ite, some common ones, such as hydroxide and cyanide, end in ide. We treat these ions as a single unit when naming. For example, the compound made of calcium and the sulfate ion is simply called calcium sulfate. Because the charge of the calcium ion is positive two and the sulfate ion is negative two, they combine in a one to one ratio. If we have a transition metal with a polyatomic ion, such as iron three sulfate, we still use the Roman numeral to indicate the charge of the metal.

Molecular compounds follow a different set of rules because they consist of nonmetals sharing electrons. Since the same two elements can combine in different ratios, we use Greek prefixes to indicate how many atoms of each element are present. Prefixes such as mono, di, tri, tetra, penta, hexa, hepta, octa, nona, and deca allow us to specify the count. For instance, carbon and oxygen can form carbon monoxide, where the prefix mono indicates one oxygen atom, or carbon dioxide, where the prefix di indicates two oxygen atoms. The second element always ends in the suffix ide. If there is only one atom of the first element, we usually omit the prefix mono. Sulfur trioxide, for example, tells us there is one sulfur atom and three oxygen atoms.

Acids represent a special class of compounds that release hydrogen ions when dissolved in water. Naming them depends on the anion they contain. If the anion name ends in ide, like chloride, we use the prefix hydro and change the suffix to ic, resulting in hydrochloric acid. This hydro prefix is reserved exclusively for these binary acids. If the anion ends in ate, like nitrate, we simply change the ending to ic and add the word acid, calling it nitric acid. If the anion ends in ite, like nitrite, we change the ending to ous, giving us nitrous acid. This system allows us to identify the components of an acid just by listening to its name.

Sometimes, ionic compounds trap water molecules within their crystal structure. These are called hydrates. We name the ionic part normally, then add the word hydrate with a Greek prefix to indicate the number of water molecules. For example, copper two sulfate pentahydrate contains five water molecules for every unit of copper two sulfate.

Let us practice with a worked example. Consider the compound iron three oxide. We know it contains iron with a charge of positive three and oxygen with a charge of negative two. To balance the total positive and negative charges, we need two iron atoms and three oxygen atoms. The formula is two iron atoms and three oxygen atoms. The name tells us exactly how to write the formula, and the formula tells us exactly how to name the substance. By mastering these conventions, we turn a collection of symbols into a clear, spoken language that defines the chemical world. These naming conventions provide the foundation we need to quantify substances in the lab. Once we can name a compound and write its formula correctly, we are ready to determine its mass.

## Key ideas

- Ionic compounds are named by stating the metal cation first, followed by the nonmetal anion with an ide suffix, using Roman numerals to specify the charge of transition metals.
- Polyatomic ions are treated as single units, and molecular compounds use Greek prefixes to explicitly state the number of atoms of each nonmetal, always ending the second element in ide.
- Acid naming follows a specific pattern based on the suffix of the anion, using the prefix hydro only for binary acids and changing the endings of oxyacids to ic or ous.
- Hydrates are named by stating the ionic compound name followed by the word hydrate with a Greek prefix to indicate the number of water molecules.
