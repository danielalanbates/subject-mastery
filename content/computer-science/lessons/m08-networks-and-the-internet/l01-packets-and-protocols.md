# Packets and Protocols

Imagine you want to send a massive encyclopedia to a friend across the country. If you tried to mail the entire set as one giant, heavy crate, it would be difficult to handle, prone to damage, and if the crate were lost, you would lose everything at once. Instead, imagine tearing the book into individual pages, placing each page into its own envelope, and numbering them. You could drop these envelopes into a mailbox one by one. They might take different routes through the postal system, but they would all arrive at your friend’s house, where they could be reassembled in the correct order. This is exactly how the internet moves information.

The internet is not a single, solid wire stretching across the globe. It is a vast, decentralized collection of interconnected networks. To manage this complexity, the internet breaks large files, such as images or videos, into small chunks of data called packets. Each packet contains a piece of the original file, along with metadata—or information about the data—that tells the receiving computer where the packet belongs and how to reconstruct the final message.

For this system to function, every device must speak the same language. This is where protocols come in. A protocol is simply a set of agreed-upon rules that govern how data is formatted and exchanged. To manage this, the internet uses the transmission control protocol and internet protocol suite, often organized into four distinct layers. At the top is the application layer, which handles high-level data like hypertext transfer protocol for websites. Below that is the transport layer, using protocols like transmission control protocol or user datagram protocol to manage how data flows. Next is the internet layer, which handles routing, and finally the link layer, which manages the physical transmission of data. This suite is often compared to the open systems interconnection model, which uses seven layers, but the four-layer model is the standard for the internet.

This architecture relies on a process called encapsulation. As your data moves down the stack from the application layer to the link layer, each protocol adds its own header, and sometimes a trailer, to the data. Think of this like putting a letter in an envelope, then putting that envelope inside a larger shipping box, and finally placing that box on a shipping pallet. Each layer wraps the data in its own set of instructions. When the receiving device gets the packet, it strips away these headers in reverse order, moving from the link layer back up to the application layer, until the original data is revealed.

When we discuss the quality of these connections, we often look at two distinct metrics: bandwidth and latency. Bandwidth is the capacity of a link to move data. Think of it like the width of a pipe; a wider pipe allows more water to flow through per second. High bandwidth means you can download a high-definition movie in seconds rather than hours. Latency, on the other hand, is the time it takes for a single packet to travel from the source to the destination. If bandwidth is the width of the pipe, latency is the time it takes for a single drop of water to travel from one end to the other. If you measure the time it takes for a packet to go to the destination and come back, that is called round trip time. Even with a very wide pipe, if the distance is vast or the signal has to pass through many relay points, the delay can be noticeable.

Latency is particularly important for real-time interactions, like a video call or an online game. You might have massive bandwidth, but if your latency is high, your video feed will stutter. Achieving a fast, reliable internet experience requires balancing both of these factors. We rely on these layers of protocols to hide the underlying complexity, allowing us to send a message across the world with the click of a button. Once your data is broken into these packets and sent through the network, the next challenge is ensuring each one finds its specific destination among billions of devices.

## Key ideas

- Data is transmitted across the internet in small, manageable units called packets, which are reassembled by the receiving device.
- Protocols are organized into a stack of layers, where each layer adds its own header to the data through a process called encapsulation.
- The transmission control protocol and internet protocol suite typically consists of four layers: application, transport, internet, and link.
- Bandwidth is the total capacity of a connection to move data over time, while latency is the one-way delay for a packet to reach its destination.
