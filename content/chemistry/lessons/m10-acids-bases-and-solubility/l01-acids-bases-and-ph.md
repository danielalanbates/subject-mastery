# Acids, Bases and pH

Imagine you are standing in a kitchen, holding a lemon in one hand and a box of baking soda in the other. If you taste the lemon, your tongue immediately registers a sharp, biting sourness. If you were to touch a paste made of water and baking soda, it would feel slippery, almost like soap. These sensory experiences are our first clues into a fundamental chemical divide. For a long time, chemists categorized substances based on these properties, but today we rely on the Bronsted-Lowry definition. This framework defines an acid as a substance that donates a hydrogen ion, which is simply a proton, to another substance. Conversely, a base is a substance that accepts that proton.

Think of this as a dance of particles. When you dissolve hydrogen chloride gas in water, the hydrogen chloride molecule gives away its hydrogen ion to a water molecule. The hydrogen chloride becomes a chloride ion, and the water molecule becomes a hydronium ion, which is written as H three O with a charge of positive one. Because the hydrogen chloride lost its proton, it acts as an acid. Because the water molecule accepted the proton, it acts as a base. This relationship forms what we call a conjugate acid-base pair. After the reaction, the chloride ion is the conjugate base of the original acid, and the hydronium ion is the conjugate acid of the water base. Every time a proton moves, it leaves behind a partner that could, in theory, accept that proton back.

Water is particularly special in this regard because it is amphoteric, meaning it can act as either an acid or a base depending on the situation. In pure water, a tiny fraction of molecules collide and trade protons with one another. One water molecule grabs a proton from its neighbor. This creates a hydronium ion and a hydroxide ion, which is O H with a charge of negative one. We call this process the self-ionization of water. At twenty-five degrees Celsius, the concentration of these ions is exactly one point zero times ten to the negative seventh moles per liter for each. Because this is a dynamic equilibrium, this balance remains constant unless we add something else to the solution.

To manage these tiny numbers, chemists use the pH scale. The p in pH stands for a mathematical operation called a negative logarithm. A logarithm simply asks a question about powers of ten. If we say the logarithm of one hundred is two, we mean that ten raised to the power of two equals one hundred. Because the concentrations of ions in water are so small, using a logarithm allows us to turn a tiny decimal into a clean, manageable number, like seven. The pH is defined as the negative logarithm of the hydronium ion concentration. If the concentration is one times ten to the negative seventh, the pH is seven, which we call neutral. Because this is a logarithmic scale, each unit change in pH corresponds to a tenfold change in the hydronium ion concentration.

Calculating this is straightforward. If you have a solution with a hydronium ion concentration of one times ten to the negative three moles per liter, you take the exponent, which is negative three, and multiply it by negative one to get a pH of three. A lower pH means a higher concentration of protons, which indicates a more acidic solution. A higher pH means a lower concentration of protons, indicating a more basic solution. We also use the p O H scale, which focuses on the hydroxide ion concentration. Conveniently, at twenty-five degrees Celsius, the pH plus the p O H always equals fourteen. This means if you know one, you can easily find the other by subtracting your value from fourteen.

Understanding these scales is vital because they dictate how chemicals behave in everything from our own blood plasma to the soil in a garden. Whether a substance is a strong acid that gives up its protons readily or a weak acid that holds onto them tightly changes how that substance reacts in a mixture. In our next lesson, we will explore the specific behaviors of strong and weak acids and bases.

## Key ideas

- A Bronsted-Lowry acid is a proton donor, while a Bronsted-Lowry base is a proton acceptor.
- Conjugate acid-base pairs are formed when an acid loses a proton to become its conjugate base, or a base gains a proton to become its conjugate acid.
- Pure water undergoes self-ionization to produce equal, tiny amounts of hydronium and hydroxide ions.
- The pH scale uses negative logarithms to represent hydronium ion concentration, where each unit change represents a tenfold change in concentration.
- At twenty-five degrees Celsius, the sum of the pH and the p O H of an aqueous solution is always equal to fourteen.
