# Potential Energy and Storage

Imagine holding a heavy boulder at the edge of a cliff, perfectly still in your hands. It has no motion, so its kinetic energy is zero, yet it possesses a hidden capacity to do dramatic work if you let it go. In our last lesson, we saw how work changes the kinetic energy of an object through motion. Today, we turn to the other side of the mechanical ledger, exploring how energy can be stored away in a system, waiting to be released. This hidden account is called potential energy, and it reveals how nature keeps books on forces acting across space.

To understand how energy gets stored in a gravitational field, picture lifting that same boulder from the ground onto a high shelf. As you hoist it upward, you exert an upward force over a vertical distance, performing positive work against the constant pull of gravity. That work does not vanish. Instead, it is tucked away into the Earth object system as gravitational potential energy. Potential energy is a scalar quantity, meaning it has a magnitude but no direction, and it can be positive or negative depending on where you place your reference point.

The zero of gravitational potential energy is entirely arbitrary. You can set it at ground level, at sea level, or even infinitely far away. Choosing a different zero level changes the numerical value and the sign of the potential energy, but it never changes the physics of how much work is done during a movement. The amount of energy stored depends on specific factors. These factors are the mass of the object, the strength of the local gravitational field, which we take as nine point eight meters per second squared near the surface of our planet, and the vertical height above your chosen reference point.

Let us walk through a step by step example. Suppose a worker hoists an iron beam with a mass of fifty kilograms up to a scaffolding platform sitting ten meters above the ground. To find out how much gravitational potential energy the beam has gained, we take its mass of fifty kilograms and multiply it by nine point eight meters per second squared, which gives the downward gravitational force, or weight, of four hundred ninety newtons. Next, we multiply that four hundred ninety newtons by the vertical height of ten meters. The resulting stored energy is four thousand nine hundred joules.

Gravity is not the only way nature stores energy. Think about pulling back the string of an archery bow, compressing a metal coil in a mattress, or stretching a rubber band. When you deform an elastic material, you must push against internal restoring forces that resist being stretched or squished. The work you do in distorting the material is stored as elastic potential energy. For an ideal spring, the force required to stretch or compress it is directly proportional to the displacement from its resting length. The spring constant tells us how stiff the spring is in newtons per meter, meaning the rate of energy storage grows steeper the further you go.

Because the force grows steadily from zero as you pull, the average force you exert over a final displacement is one half of the maximum force. Multiplying that average force by the displacement gives the formula for elastic potential energy. The stored energy equals one half multiplied by the spring constant and multiplied by the square of the displacement. Imagine a stiff spring with a spring constant of one hundred newtons per meter. If you pull it outward by a displacement of two meters, you find the stored energy by multiplying one half by one hundred and by four square meters, yielding two hundred joules.

Both gravitational and elastic potential energy are tied to conservative forces. A conservative force is one where the work done moving an object between two points depends only on the initial and final positions, and never on the winding path taken in between. If you slide a book up a ramp or lift it straight up to the same height, gravity performs the exact same amount of net work. Friction, by contrast, is a nonconservative force because a longer path wastes more energy as heat.

Both forms of potential energy share a vital trait: they belong to the system as a whole rather than to any single isolated object. The gravitational energy of our boulder depends on its interaction with the entire Earth, just as elastic energy depends on the internal atomic bonds of the deformed spring. Recognizing how these fields and materials store energy sets the stage for understanding how total mechanical energy remains constant when conservative forces are the only players on the field.

## Key ideas
- Potential energy represents stored energy within a system due to relative positions or configurations interacting through conservative forces, and it is a scalar quantity.
- Gravitational potential energy is calculated as mass times nine point eight meters per second squared times height, and its reference zero level is entirely arbitrary.
- Elastic potential energy is calculated as one half times the spring constant times the displacement squared, accounting for the linearly increasing restoring force.
- Conservative forces perform work that depends solely on initial and final positions rather than the specific path taken, unlike nonconservative forces like friction.
- Both gravitational and elastic storage mechanisms belong to the system as a whole rather than to any single isolated object.
