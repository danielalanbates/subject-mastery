# Zwingli and Reformed Beginnings

Imagine walking into a church in early sixteenth-century Switzerland. You would expect to see elaborate altars, statues of saints, and perhaps a priest performing the Mass in Latin. But in the city of Zurich, a man named Huldrych Zwingli began to change everything. While Martin Luther was shaking the foundations of the church in Germany, Zwingli was leading a parallel reformation in the Swiss cantons. His movement was not just a copy of Luther’s; it had its own distinct heartbeat, focused on a radical return to the simplicity of the New Testament.

Zwingli was a priest and a humanist scholar, deeply influenced by the Greek New Testament published by Erasmus. When he became the pastor at the Great Minster church in Zurich in 1519, he did something startling: he began preaching through the Gospel of Matthew verse by verse. Instead of following the traditional church calendar or reciting established liturgy, he insisted that the Bible alone should dictate the life of the church. This principle—that whatever is not explicitly commanded in Scripture should be removed—became a hallmark of the Reformed tradition.

This approach led to significant changes in Zurich. Zwingli and his followers removed images, organs, and relics from churches, believing they distracted from the worship of God. They emphasized the preaching of the Word as the central act of the service. Unlike the Lutheran reformation, which often kept older rituals if they did not directly contradict the Bible, Zwingli’s reform was more iconoclastic, meaning he sought to clear away anything that might lead to outward idolatry. He wanted the faith to be purely intellectual and spiritual, centered entirely on the sovereign authority of God.

As the Reformation spread, leaders realized they needed to see if their movements could unite against the Roman Catholic Church. In 1529, the German prince Philip of Hesse invited Zwingli and Luther to meet at the Marburg Castle to find common ground. They agreed on fourteen out of fifteen major points of theology, including the nature of human sin and the necessity of faith. However, they hit a wall on the fifteenth point: the meaning of the Lord’s Supper.

Luther held to the view that Christ was truly present "in, with, and under" the bread and wine. He insisted on the literal words of Jesus at the Last Supper, given in the King James Version at Matthew 26:26 as "This is my body." Zwingli, however, argued that the word "is" should be understood as "signifies." For Zwingli, the bread and wine were symbols—a memorial of Christ’s sacrifice. He believed that because Christ had ascended to heaven, his physical body could not be in two places at once. The two reformers argued fiercely at Marburg. Luther famously scribbled the words of Jesus on the table, refusing to budge. The meeting ended without an agreement, and the paths of the Lutheran and Reformed traditions permanently diverged.

The Reformed tradition that emerged from Zurich emphasized the absolute sovereignty of God in all things. If God is truly the ruler of the universe, they reasoned, then human effort plays no part in salvation. This led to a high view of divine providence and a strong emphasis on the moral life of the believer. While the Lutheran movement looked to the German princes for protection, the Reformed movement often thrived in city-states where the church and the city council worked together to reshape society according to biblical law.

Zwingli’s life was cut short by the very religious conflicts he helped ignite. In 1531, war broke out between the Protestant and Catholic cantons of Switzerland. Zwingli accompanied the Zurich troops as a chaplain and was killed on the battlefield. His death was a massive blow to the movement, but his ideas were already taking root. His insistence on the authority of Scripture and his rejection of traditional rituals provided a framework that would soon be refined and expanded by a younger lawyer from France.

The Reformed tradition would not remain confined to the mountains of Switzerland. It was destined to find a new center, a city that would become the intellectual heart of the movement. As Zwingli’s work established the foundations in Zurich, the focus of the Reformation shifted westward toward the city of Geneva, where a new leader was about to emerge and codify this theology for the world.

## Key ideas

- Huldrych Zwingli initiated the Reformation in Zurich by emphasizing the preaching of the Bible as the sole authority for church life and removing elements not explicitly commanded in Scripture.
- The Reformed tradition is characterized by a strong emphasis on the absolute sovereignty of God and a symbolic view of the Lord's Supper.
- The Marburg Colloquy of 1529 revealed a fundamental divide between Luther and Zwingli, as they could not agree on whether the bread and wine were the actual body and blood of Christ or signs of his presence.
- Zwingli’s death in the Swiss religious wars left a vacuum that allowed the Reformed movement to transition from a local Swiss phenomenon to an international theological force centered in Geneva.
