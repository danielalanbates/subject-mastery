# Reliable Delivery with TCP

Imagine you are sending a long, detailed letter to a friend, but the only way to mail it is by tearing each page into tiny scraps and dropping them into a massive, chaotic wind tunnel. Some scraps might arrive at your friend's house in the wrong order, some might flip upside down, and quite a few might simply blow away and be lost forever. If your friend receives a jumbled pile of paper, they cannot read your message. They need a system to track every scrap, request the ones that went missing, and assemble the rest in the correct sequence. This is the exact challenge the internet faces every time you load a webpage, and it is the problem solved by the Transmission Control Protocol, or TCP.

In our previous lesson, we learned how the Internet Protocol acts like a postal service. It puts an address on a packet and routes it across the globe. However, that protocol is what we call connectionless. It simply sends the packet off and hopes for the best. It does not check if the destination actually received it. If a router is overwhelmed and drops a packet, the Internet Protocol does not care. That is where the Transmission Control Protocol steps in. It sits on top of the Internet Protocol to provide a layer of reliability, ensuring that data arrives exactly as it was sent.

Before any data is actually exchanged, the Transmission Control Protocol establishes a formal connection through a process called the three-way handshake. First, the sender sends a synchronization packet to the receiver to initiate the request. The receiver then responds with a synchronization acknowledgement packet, which confirms the request and signals that the receiver is ready. Finally, the sender sends an acknowledgement packet back to the receiver. Once this three-part exchange is complete, the two computers are synchronized and ready to begin the reliable transfer of data.

The Transmission Control Protocol achieves reliability by assigning a unique sequence number to every single byte of data in a transmission. Think of these as page numbers on the scraps of your letter. When your computer sends a stream of data, it breaks it into segments and labels each one. When the receiving computer gets these segments, it looks at the numbers. If it receives segment number five but skips straight to segment number seven, it knows immediately that segment number six is missing.

Once the receiver notices a gap, it sends an acknowledgement back to the sender. This is essentially a message that says, "I received everything up to segment five, but I am still waiting for six." If the sender does not receive an acknowledgement within a specific window of time, it assumes the packet was lost in the chaotic wind tunnel of the internet and automatically sends a fresh copy.

The receiver also uses those sequence numbers to fix the jumbled order of arrival. As segments arrive, the receiver places them in a buffer. It delivers data to your web browser in the correct, sequential order as soon as it is available. If a segment arrives out of order, the receiver holds it in memory until the missing pieces arrive, ensuring that your data stream remains coherent rather than a fragmented mess.

However, this high level of reliability comes with a cost. The constant back-and-forth communication, the tracking of sequence numbers, and the waiting for missing packets take time. Sometimes, speed is far more important than perfection. If you are participating in a live video call or playing a fast-paced online game, you do not want your computer to pause the stream just to wait for a lost packet from three seconds ago. By the time that old packet arrived, it would be useless anyway.

For these scenarios, we use the User Datagram Protocol, or UDP. This protocol is the opposite of the Transmission Control Protocol. It is a fire-and-forget system. It does not track sequence numbers, it does not ask for missing data to be resent, and it does not wait for acknowledgements. It simply sends the data as fast as possible. If a few packets are lost during a video call, you might see a momentary glitch or a slight blur in the image, but the conversation continues in real time. Choosing between these protocols is a classic trade-off in computer science: we balance the need for perfect data integrity against the need for immediate, low-latency performance.

## Key ideas

- The Transmission Control Protocol establishes a connection using a three-way handshake before data transfer begins.
- The Transmission Control Protocol ensures reliability by assigning sequence numbers to data packets so the receiver can detect losses and reorder segments that arrive out of sequence.
- The sender tracks which packets have been acknowledged and automatically retransmits any data that does not receive a confirmation within a set time frame.
- The User Datagram Protocol offers a faster alternative for time-sensitive applications where skipping lost data is preferable to the delay caused by retransmission.
