# Integration by Partial Fractions

Imagine you are trying to add two fractions with different denominators, say one over the quantity x minus one, and one over the quantity x plus two. To combine them into a single fraction, you find a common denominator, multiply out the numerator, and add the terms. You end up with a single rational function where the degree of the polynomial in the numerator is lower than the degree in the polynomial in the denominator. Now, suppose someone hands you that final combined fraction and asks you to find its antiderivative. Working backward from that single, complicated fraction directly can feel nearly impossible, even after exploring trigonometric integrals and substitutions. Today, we learn how to reverse that fraction addition. This technique is called integration by partial fractions, and it lets us take a complicated rational function, break it down into simpler pieces, and integrate each piece with ease.

To understand why this works, think about the building blocks of rational functions. A rational function is a fraction where both the numerator and the denominator are polynomials. If the degree of the numerator is greater than or equal to the degree of the denominator, our first step is always to use polynomial long division to simplify the expression into a polynomial plus a proper fraction. A proper fraction is simply one where the degree of the numerator is strictly less than the degree of the denominator. Once we have a proper fraction, we look closely at the denominator. We factor that denominator completely into linear factors, which look like a constant times x plus another constant, and irreducible quadratic factors, which cannot be factored further using real numbers.

The core idea of partial fractions is that any proper rational function can be written as a sum of simpler fractions whose denominators are the individual factors of our original denominator. If our denominator has distinct linear factors, we write an unknown constant over each factor. For instance, if the denominator factors into the product of the quantity x minus one and the quantity x plus two, we set our rational function equal to a constant, say letter b, over the quantity x minus one, plus another constant, say letter c, over the quantity x plus two. Our goal is to find those unknown constants.

Let us walk through a complete example step by step. Suppose we want to evaluate the indefinite integral of a rational function. In the numerator, we have the number five. In the denominator, we have x squared plus x minus two, with respect to x. First, we factor that denominator. The quadratic expression factors into the product of the quantity x minus one and the quantity x plus two. Since the numerator is just the number five, which has a degree of zero, and our denominator has a degree of two, this is a proper fraction ready for decomposition. We set five over the product of the quantity x minus one and the quantity x plus two equal to letter b over the quantity x minus one plus letter c over the quantity x plus two.

To find the values of letter b and letter c, we clear the denominators by multiplying both sides of the equation by the original factored denominator, the product of the quantity x minus one and the quantity x plus two. On the left side, we are left simply with the number five. On the right side, letter b is multiplied by the quantity x plus two, and letter c is multiplied by the quantity x minus one. Because this equation must hold true for any value of x, we can choose convenient values of x to solve for our constants. If we choose x to be equal to one, the term with letter c disappears. We get five equals letter b times the quantity one plus two, which means five equals three times letter b, so letter b equals five thirds. Next, if we choose x to be equal to negative two, the term with letter b disappears. We get five equals letter c times the quantity negative two minus one, which means five equals negative three times letter c, so letter c equals negative five thirds.

Now our single difficult integral splits into two much simpler integrals. We are integrating the quantity five thirds over the quantity x minus one, plus negative five thirds over the quantity x plus two, with respect to x. Both of these terms integrate directly using our logarithmic rules. The antiderivative of five thirds over the quantity x minus one is five thirds times the natural logarithm of the absolute value of the quantity x minus one. The antiderivative of negative five thirds over the quantity x plus two is negative five thirds times the natural logarithm of the absolute value of the quantity x plus two. We add our constant of integration, letter k, at the end. We can check our work by differentiating this final expression, using the chain rule and logarithm rules, which will bring us right back to our original integrand of five over the quantity x squared plus x minus two.

As we move forward, we will see how these algebraic tools and integration techniques prepare us for approximating definite integrals when exact antiderivatives remain stubbornly out of reach.

## Key ideas
- Integration by partial fractions decomposes complex proper rational functions into simpler fractional building blocks that are easy to integrate.
- The denominator of the rational function must be factored completely into linear and irreducible quadratic factors before decomposition begins.
- Unknown constants in the numerators of the partial fractions are found by clearing denominators and substituting strategic values for the variable.
- The resulting simpler fractions integrate directly into logarithmic and sometimes inverse trigonometric terms.
