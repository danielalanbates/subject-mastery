# Paul and the Gentile Mission

Imagine standing on a dusty road in the first century, watching a man named Saul of Tarsus. He is a zealous Pharisee, a man deeply committed to the traditions of his ancestors, and a fierce opponent of the new movement following Jesus. He is convinced that this group is a dangerous deviation from the path of Israel. Yet, his life is about to undergo a transformation so radical that it will shift the trajectory of human history. While traveling to Damascus, in what is now Syria, to arrest followers of Jesus, he experiences a blinding light and a voice that asks why he is persecuting him. This encounter with the risen Christ turns the persecutor into an apostle, a man who will become the primary architect of the mission to the non-Jewish world.

This transition from the Jerusalem-centered church we discussed in our last lesson to a global movement is largely the story of Paul. After his conversion, Paul does not immediately run to the other apostles. Instead, he spends time in reflection and begins to preach in the synagogues of Damascus. He carries a revolutionary message: the God of Israel has fulfilled his promises through Jesus, and this salvation is now open to all people, regardless of their ethnic background. This was a radical proposition. For centuries, the covenant between God and his people had been marked by the sign of circumcision and adherence to the laws given to Moses.

Paul’s argument is rooted in his understanding of grace. He maintains that a person is made right with God through faith in Jesus Christ, not by performing the works of the law. If a Gentile, or non-Jew, must become a Jew first by being circumcised, then the death of Christ is rendered unnecessary. Paul travels extensively across the Roman world, establishing communities in major cities like Ephesus, in modern-day Turkey, and Corinth, in modern-day Greece. These churches are vibrant, diverse gatherings of people who have left behind pagan idols to worship the one God of Israel.

However, this mission creates immense tension. Some Jewish Christians, often called Judaizers, argue that Gentiles must adopt the full weight of the Mosaic Law to be part of the covenant. They see the law as the essential boundary marker of God's people. To settle this, a pivotal meeting takes place in Jerusalem around 48 or 49 CE. This is the Council of Jerusalem, where apostles and elders gather to debate the question. They listen to Peter, who reminds them that God gave the Holy Spirit to the Gentiles just as he did to the Jews, and they hear Paul’s account of his mission.

The council reaches a historic compromise. They agree that Gentiles do not need to be circumcised or follow the entire law of Moses to belong to the new community. However, they ask the Gentile believers to abstain from certain practices that would be particularly offensive to their Jewish neighbors, such as eating food sacrificed to idols or consuming blood. This decision is a watershed moment. It prevents the church from becoming merely a sect within Judaism and allows it to flourish as a multi-ethnic, international faith. It acknowledges that the gospel is meant for all nations, even while it honors the deep Jewish roots of the movement.

Paul’s missionary journeys are grueling. He faces shipwrecks, imprisonment, and constant opposition from both local authorities and religious leaders who fear his influence. Yet, his letters, which later become a significant portion of the New Testament, provide a roadmap for these young churches. He writes to address their questions about how to live in a pagan society, how to handle conflicts within the body, and how to maintain their identity as people of the resurrection. His work transforms the church from a localized group into a network of communities stretching from the Levant to the heart of the Roman Empire.

As the Gentile mission expands, these new communities must grapple with how to organize themselves and express their faith in a world that is often hostile to them. They have to decide what their worship looks like when they gather, how they choose their leaders, and how they define their sacred rituals. These questions of structure and practice will become the focus of the early church as it establishes its rhythm of life and its internal order.

## Key ideas
- Paul’s conversion marks a turning point where the Jesus movement begins to transition from a primarily Jewish group into a world-spanning mission for all peoples.
- The core of Paul’s theology is that salvation is a gift of grace received through faith, which removes the requirement for Gentiles to undergo circumcision or follow the full Mosaic Law.
- The Council of Jerusalem serves as the first major legislative gathering of the church, where leaders decide that Gentile converts are not required to adopt Jewish legal practices to join the faith.
- Through his extensive travels and letters, Paul establishes a pattern for church life that emphasizes unity across ethnic lines and provides a theological foundation for the growing Christian community.
