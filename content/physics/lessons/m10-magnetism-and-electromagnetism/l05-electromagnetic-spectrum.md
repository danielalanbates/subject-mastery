# The Electromagnetic Spectrum

When you warm your hands over a campfire, you feel a gentle heat you cannot see. When you listen to your favorite radio station, invisible messages sweep right through the walls of your home. These seemingly different phenomena are actually members of one grand family. In our last lesson, we saw how changing magnetic fields generate electric fields, and how rotating coils of wire in power plants create currents that travel across the country. Today, we take the final step in classical electromagnetism by discovering that these self-sustaining electric and magnetic fields can detach entirely from their wires and travel through empty space as waves.

This family of travelling energy is known as the electromagnetic spectrum. Every wave in this spectrum shares a common birthplace in the universe, which is an accelerating electric charge. When a charge shakes back and forth, it creates a fluctuating electric field, which in turn induces a fluctuating magnetic field. This dance of fields props each other up, marching outward at a staggering speed. Electromagnetic waves are transverse waves, meaning that the electric and magnetic fields oscillate perpendicular to each other and also perpendicular to the direction in which the wave propagates. In a vacuum, this speed is a universal constant, coming in at about three hundred million meters per second, which is often called the speed of light. Because every electromagnetic wave travels at this same speed, there is a strict mathematical relationship between how often the wave oscillates and how long each wave cycle is. As the frequency of the wave increases, its wavelength must shrink proportionally.

We organize this vast spectrum by arranging the waves according to their frequencies and wavelengths. At one end of the spectrum lie radio waves, possessing the longest wavelengths and the lowest frequencies. These waves can stretch for kilometers or be as short as a few centimeters, carrying long-distance broadcasts around obstacles. Moving upward in frequency, we encounter microwaves, which operate at higher frequencies such as two point four gigahertz, carrying Wi-Fi signals and heating the water molecules in our food. Following microwaves, we find infrared radiation, which we perceive as thermal warmth. A very narrow band right in the middle of the spectrum forms the visible light that our eyes have evolved to detect, ranging from low frequency red light to high frequency violet light.

Beyond violet light lies ultraviolet radiation, carrying enough energy to cause sunburns, and further up still are X-rays, which can pass through human tissue to reveal our bones. At the absolute extreme end of the spectrum are gamma rays, produced by radioactive atomic nuclei and cosmic cataclysms. These carry the highest frequencies, the shortest wavelengths, and the most energy of all. Despite their vastly different properties and uses, from tuning into a broadcast to receiving a medical scan, every single one of these phenomena is fundamentally the same physical entity. They are oscillating electromagnetic disturbances propagating through space, differing only in their place on the energy continuum.

Let us trace a quick example to see how these properties relate. Imagine a local radio station broadcasting its signal at a frequency of one hundred megahertz, which means one hundred million cycles per second. To find the wavelength of this wave, we take the speed of light, about three hundred million meters per second, and divide it by the frequency of one hundred million per second. The result comes out to about three meters, meaning each complete wave cycle spans the length of a small room. If we compare this to a green laser pointer, whose frequency is around six hundred trillion hertz, the calculation yields a wavelength of about five hundred billionths of a meter, which is roughly the size of a tiny microbe.

Understanding how these waves travel and interact with matter opens the door to studying how they behave when they bounce off surfaces, bend through lenses, and form images.

## Key ideas
- Electromagnetic waves are transverse waves consisting of mutually inducing electric and magnetic fields that oscillate perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation.
- All electromagnetic waves travel through a vacuum at the constant speed of light, which is about three hundred million meters per second.
- The electromagnetic spectrum ranges continuously from low frequency, long wavelength radio waves and microwaves up to high frequency, short wavelength gamma rays.
- Wave frequency and wavelength are inversely proportional, meaning higher frequency waves always have shorter wavelengths.
