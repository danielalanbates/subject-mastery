# Electric Potential and Voltage

Imagine pushing a boulder up a steep hill. As you roll it higher, you are doing mechanical work against the force of gravity, storing energy in the boulder. If you let go, gravity pulls it back down, converting that stored potential energy into motion. A very similar thing happens with invisible electric charges, except the landscape is shaped by electric fields rather than gravity. In our last lesson, we explored how electric charges create invisible fields around themselves, exerting pushes and pulls on one another. Today, we look at the energy stored in those fields by asking what it takes to move a charge through them.

To understand how work moves a charge, recall that an electric field exerts a force on any charged particle placed inside it. If you have a positive charge sitting near another positive charge, they repel each other. To push them closer together, you have to apply an external force and push against that natural repulsion. Just like compressing a spring or lifting a heavy object, you are doing work. That work does not disappear. It is stored as electric potential energy within the system of charges. If you release the charges, the electric field converts that stored potential energy back into kinetic energy, sending the particles flying apart.

Now, calculating the total potential energy of a complex system of charges can get complicated, so physicists use a more convenient shortcut called electric potential. While electric potential energy belongs to a whole group of interacting charges, electric potential is a property of a single point in space within an electric field. It tells you how much potential energy a specific amount of charge would possess if you placed it at that exact location. We measure this electric potential in volts, where one volt is equal to one joule of energy per coulomb of charge.

This brings us directly to voltage, which is simply the difference in electric potential between two different points. Think of voltage as an electrical pressure or a push. Just as water naturally flows from a high point in a pipe to a low point because of a pressure difference, positive charges tend to move from a region of high electric potential to a region of low electric potential, provided there is a path for them to follow. Conversely, negative charges like electrons naturally move from low electric potential to high electric potential. The greater the voltage between two points, the harder the electric field pushes on the charges, and the more energy each charge delivers as it moves.

We can also map this landscape using surfaces where the electric potential is the same everywhere, which physicists call equipotential surfaces. Because every point on such a surface shares an identical electric potential, moving a charge along an equipotential surface requires zero net work. The electric field is always perpendicular to these equipotential surfaces, and the strength of a uniform electric field equals the voltage divided by the distance between two plates or points.

Let us walk through a concrete example to see how this works in practice. Suppose you have a battery that provides a voltage of twelve volts. That number tells you that the battery does twelve joules of work on every single coulomb of charge that passes through it. Imagine moving a small charge of two coulombs from the negative terminal to the positive terminal of this battery. To find the total work required, you multiply the charge in coulombs by the voltage in volts. Two coulombs multiplied by twelve volts gives twenty-four joules of work. That means the battery imparts twenty-four joules of electric potential energy to those charges, energy they can later use to power a device like a light bulb or a motor.

A good way to check your intuition here is to think about a waterfall. The height of the waterfall is like the voltage, representing a difference in potential energy per unit of mass or, in our case, per unit of charge. A taller waterfall gives water more gravitational potential energy to release as it crashes down, just as a higher voltage gives charges more electrical potential energy to release as they move through a circuit. Without this difference in potential, nothing moves and no energy is transferred.

Grasping how work, potential energy, and voltage intertwine is essential for understanding how electrical systems operate. In our next lesson, we will see what happens when we provide a continuous pathway for these charges to flow, examining how current moves through materials and how resistance shapes that flow.

## Key ideas
- Electric charges in an electric field possess potential energy that depends on their positions relative to one another.
- Electric potential is the potential energy per unit of charge at any given point in space, measured in volts, while voltage represents the difference in electric potential between two points.
- Positive charges naturally move from regions of high electric potential to regions of low electric potential, whereas negative charges move from low to high potential.
- Equipotential surfaces represent regions of constant electric potential where moving a charge requires zero work, and the electric field strength equals the voltage divided by the distance.
