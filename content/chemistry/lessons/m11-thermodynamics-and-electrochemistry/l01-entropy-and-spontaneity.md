# Entropy and Spontaneity

Imagine you are holding a deck of cards. If you drop the deck on the floor, the cards scatter in a chaotic mess across the room. You have never dropped a neat stack of cards only to have them land in a perfectly ordered, sequential pile. This is not because of a lack of skill; it is because the universe naturally favors disorder. In chemistry, we call this tendency toward chaos entropy. In our previous lessons, we looked at how substances dissolve and reach equilibrium. Now, we want to understand why certain chemical reactions happen on their own while others require constant energy input. This brings us to the concept of spontaneity.

A spontaneous process is one that occurs without any ongoing outside intervention. If you leave an ice cube on a warm counter, it melts. You do not need to do anything to make that happen. The heat flows from the surroundings into the ice, and the solid water turns into a liquid. Conversely, if you put a glass of water in a freezer, it will eventually turn to ice. However, that process is only spontaneous if the freezer is actively removing heat. If the power goes out, the melting becomes the spontaneous direction. Spontaneity tells us the direction a process will take, but it tells us nothing about how fast the reaction occurs. A diamond might spontaneously turn into graphite, but the process is so slow that you will never see it happen in your lifetime.

Entropy is the scientific measure of this drive toward disorder. You can think of entropy as the spreading out of energy and matter. When a solid melts into a liquid, or a liquid evaporates into a gas, the particles gain more freedom to move. They occupy more space and have more ways to arrange themselves. Because there are more possible states for the gas than for the solid, the system has higher entropy. Crucially, entropy is a state function. This means its change depends only on the initial and final states of the system, not on the path taken to get there.

The second law of thermodynamics formalizes this: in any spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe must increase. The universe is constantly moving toward a state of maximum disorder. We must also consider the surroundings. For a process occurring at a constant temperature and pressure, the entropy change of the surroundings is equal to the negative enthalpy change of the system divided by the absolute temperature in kelvins. This explains why exothermic processes, which release heat into the surroundings, often increase the total entropy of the universe.

To see this in a chemical context, consider the decomposition of a solid into gases. Take the reaction where solid calcium carbonate, with one calcium, one carbon and three oxygen atoms in each formula unit, breaks down into solid calcium oxide, with one calcium and one oxygen atom, plus carbon dioxide gas. In this reaction, we start with a highly ordered solid and end with a solid plus a gas. Because gas molecules move rapidly and occupy a much larger volume than solid particles, the entropy of the system increases significantly. This increase in disorder is a major factor that drives the reaction to proceed.

We must also respect the third law of thermodynamics, which gives us a baseline for our measurements. It states that the entropy of a perfect, pure crystal at a temperature of absolute zero is exactly zero. At absolute zero, the particles have the least energy they can have, and there is only one possible way for the atoms to be arranged. As we add heat and increase the temperature, the atoms begin to vibrate and move, which increases the entropy. This allows chemists to assign an absolute entropy value to substances at standard conditions. By comparing the entropy of the products to the entropy of the reactants, we can determine the change in entropy for any given chemical reaction.

If the change in entropy is a positive number, it means the system has become more disordered. If it is negative, the system has become more ordered. However, entropy alone does not tell the whole story. Sometimes, a reaction happens even when the system becomes more ordered, such as when a gas condenses into a liquid. This tells us that the entropy of the surroundings also plays a vital role. The entropy of the system and the entropy of the surroundings together determine the overall spontaneity of the universe. To truly predict whether a reaction will proceed, we need a way to combine these factors into a single, reliable value that accounts for both the energy changes and the disorder of the system. In our next lesson, we will introduce a powerful tool that bridges these concepts to define exactly how chemical systems reach their final state of stability.

## Key ideas

- A spontaneous process occurs naturally without continuous external energy input, though it does not indicate how quickly the process will happen.
- Entropy is a state function that measures the dispersal of energy and matter, where a higher state of disorder corresponds to higher entropy.
- The second law of thermodynamics states that the total entropy of the universe always increases during any spontaneous process.
- For a process at constant temperature and pressure, the entropy change of the surroundings is equal to the negative enthalpy change of the system divided by the temperature.
- The third law of thermodynamics establishes that a perfect crystal at absolute zero temperature has an entropy value of zero.
