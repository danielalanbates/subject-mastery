# Avignon and the Western Schism

Imagine a house divided against itself, where two or three people claim to be the sole head of the household, each issuing conflicting orders and demanding total loyalty. This is essentially what happened to the Western Church in the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. Following the period of intense papal authority that defined the High Middle Ages, the papacy entered a season of deep humiliation and fragmentation. This crisis began in 1309, when the papal court moved from Rome to the city of Avignon, located in what is now southern France.

For nearly seventy years, the popes resided in Avignon rather than the traditional seat of Saint Peter in Rome. Because this period lasted roughly the same length of time as the biblical exile of the Jews in Babylon, critics of the era dubbed it the Babylonian Captivity of the Church. During these years, the papacy became increasingly entangled with the French monarchy. Many contemporaries, especially those outside of France, worried that the Bishop of Rome had become a mere puppet for French interests. While the Avignon popes were efficient administrators, their focus on taxation and bureaucracy alienated many believers who longed for a papacy that was independent and focused on spiritual leadership.

In 1377, Pope Gregory the Eleventh finally returned the papacy to Rome. However, his death shortly afterward triggered a chaotic election. Under pressure from Roman mobs demanding an Italian pope, the cardinals elected Urban the Sixth. Soon, however, the cardinals regretted their choice, claiming they had acted under duress. They fled Rome, declared Urban’s election invalid, and chose a rival pope who returned to Avignon. This sparked the Great Western Schism. For decades, Europe was split into competing camps, with one pope in Rome and another in Avignon. Each excommunicated the other, leaving the faithful in a state of deep confusion about who held the true keys of authority.

The situation worsened in 1409 at the Council of Pisa. Hoping to resolve the deadlock, church leaders gathered to depose both rival popes and elect a new one. Instead, the existing popes refused to step down. The result was three simultaneous claimants to the papal throne. This was not merely a political embarrassment; it touched the core of medieval life. The sacraments, the legitimacy of bishops, and the spiritual standing of the entire church seemed to hang in the balance. The divisions of the previous era now seemed magnified by the failure of the central leadership to provide a unified witness.

The crisis finally reached a breaking point with the Council of Constance, which met from 1414 to 1418. This was a massive gathering of churchmen from across Europe. The council succeeded in ending the schism by securing the resignation or deposition of the rival claimants and electing a single, universally recognized pope, Martin the Fifth. However, the council did something else that would haunt the papacy for centuries. It issued a decree known as Haec Sancta, which declared that a general council of the church derives its power directly from Christ and that everyone, including the pope, is bound to obey it in matters of faith and the ending of schism. Furthermore, the council issued the decree Frequens, which mandated that general councils be held at regular intervals to prevent future schisms and ensure the ongoing reform of the church.

This raised a revolutionary question: does the ultimate authority in the church reside in the person of the pope, or in the collective body of the church represented by a council? This movement, known as Conciliarism, suggested that the church should function more like a constitutional monarchy than an absolute one. While the papacy eventually reasserted its supreme authority in later years, the trauma of the schism and the rise of Conciliarism left a permanent mark on the Western consciousness. It forced people to ask difficult questions about the nature of power, the necessity of reform, and the integrity of the clerical hierarchy.

These questions about authority and the need for reform did not vanish when the schism ended. Instead, they simmered beneath the surface, providing fertile soil for more radical voices who argued that the church’s problems were not just administrative, but theological. In these same decades, critics in England and in Bohemia had been questioning the very foundations of the medieval ecclesiastical system, and the Council of Constance itself sat in judgment on them. Their dissent would challenge the church in ways that even the Great Schism had not, leading to a profound re-examination of the role of Scripture and the individual believer.

## Key ideas

- The Avignon Papacy, or Babylonian Captivity, saw the popes reside in France for about seventy years, leading to widespread perceptions of corruption and subservience to the French crown.
- The Western Schism fractured the church by creating rival lines of popes in Rome, Avignon, and eventually Pisa, causing deep confusion regarding the spiritual legitimacy of the clergy.
- The Council of Constance ended the schism by electing a single pope and established the decrees Haec Sancta and Frequens, which promoted the theory of Conciliarism and mandated regular councils.
- The crisis of the papacy weakened the prestige of the institutional church and set the stage for later reformers to challenge the structure and theology of the late medieval religious order.
