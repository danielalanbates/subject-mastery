# The Ecumenical Movement

Imagine walking into a room filled with people who all claim to be part of the same family, yet they have spent centuries living in separate houses, barely speaking to one another. Each group has its own traditions, its own way of interpreting the family history, and even its own set of rules for how to behave at the dinner table. This was the state of global Christianity by the turn of the twentieth century. After centuries of division, ranging from the Great Schism of 1054 CE between East and West to the fracturing of the Protestant Reformation, the church found itself fragmented into thousands of denominations. Yet, as the world became more interconnected, a movement began to grow that asked a simple, piercing question: what would it look like for these families to finally sit at the same table?

The formal turning point for this desire for unity, known as the ecumenical movement, arrived in 1910 at the World Missionary Conference in Edinburgh, Scotland. Missionaries working in Africa, Asia, and the Americas realized that their efforts to share the gospel were often hindered by the bewildering array of competing denominations they imported from the West. They saw that a divided church struggled to present a unified message. Edinburgh was not a place where major theological debates were settled, but it was the place where the conversation about visible unity first became a global priority for Protestant and Anglican leaders.

This momentum was bolstered by the 1925 Life and Work conference in Stockholm and the 1927 Faith and Order conference in Lausanne. These gatherings laid the essential groundwork for the World Council of Churches, which was formally established in 1948 in Amsterdam. By this time, the world had been shattered by the horrors of two world wars. Christians realized that their divisions had left them weakened in the face of such immense human suffering. The Council sought to provide a permanent space where different traditions could engage in dialogue, common prayer, and cooperative service. It brought together a wide range of Protestant and Anglican bodies, and importantly, many of the Orthodox churches from Eastern Europe and the Middle East joined the movement as well.

The ecumenical movement distinguishes between two goals. Some groups seek organic union, which implies a single organizational structure. However, most participants aim for visible unity, where different traditions recognize each other’s baptisms and ministries, allowing for shared life while maintaining distinct identities. For the Orthodox, this often means a return to the faith and order of the early, undivided church. Oriental Orthodox churches, such as the Coptic, Armenian, and Syrian traditions, alongside the Church of the East, have been vital participants in these dialogues, offering their ancient perspectives on the nature of the church. Similarly, Anabaptist traditions like the Mennonites and Brethren have engaged in ecumenical work, often emphasizing peace, justice, and service as the primary expressions of Christian unity.

A significant shift occurred in the mid-twentieth century when the Roman Catholic Church began to engage more deeply. While the Catholic Church maintains that the Church of Christ subsists in the Catholic Church, it acknowledges that many elements of sanctification and truth are found outside its visible boundaries. This shift opened the door for new dialogues. It allowed for cooperation in areas like international development and peace-building.

We must be honest about the limitations. Deep disagreements persist regarding the nature of papal authority, the role of tradition versus scripture, and various ethical issues. Furthermore, many Pentecostal and evangelical churches have remained outside the formal structures of the World Council of Churches. These groups often prefer to pursue unity through shared mission, revival, and personal conversion rather than institutional dialogue. These tensions remind us that the ecumenical movement is a work in progress, often characterized by as much friction as it is by progress. Ultimately, the quest for unity forces every tradition to ask whether its specific identity is a genuine reflection of the gospel or an obstacle to the prayer of Jesus that his followers might be one.

## Key ideas

- The 1910 World Missionary Conference in Edinburgh, alongside the 1925 and 1927 conferences in Stockholm and Lausanne, provided the foundational structure for the modern ecumenical movement.
- The ecumenical movement generally distinguishes between organic union, which involves a single organizational structure, and visible unity, which focuses on the mutual recognition of baptisms and ministries.
- Oriental Orthodox churches, the Church of the East, and Anabaptist traditions have contributed unique perspectives to ecumenical dialogues, often emphasizing ancient liturgical heritage or a commitment to peace and service.
- While the Roman Catholic Church maintains that the Church of Christ subsists in the Catholic Church, it has engaged in significant ecumenical dialogue since the mid-twentieth century, acknowledging other Christians as separated brethren.
- Pentecostal and many evangelical traditions often prioritize unity through shared mission and spiritual revival rather than through the institutional frameworks of the World Council of Churches.
