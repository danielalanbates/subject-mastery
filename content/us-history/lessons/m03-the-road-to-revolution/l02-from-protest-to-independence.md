# From Protest to Independence

Imagine a cold night in Boston harbor, where the scent of salt air is suddenly overwhelmed by the smell of tea leaves soaking in brine. On December 16, 1773, a group of colonists disguised themselves and dumped three hundred and forty-two chests of East India Company tea into the water. This was not merely a protest against a tax; it was a defiant rejection of British authority over colonial trade. The British government, led by Prime Minister Lord North, saw this destruction of property as an intolerable challenge to the rule of law. They responded with a series of punitive measures known as the Coercive Acts, or as the colonists called them, the Intolerable Acts.

These acts went far beyond simple financial penalties. Parliament closed the port of Boston until the tea was paid for, drastically curtailed the Massachusetts government, and allowed British officials to be tried for crimes in other colonies or even back in Britain. Rather than isolating Massachusetts, these harsh measures horrified other colonies. They feared that if Britain could dismantle the charter of one colony, no colonial government was safe. This shared anxiety led to the meeting of the First Continental Congress in Philadelphia in 1774. Delegates from twelve colonies gathered to coordinate a unified response, signaling a profound shift from individual colonial protests to a collective, inter-colonial resistance movement.

As tensions reached a breaking point, the countryside transformed into an armed camp. In April 1775, British troops marched from Boston to the nearby towns of Lexington and Concord to seize hidden stores of colonial gunpowder and arrest rebel leaders. They were met by colonial militiamen on the green at Lexington. A shot rang out—no one knows who fired it first—and the American Revolution began in earnest. These early skirmishes proved that the conflict was no longer a political dispute over taxes; it had become a military confrontation. The Second Continental Congress soon convened, eventually assuming the role of a provisional government and creating the Continental Army.

For many colonists, the goal remained reconciliation with the Crown well into 1775. However, the publication of Thomas Paine’s pamphlet, Common Sense, in early 1776, fundamentally altered the public conversation. Paine wrote in plain, accessible language, arguing that it was absurd for an island to rule a continent and that monarchy was a deeply corrupt form of government. He advocated for total independence and the creation of a republic. His work spread like wildfire through taverns, workshops, and meeting houses, giving voice to the growing frustration and convincing many ordinary people that breaking away was not just possible, but necessary.

Historians have long debated the underlying causes of this rapid shift toward independence. Some scholars, often called Whig historians, emphasize the ideological struggle for liberty and the influence of Enlightenment ideas about natural rights. They view the Revolution as a principled stand against tyranny. Other historians, sometimes described as Progressive or economic historians, point toward the colonial elite’s desire to protect their own interests, trade, and land claims from imperial regulation. They argue that the Revolution was as much about domestic power as it was about British policy.

A third group of historians focuses on the social dimensions of the conflict. They argue that the Revolution was a bottom-up movement, fueled by the participation of artisans, laborers, and farmers who saw their own economic and social grievances reflected in the broader political struggle. These different interpretations are not necessarily mutually exclusive. Most modern scholars see the Revolution as a complex fusion of high-minded political theory, cold economic calculation, and the radicalization of a diverse population. The revolution was not a single event, but a series of choices made by people across the social spectrum.

By the spring of 1776, the momentum for a complete break from Britain had become unstoppable. The military clashes, the failure of diplomacy, and the persuasive power of writers like Paine had pushed the Continental Congress toward a final, momentous decision. The colonies were no longer just seeking a redress of grievances; they were moving toward the creation of a new, sovereign nation. As the delegates prepared to articulate their vision for this new country, they turned their attention to drafting a formal document that would justify their separation to the world.

## Key ideas

- The Coercive Acts transformed local colonial resistance into a unified, inter-colonial political movement by demonstrating Britain's willingness to strip away colonial self-governance.
- The skirmishes at Lexington and Concord in 1775 marked the transition from political protest to an armed struggle for control over local territory and resources.
- Thomas Paine’s Common Sense played a crucial role in shifting public opinion toward independence by using plain language to challenge the legitimacy of monarchy and imperial rule.
- Historians interpret the causes of the Revolution through various lenses, including the influence of Enlightenment political philosophy, economic motives of the colonial elite, and the widespread social unrest of the laboring classes.
