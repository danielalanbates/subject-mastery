# Symmetric and Public-Key Encryption

Imagine you want to send a secret letter to a friend, but you know that a prying neighbor might intercept it along the way. If you use a simple code, like shifting every letter of the alphabet by three positions, your neighbor will likely figure it out in minutes. To keep the message truly private, you need a method that is practically impossible to break without a specific key. In our last lesson, we discussed how attackers use social engineering to trick people into giving up their credentials. Today, we look at the technical defenses that protect data even when an attacker manages to intercept the message itself.

The first and oldest method of securing information is symmetric encryption. Think of this like a sturdy metal box with a single physical key. You place your document inside, lock it, and send the box to your friend. For your friend to read the document, they must possess an identical copy of that same key. This is why it is called symmetric; both the sender and the receiver share the exact same secret. If you both have the key, you can communicate securely. The challenge, however, is how to get that key to your friend in the first place. If you send the key through the mail, the same neighbor who intercepts your letters might also steal the key, rendering your security useless.

To solve the problem of key distribution, computer scientists developed public-key encryption, also known as asymmetric encryption. In this system, every person has two mathematically linked keys: a public key and a private key. You can think of your public key as an open padlock that you distribute to the entire world. Anyone can take one of your padlocks, place their message in a box, and lock it shut. Once that padlock is snapped closed, even the person who locked it cannot open it again. Only your unique, private key, which you keep hidden, can unlock the box. This allows anyone to send you a secure message without needing to share a secret key beforehand.

In the modern world, we rarely use just one of these methods; we combine them to secure web traffic. When you visit a secure website, your browser and the server perform a handshake. They use public-key encryption to safely exchange a temporary, symmetric key. Once both sides have this shared secret, they switch to symmetric encryption for the rest of the conversation. This approach is brilliant because it provides the security of the public-key system for the initial exchange, while taking advantage of the speed and efficiency of symmetric encryption for the large volumes of data that follow, such as images and text on a webpage.

Beyond encryption, we also use these key pairs for digital signatures. A digital signature is a way to prove that a message truly came from you and has not been altered. Imagine you have a special stamp that creates a unique, complex pattern. You use your private key to sign a digital document. Anyone who has your public key can verify that only your private key could have created that specific signature. If the message was changed by even a single bit during transit, the signature would no longer match the document, alerting the receiver that the information is untrustworthy. This provides authenticity and integrity, ensuring that the identity of the sender is verified and the content remains intact.

While these tools provide robust protection, they do not inherently tell us if the person on the other end is who they claim to be. A malicious actor could generate their own pair of keys and pretend to be a bank or a trusted service. This is why we rely on digital certificates and authorities to vouch for the ownership of public keys. By linking a real-world identity to a public key, these systems allow us to navigate the internet with confidence. Even with these powerful mathematical safeguards in place, the human element remains a significant factor, as we must trust that the underlying software and the certificate authorities themselves are acting in good faith. As we move forward, we will see that keeping data secret is only half the battle; we must also ensure that the data we store is protected from tampering and that our own access remains secure through the use of irreversible mathematical functions.

## Key ideas

- Symmetric encryption uses a single shared key for both locking and unlocking data, which requires a secure way to distribute that key to the intended recipient.
- Public-key encryption uses a matched pair of keys, where a public key encrypts data and a corresponding private key decrypts it, solving the problem of secure key exchange.
- Web traffic typically combines these methods by using public-key encryption to safely share a temporary key, which is then used for faster symmetric encryption during the session.
- Digital signatures use private keys to provide proof of origin and integrity, allowing a receiver to verify that a message was sent by a specific person and has not been altered.
