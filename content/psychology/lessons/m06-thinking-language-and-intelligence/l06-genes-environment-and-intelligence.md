# Genes, Environment and Intelligence

Imagine two identical twins who were separated at birth and raised in completely different households. One grows up in a quiet, book-filled home in a wealthy suburb, while the other is raised in a resource-poor neighborhood in a bustling city. When they meet as adults and take an intelligence test, how similar are their scores? This is the classic setup for the nature-versus-nurture debate, a question researchers have explored for decades to understand what truly shapes the human mind.

To untangle the influences of biology and experience, psychologists rely on twin and adoption studies. These studies provide strong correlational evidence. Identical twins share one hundred percent of their genes, while fraternal twins share about fifty percent. When researchers compare the intelligence test scores of identical twins raised together versus those raised apart, they find a high degree of correlation. This suggests that genetics play a significant role in individual differences in cognitive ability. Adoption studies provide further evidence; as children reach adulthood, their intelligence scores often correlate more strongly with their biological parents than with their adoptive parents.

It is vital to understand what heritability means. Heritability is a statistical estimate that describes the proportion of variation among individuals in a population that can be attributed to genetic differences. It is not a measure of how much of a single person's intelligence is due to their genes. Heritability estimates are population-specific. They can change if the environment changes. If you have high intelligence, you cannot say that a certain percentage of your brain power was inherited and the rest was learned. Furthermore, these estimates do not explain why one group might score differently than another group on average.

The influence of the environment is not static. Evidence from longitudinal studies shows that shared environmental influences on intelligence scores tend to decline from childhood to adulthood. Conversely, genetic influences appear to increase as we age. This may happen because, as we grow older, we gain more freedom to choose environments that suit our genetic predispositions. This is known as gene-environment correlation. For example, a child with a genetic tendency toward curiosity might seek out books and challenging puzzles, which in turn boosts their cognitive development.

Think of genetics as providing a potential range for growth, while the environment determines where you land within that range. For humans, these environmental factors include nutrition, access to quality schooling, and the stability of the home environment. Research consistently shows that children who grow up in poverty, facing chronic stress and limited intellectual stimulation, often struggle to reach their cognitive potential compared to those with more resources. These environmental disadvantages can suppress the expression of genetic potential.

We must also consider the Flynn effect. Researchers have observed that average intelligence test scores in many populations rose steadily throughout the twentieth century. This trend suggests that environmental factors, such as better nutrition, improved public health, and more complex educational demands, have a powerful impact on cognitive performance. However, the Flynn effect is a descriptive observation of trends over time; it does not prove that intelligence itself is a fixed or unchangeable trait, nor does it tell us exactly which environmental changes are most responsible.

We must be careful to avoid biological determinism. A person might have a genetic predisposition for high mathematical ability, but if they are never exposed to numbers or taught basic arithmetic, that potential remains untapped. The interaction between our DNA and our surroundings is dynamic and constant. Intelligence is a complex trait influenced by thousands of genes, each contributing a tiny effect, all of which are filtered through the lens of our life experiences. We are not merely products of our biological inheritance, nor are we blank slates molded entirely by our surroundings. We are the result of a lifelong conversation between our nature and our nurture.

## Key ideas

- Heritability is a statistical estimate of how much variation in a trait within a specific population is due to genetic differences, rather than a fixed percentage of an individual's intelligence.
- Twin and adoption studies provide strong correlational evidence that genetics significantly influence cognitive ability, but these influences are shaped by the environment.
- Shared environmental influences on intelligence tend to decline from childhood to adulthood, while genetic influences appear to increase as individuals gain more control over their environments.
- Gene-environment correlation and interaction mean that genetic predispositions can influence the environments people seek out, which in turn affects their cognitive development.
- The Flynn effect shows that population-level intelligence scores have risen over time, suggesting that environmental factors like nutrition and education play a major role in cognitive performance.
