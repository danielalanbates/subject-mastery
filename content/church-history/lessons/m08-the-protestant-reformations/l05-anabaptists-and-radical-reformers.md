# Anabaptists and Radical Reformers

Imagine a cold January evening in Zurich, Switzerland, in the year 1525. A small group of men gathers in a private home. They are not meeting to discuss theology in a university classroom, nor are they organizing a state-sanctioned church. They are here to break from the traditions of the past, including the baptism of infants that had been the standard practice for over a thousand years. One by one, these men go to the water, not as helpless infants, but as conscious believers choosing to dedicate their lives to Christ. This moment, known as the first believers’ baptism, marked the birth of the Anabaptist movement.

In our previous lesson, we looked at how John Calvin sought to build a godly society in Geneva, working closely with the city council to reform church and state together. The Anabaptists, however, took a different path. They believed the Reformers like Huldrych Zwingli and Calvin were not going far enough. They argued that the church should be entirely separate from the state and that true faith could only exist where individuals made a voluntary choice to follow Jesus. Because they insisted on baptizing adults who professed their faith, their enemies mockingly called them Anabaptists, which means re-baptizers. They viewed themselves simply as believers.

The movement was internally diverse. It included the Swiss Brethren, the mystical-leaning Anabaptists of South Germany, and later, the Dutch Anabaptists who would eventually become known as Mennonites. Women played significant roles in this movement, often at great personal risk. Figures such as Margaretha Blaurock and the Dutch martyr Anna Jansz were instrumental in spreading the faith and maintaining the community, often through writing and teaching.

In 1527, a group of Swiss Brethren met in the town of Schleitheim to outline their vision. The document they produced, the Schleitheim Confession, was a bold statement of their convictions. It contained seven articles covering topics such as the Lord’s Supper, which they viewed as a memorial of Christ’s sacrifice, and the practice of the ban. The ban, or shunning, was a form of church discipline used to maintain the holiness of the congregation. The confession also established the pastoral office and emphasized pacifism, the refusal to use violence, and the rejection of oaths.

Tragically, this radical vision was not always peaceful. In 1534, a different group of radicals took control of the German city of Münster. Led by Jan Matthys and John of Leiden, they believed the end of the world was imminent and established a militant, apocalyptic regime. They enforced their views through violence, practiced polygamy, and held the city against the surrounding armies. The Münster uprising ended in 1535 after a long siege. The leaders were captured, tortured, and executed. While the vast majority of Anabaptists were peace-loving people who sought only to live quiet, separate lives, the events in Münster gave their enemies a reason to label them all as dangerous revolutionaries.

This led to a wave of brutal persecution. Both Catholics and fellow Protestants viewed the Anabaptists as heretics and rebels. They were arrested, imprisoned, and often executed by drowning, burning, or beheading. Thousands died for their convictions, yet the movement survived. A former Dutch priest named Menno Simons became a crucial leader during this time. He helped stabilize the movement, emphasizing non-violence and the importance of a life transformed by the Holy Spirit. His followers carried on the tradition of the believers’ church, emphasizing humility and service to neighbors.

The Anabaptist story is a sobering reminder of how difficult it was for early modern society to accept religious diversity. They were a people who stood apart, believing that the church was a community of committed followers rather than a national institution. Their insistence on the separation of church and state and the necessity of individual conscience left a lasting mark on the development of religious liberty. These ideas would eventually influence the later Baptist traditions and contribute to the broader landscape of modern Western thought regarding the freedom of the individual to choose their own faith.

## Key ideas

- The Anabaptist movement began in 1525 with the practice of believers’ baptism, asserting that faith must be a voluntary, personal choice.
- The Schleitheim Confession established core practices for the movement, including the memorial view of the Lord’s Supper, the use of the ban for church discipline, and the rejection of violence.
- The violent uprising at Münster led by Jan Matthys and John of Leiden was an outlier that brought severe, widespread persecution upon the peaceful majority of Anabaptists.
- Anabaptist emphasis on the separation of church and state and the importance of individual conscience laid essential foundations for later Baptist traditions and the development of religious liberty in the West.
