# Basic Probability

Imagine you roll two ordinary six-sided dice, and you want to know how likely it is that the sum of the numbers showing on top equals eight. In our previous lesson, we used the binomial theorem to expand expressions involving combinations. Today, we turn that counting machinery directly toward measuring uncertainty. Probability gives us a precise mathematical language to measure how likely an event is to happen, turning vague hunches about chance into exact numbers.

To make this rigorous, we start by defining the sample space, which is the set of every possible outcome of an experiment. If you flip a coin twice, the sample space consists of four outcomes: landing heads first and heads second, heads then tails, tails then heads, and tails then tails. When every single outcome in a sample space has an identical chance of occurring, we call them equally likely outcomes. To find the probability of any specific event, we count how many outcomes make up that event and divide that number by the total number of outcomes in the entire sample space.

Because a count can never be negative, and the number of successful outcomes can never exceed the total size of the sample space, every probability is a real number ranging from zero to one inclusive. A probability of zero means an event is entirely impossible, while a probability of one means the event is certain to happen. For example, if you draw one card from a standard deck of fifty-two cards, the probability of drawing a red queen is the number of red queens, which is two, divided by fifty-two, which simplifies to one over twenty-six.

Sometimes it is much easier to calculate the probability that an event does not happen rather than the probability that it does. This leads us to the complement rule. The complement of an event is the collection of all outcomes in the sample space that are not in that event. Because an event either happens or it does not, the sum of the probability of an event and the probability of its complement must always equal one. If the probability of rain tomorrow is one over four, then the probability that it does not rain is one minus one over four, which gives three over four.

When we want to find the probability that either one of two events occurs, we use the addition rule for the word 'or'. If two events cannot possibly happen at the exact same time, we simply add their individual probabilities together. For instance, if you roll a single six-sided die, the probability of rolling a one is one over six, and the probability of rolling a six is also one over six. Because a single roll cannot be both a one and a six, the probability of rolling a one or a six is one over six plus one over six, which equals two over six, or one over three. If two events can overlap, we must subtract the probability of their intersection so we do not count the overlapping outcomes twice.

Next, we look at what happens when we perform multiple actions in sequence, such as flipping a coin and then rolling a die. When the outcome of the first event has no effect whatsoever on the outcome of the second event, we call the events independent. To find the probability that two independent events both happen, we use the multiplication rule, which states that we simply multiply their individual probabilities together. The probability of getting heads on a coin flip is one over two, and the probability of rolling a six on a die is one over six; therefore, the probability of getting heads and then rolling a six is one over two times one over six, which equals one over twelve. Now we can answer our opening question. By the multiplication principle, two dice have six times six, or thirty-six, equally likely outcomes, and five of them sum to eight: two and six, three and five, four and four, five and three, and six and two. So the probability of a sum of eight is five over thirty-six. By combining our understanding of systematic counting with probabilities, we can tackle intricate games of chance and complex random processes with confidence. As we move forward, we will leave numerical probability behind and cross into the rigorous world of mathematical logic, implication, and direct proof.

## Key ideas
- The sample space of an experiment is the set of all possible outcomes, and probability is computed by dividing the number of successful outcomes by the total number of equally likely outcomes.
- Every probability is a real number from zero to one, where zero represents an impossible event and one represents a certainty.
- The complement rule states that the probability of an event happening equals one minus the probability of that event not happening.
- The addition rule for mutually exclusive events allows us to add their individual probabilities to find the chance of either event occurring.
- The multiplication rule states that the joint probability of two independent events occurring together is the product of their individual probabilities.
