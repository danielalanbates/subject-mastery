# Dissolution and Solubility

Think about the last time you stirred a spoonful of sugar into a cup of hot tea. As you swirl the spoon, the solid sugar crystals seem to vanish, disappearing into the liquid until the tea tastes sweet. This process is called dissolution, and it is happening all around us, from the minerals dissolved in our drinking water to the oxygen gas circulating in our blood. In our previous look at the solid state, we examined how atoms and molecules pack into rigid, orderly structures. Now, we turn our attention to what happens when those structures break apart and disperse into a liquid solvent to form a homogeneous mixture, which we call a solution.

The fundamental rule governing why some substances dissolve while others do not is often summarized by the phrase like dissolves like. This means that substances with similar intermolecular forces tend to form solutions together. Polar solvents, like water, are excellent at dissolving polar solutes or ionic compounds because the partial charges on the water molecules can surround and stabilize the solute particles. We call this interaction solvation. For ionic compounds like sodium chloride, water molecules pull the sodium and chloride ions away from the crystal lattice, surrounding each ion in a cage of water molecules. If the solute is composed of ions, the resulting solution will conduct electricity, labeling it an electrolyte. Conversely, substances that do not dissociate into ions, such as sugar, are called nonelectrolytes and do not conduct electricity in water.

Not every substance will dissolve indefinitely. If you keep adding salt to a glass of water, eventually you will reach a point where no more salt can dissolve, no matter how much you stir. At this stage, the solution is saturated. In a saturated solution, the rate at which the solid dissolves is exactly equal to the rate at which the dissolved solute crystallizes back into a solid. It is a state of dynamic equilibrium. Before reaching this point, the solution is unsaturated, meaning it can still hold more solute.

Temperature plays a major role in how much solute a solvent can hold. For most solid solutes, solubility increases as the temperature of the liquid rises. Think of the extra energy in the system helping to overcome the forces holding the solid together. However, gases behave differently. When you heat a carbonated beverage, the gas escapes more quickly. The solubility of gases generally decreases as temperature increases because the added thermal energy allows the gas molecules to overcome the intermolecular forces holding them in the liquid, letting them escape into the atmosphere.

Pressure also influences solubility, but its impact depends on the state of the solute. For solids and liquids, pressure has a negligible effect on solubility. However, for gases, pressure is a major factor. This relationship is described by Henry’s Law, which states that the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid. Imagine a sealed bottle of soda. The pressure of carbon dioxide gas above the liquid is very high, which forces a large amount of carbon dioxide to remain dissolved in the soda. When you pop the cap, the pressure drops instantly, the solubility of the carbon dioxide decreases, and the gas rushes out in the form of bubbles.

To see this in a calculation, consider a scenario where the solubility of a gas is zero point zero three grams per liter at a pressure of one atmosphere. According to Henry’s Law, if we double the pressure to two atmospheres, the new solubility will be two times the original amount, giving us zero point zero six grams per liter. This linear relationship is a powerful tool for predicting how much gas a liquid can hold under different environmental conditions.

Understanding these principles of dissolution helps us move beyond merely mixing substances. It allows us to predict the behavior of chemical systems, whether in a laboratory flask or a biological cell. Solubility is not just about whether something disappears; it is about the energetic balance between the forces holding a substance together and the forces the solvent can offer to pull it apart. Having explored the limits of what a solvent can hold, we are now ready to look at how we quantify the amount of solute in those mixtures. In our next lesson, we will learn how to calculate the exact concentration of solutions using the concept of molarity.

## Key ideas

- The principle of like dissolves like explains that substances dissolve best in solvents with similar intermolecular forces, such as polar water molecules dissolving polar or ionic solutes.
- A saturated solution exists in a state of dynamic equilibrium where the rate of dissolution equals the rate of crystallization, meaning no additional solute can dissolve at that temperature.
- The solubility of solid solutes typically increases with temperature, while the solubility of gases typically decreases as temperature rises.
- Henry’s Law dictates that the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid surface, while pressure has a negligible effect on the solubility of solids and liquids.
