# Climate, Biomes and the Biosphere

If you were to board an airplane in tropical Ecuador and fly straight north to the arctic tundra of northern Canada, you would witness a dramatic transformation of life on Earth. Lush, towering forests packed with millions of species would slowly give way to temperate deciduous woodlands, then to vast stretches of coniferous needle-leaf trees, and finally to low-growing vegetation hugging frozen ground. Having recently explored how individual animals grow, develop, and reproduce, we now zoom out to the broad ecological stage where those organisms live. What fundamental physical forces dictate why specific plants and animals live where they do? The answer begins with climate and physical geography.

Climate is the long-term pattern of weather in a given area, and its primary engine is the Sun. Because the Earth is a sphere, solar radiation strikes the equator directly, concentrating thermal energy over a small surface area. Near the poles, that same amount of solar energy strikes at a slanting angle, spreading across a much larger surface and traveling through a thicker layer of atmosphere. This uneven heating creates global patterns of temperature and drives massive atmospheric circulation currents. Near the equator, intense heat warms the air, causing it to expand and rise. As this warm, moist air ascends into the upper atmosphere, it cools. Cool air holds less water vapor than warm air, so the moisture condenses and drops as abundant tropical rain.

While the Earth’s spherical shape explains why the equator is hotter than the poles, it does not explain why we have seasons. Seasonality is caused by the tilt of the Earth’s axis of rotation. The Earth is tilted at an angle of approximately twenty-three point five degrees relative to the plane of its orbit around the Sun. As the planet makes its annual journey, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun for half the year and away for the other half. This tilt changes the intensity of solar radiation and the length of the day, driving the seasonal cycles of growth and dormancy that we see in temperate and polar regions.

The rising air at the equator also sets the stage for global precipitation patterns. Once this air loses its moisture near the equator, the dry air currents push north and south toward thirty degrees latitude. As the air descends back toward Earth's surface, it warms up again. Warm, dry air absorbs moisture from the ground rather than releasing rain. Consequently, most of the planet's major hot deserts, such as the Sahara and the Australian Outback, are located in bands around thirty degrees north and south of the equator. This continuous cycle of rising moist air and falling dry air is known as a Hadley cell. Similar atmospheric circulation loops operate at higher latitudes, creating alternating belts of high precipitation and arid conditions across the globe.

Regional physical geography further reshapes these climates. Consider mountains. When warm, moist air blowing off an ocean encounters a mountain range, the air is forced upward. As it ascends, it cools and drops its moisture on the windward side of the peaks, fueling dense forests. By the time the air passes over the crest to the leeward side, it has lost nearly all its moisture. As it descends, it warms and draws water out of the landscape. This phenomenon, called a rain shadow, creates dry scrublands or deserts directly behind major mountain chains. Oceans also moderate climate because water has a high capacity to absorb and retain heat, keeping coastal regions milder than inland continental areas at the same latitude.

These abiotic, or non-living, conditions determine the distribution of biomes. A terrestrial biome is a major biogeographic region characterized by specific climate patterns and dominant types of vegetation. Ecology often maps these biomes using two primary variables: average annual temperature and average annual precipitation. High precipitation and high temperatures produce tropical rainforests; low precipitation and high temperatures yield tropical deserts; low precipitation and very low temperatures produce arctic tundra. Because plants cannot move, natural selection favors structural traits that suit these extremes, such as the narrow, waxy needles of conifers, which lose little water when the ground is frozen, and their cone-shaped crowns, which shed heavy snow in the cold boreal forest.

In contrast to terrestrial biomes, aquatic biomes are defined primarily by their physical and chemical environments rather than by dominant vegetation. The most fundamental distinction is salinity. Marine biomes, such as oceans and coral reefs, have salt concentrations averaging about three and a half percent, while freshwater biomes, like lakes and rivers, have salt concentrations of less than zero point one percent. Within these water bodies, life is further partitioned by light penetration. The upper layer of water where there is sufficient light for photosynthesis is called the photic zone. Below this lies the aphotic zone, a dark region where little to no light penetrates and organisms must rely on food drifting down from the surface.

Together, all the biomes on Earth combine to form the biosphere, the global ecological system integrating all living beings and their interactions with the physical environment. Understanding how climate and geography shape these biomes provides the template for life. But within these broad regions, organisms do not exist in isolation. They form dynamic groups of individuals of the same species that adapt and compete within these abiotic limits. ## Key ideas

- The uneven heating of Earth's spherical surface and the tilt of its axis create global temperature gradients and seasonal cycles.
- Global atmospheric circulation, such as Hadley cells, creates predictable patterns of high precipitation near the equator and arid conditions at thirty degrees latitude.
- Terrestrial biomes are categorized by their average annual temperature and precipitation, which dictate the types of vegetation that can survive there.
- Aquatic biomes are classified by physical and chemical factors, specifically their salinity and the depth of light penetration in the photic and aphotic zones.
- The biosphere represents the sum of all Earth's ecosystems, where the physical environment sets the ultimate limits on where life can exist.
