# The Twenty-First Century

Imagine the American landscape at the start of the new millennium. The country was reeling from the shock of the September 11 attacks, a turning point that fundamentally shifted the national focus toward security and foreign engagement. Yet, beneath the surface of this geopolitical transformation, the United States was also hurtling toward a massive domestic collapse. In late 2008, the world witnessed the most severe financial crisis since the Great Depression. A housing bubble, fueled by complex financial products and risky lending, finally burst. As home values plummeted, major financial institutions faced insolvency. The government intervened with massive emergency bailouts to stabilize the banking system. In response, Congress passed the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act in 2010, which introduced the most significant changes to financial regulation since the 1930s.

This economic turmoil served as the backdrop for the 2008 election, which resulted in the victory of Barack Obama. As the first African American president, Obama entered office facing the dual challenges of a global financial crisis and two ongoing wars, in Iraq and Afghanistan. His administration pushed for the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act to stimulate the economy, alongside the Affordable Care Act, a significant and contentious overhaul of the national healthcare system. Throughout his two terms, the country grappled with deep ideological divisions regarding the role of government, the scope of federal spending, and the reach of social policy.

The political environment grew increasingly polarized, setting the stage for the 2016 election. Donald Trump, a businessman and media personality, defeated Hillary Clinton in a campaign that tapped into the frustrations of voters who felt left behind by globalization and changing cultural norms. His presidency focused on deregulation and a shift toward an America First foreign policy. In 2017, he signed the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, which significantly lowered corporate and individual income tax rates. His judicial appointments, including three Supreme Court justices, significantly altered the legal landscape. This shift culminated in the 2022 decision in Dobbs versus Jackson Women’s Health Organization, which overturned the 1973 ruling in Roe versus Wade, ending the constitutional right to an abortion and returning the authority to regulate or restrict the practice to individual states.

As a new decade began, the world was upended by the COVID-19 pandemic. Beginning in early 2020, the virus spread rapidly, leading to widespread lockdowns and a severe economic contraction. In 2021, the Biden administration enacted the American Rescue Plan Act, a massive relief package intended to address the ongoing economic fallout of the pandemic. During this same period, the murder of George Floyd in 2020 sparked nationwide Black Lives Matter protests, which prompted a profound national reckoning regarding systemic racism and police conduct.

In the 2020 election, Joe Biden defeated Donald Trump. Biden’s presidency began in the immediate aftermath of the January 6, 2021, attack on the United States Capitol, an event that underscored the fragility of the nation's democratic institutions. Later that year, in August 2021, the United States completed its withdrawal of troops from Afghanistan, ending the longest war in American history. Biden also signed major laws on infrastructure, semiconductor manufacturing, and climate and health spending, which supporters praised as long-term investments and critics attacked as excessive spending. Throughout this era, the United States faced an intensifying strategic competition with China, characterized by significant trade tensions and a fierce rivalry over the development of critical technologies. In July 2024, Biden withdrew from his reelection race, and Vice President Kamala Harris became the Democratic nominee. Donald Trump defeated Harris that November and returned to the presidency in January 2025, becoming the second president, after Grover Cleveland, to serve two nonconsecutive terms.

As we conclude this study of American history, we see a nation that has moved from a collection of colonial outposts to a global superpower, constantly wrestling with its founding ideals. The story of the United States is one of persistent tension between the desire for individual freedom and the necessity of collective governance. While this course ends here, the historical process remains ongoing, as each new generation of Americans continues to interpret and reshape the nation's path forward.

## Key ideas

- The 2008 financial crisis triggered major regulatory responses like the Dodd-Frank Act, while the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act and the 2021 American Rescue Plan represented significant shifts in fiscal policy.
- The 2022 Dobbs decision fundamentally altered American jurisprudence by overturning the federal protection of abortion rights, reflecting deep ideological divisions.
- The 2020 Black Lives Matter protests and the January 6, 2021, Capitol attack highlighted intense social and political fractures regarding racial justice and the stability of democratic processes.
- The United States has transitioned into an era of heightened strategic competition with China while navigating the complex global aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic and the withdrawal from Afghanistan.
- The 2024 election, in which Donald Trump defeated Vice President Kamala Harris, returned Trump to the presidency in January 2025 for a second, nonconsecutive term.
