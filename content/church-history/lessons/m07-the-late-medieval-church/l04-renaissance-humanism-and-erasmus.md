# Renaissance Humanism and Erasmus

Imagine walking into a dusty library in the fifteenth century. You are looking for a classic text, perhaps a work by a Roman orator or a letter from an early church father. Instead of finding a modern summary or a medieval commentary, you discover the original manuscript, written in the elegant, clear Latin of antiquity. This experience was at the heart of the Renaissance, a period of intellectual rebirth that sought to move away from the dense, technical style of medieval scholasticism and return directly to the ancient sources. This movement was known as Humanism. It was not a rejection of faith, but rather a conviction that by studying the foundational texts of the classical world and the early church, one could cultivate a more refined mind and a deeper, more authentic Christian life.

In our previous lesson, we explored how medieval mystics sought a direct, interior experience of God, often bypassing the formal structures of the institutional church. Renaissance Humanists shared this desire for directness, but they pursued it through the rigorous study of language and history. They adopted the motto, "back to the sources," or ad fontes. They believed that centuries of medieval interpretation had obscured the true meaning of both classical wisdom and the biblical text itself. By mastering Greek, Hebrew, and classical Latin, these scholars sought to strip away layers of later tradition to encounter the original intentions of the authors.

The timing of this intellectual shift was crucial, thanks to a revolutionary invention: the printing press. Developed by Johannes Gutenberg in the mid-fifteenth century, this technology changed the world. Before the press, books were painstakingly copied by hand, making them expensive, rare, and prone to errors in transcription. With movable type, knowledge could be mass-produced and distributed with unprecedented speed. Ideas that once stayed within the walls of a single monastery could now travel across Europe, fueling debates and sparking new movements. The printing press was the primary engine that drove the spread of Humanist thought and, eventually, the Reformation.

The most famous of these Christian Humanists was Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam. A brilliant scholar and a master of Latin, Erasmus used his literary skills to champion a reform of the church from within. He was deeply critical of the corruption, superstition, and dry legalism he saw in the church of his day. He did not want to tear the church down; he wanted to return it to the simplicity of the gospel. Erasmus believed that the true Christian life was a "philosophy of Christ," a way of living characterized by humility, charity, and a deep, personal devotion to the teachings of Jesus, rather than a rigid adherence to empty rituals.

Erasmus’s most significant contribution to the church was his publication of the Greek New Testament in 1516. For centuries, the Western church had relied almost exclusively on the Latin Vulgate, a translation that had become the standard but was riddled with errors that had crept in over a millennium of copying. By publishing the original Greek alongside a fresh, corrected Latin translation, Erasmus essentially invited scholars and laypeople alike to judge the church's practices against the actual words of the apostles. His work revealed that some common church practices lacked a clear foundation in the New Testament. This was a radical move. It empowered individual readers to engage with the scripture for themselves, setting the stage for the dramatic challenges to papal authority that would soon follow.

Erasmus’s calls for reform were often sharp and witty. In his famous work, The Praise of Folly, he satirized the ignorance of some clergy and the vanity of the religious orders, urging them to abandon their pride and return to the spirit of the New Testament. While he remained a loyal member of the Roman Catholic Church until his death, his work provided the intellectual tools that later reformers would use to question the very structure of the medieval church. He famously remarked that he hoped to see the day when even the farmer at his plow or the weaver at his loom could recite the scriptures.

The Humanist movement did more than just recover old texts; it fundamentally changed how people understood their relationship to the past and to the divine. By emphasizing the importance of human reason and the dignity of the individual, it encouraged a more critical and engaged form of faith. As the intellectual climate of Europe shifted toward this new focus on original sources and textual accuracy, the political and religious landscape of the East was facing a different kind of crisis. We now turn our attention to the final days of the Byzantine Empire and the enduring influence of the church in the East.

## Key ideas

- Renaissance Humanism was an intellectual movement that prioritized returning to original classical and biblical sources to renew both culture and the church.
- The invention of the printing press acted as a catalyst for the spread of new ideas, making texts accessible to a wider audience and challenging the church's control over information.
- Desiderius Erasmus used his scholarship to advocate for a "philosophy of Christ," emphasizing simple, heartfelt faith over the rigid rituals and perceived corruption of the late medieval church.
- By publishing the Greek New Testament, Erasmus provided a foundation for reformers to evaluate church teachings against the original biblical text, permanently shifting the approach to theology and authority.
