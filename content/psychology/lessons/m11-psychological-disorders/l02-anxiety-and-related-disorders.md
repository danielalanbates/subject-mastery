# Anxiety, Obsessive-Compulsive and Trauma Disorders

Imagine you are standing at the edge of a high balcony. Most people feel a quick jolt of caution, a natural signal that helps keep us safe. But for some, that signal becomes a constant, deafening alarm that interferes with daily life. In our last lesson, we discussed how psychologists define and diagnose disorders using the manual known as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. Today, we turn our focus to conditions where that internal alarm system is stuck in the on position, or where it has been permanently altered by past experiences.

Anxiety disorders are the most common class of mental illness, with lifetime prevalence rates of about thirty percent. These conditions are characterized by excessive, persistent fear and apprehension. Consider phobias, which affect about seven to nine percent of the population. These are intense, irrational fears of specific objects or situations, such as heights or flying. While learning theories suggest that some phobias may be acquired through classical conditioning—a claim supported by laboratory conditioning studies and longitudinal data—this is only one of several potential pathways.

This is different from panic disorder, which affects about two to three percent of people. This diagnosis requires recurrent, unexpected panic attacks plus at least one month of persistent concern about additional attacks or their consequences. Then there is generalized anxiety disorder, or GAD, which affects about three to five percent of the population. Unlike the sudden spikes of a panic attack, GAD is a state of constant, low-level dread. It requires excessive worry more days than not for at least six months.

While these conditions focus on anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder, or OCD, involves a different kind of internal struggle. Affecting about one to two percent of people, it is defined by obsessions—intrusive, unwanted thoughts—and compulsions—repetitive behaviors that are time-consuming, taking more than one hour per day, or cause significant distress. Post-traumatic stress disorder, or PTSD, affects about six to eight percent of the population in the United States. It requires exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence. Symptoms fall into four clusters: intrusive memories, avoidance of reminders, negative shifts in mood or thoughts, and alterations in arousal or reactivity.

These disorders frequently co-occur with one another, with depression, and with substance use. For example, over half of those with generalized anxiety disorder also meet the criteria for another anxiety or mood disorder.

What causes these conditions? Current research points to a complex interplay of factors. Heritability studies suggest that genetics play a role, with heritability estimates of about thirty to forty percent for anxiety disorders. From a biological perspective, functional magnetic resonance imaging studies have shown a correlation between anxiety and heightened activity in the amygdala, an area deep in the middle of the brain. However, these correlational findings do not prove the amygdala causes anxiety. Similarly, reduced activity in the prefrontal cortex, which sits at the front of the brain, is often observed in people with anxiety, but this is also a correlational finding. More specifically, PTSD involves dysregulation of the stress-response system known as the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, while OCD is linked to abnormalities in circuits connecting the cortex, the striatum, and the thalamus.

Psychological factors also matter. Cognitive perspectives emphasize how our interpretations of events influence our anxiety levels. If someone consistently interprets ambiguous situations as threatening, they are more likely to experience chronic anxiety.

Regarding treatment, first-line psychosocial interventions, such as cognitive behavioral therapy with exposure, have strong empirical support. For OCD, a specific type of therapy called exposure and response prevention is the gold standard. Pharmacological treatments, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, also outperform placebo in multiple randomized controlled trials, though the average advantage is small to moderate.

It is important to remember that these conditions are not a sign of weakness. They are complex health challenges. If you or someone you know is struggling, please reach out for professional help. In the United States, you can call or text nine eight eight to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline for support. Understanding these patterns is a vital step in learning how to support mental health.

## Key ideas

- Anxiety disorders are the most common class of mental illness, often characterized by high rates of comorbidity with depression and other anxiety conditions.
- Diagnostic criteria for these disorders are specific, requiring defined durations for worry, time-consuming engagement in rituals for obsessive-compulsive disorder, or specific symptom clusters following trauma.
- Evidence for the causes of these disorders is multifactorial, involving genetic predispositions, neurobiological markers, and learned cognitive patterns, though imaging data currently show correlations rather than direct causation.
- Treatment for these disorders is evidence-based, with cognitive behavioral therapy and specific pharmacological interventions showing strong support in clinical trials.
