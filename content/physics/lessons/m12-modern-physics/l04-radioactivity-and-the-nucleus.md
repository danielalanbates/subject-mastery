# Radioactivity and the Nucleus

Imagine holding a tiny, invisible speck of matter that is constantly, quietly spitting out invisible bullets of energy and heavy particles, changing its very identity with every shot it fires. This is not science fiction; it is happening right now inside every gram of uranium on Earth, and even inside the potassium in your own body. In our last lesson, we explored the Bohr atom, learning how electrons occupy distinct energy levels around a central positive core. But the atom has a deeper secret hidden inside that core. Today, we turn our attention inward to the nucleus itself, asking what holds it together against immense forces, and how unstable atomic nuclei undergo radioactive decay.

To understand the nucleus, we must first confront a major puzzle. Inside a tiny space at the center of the atom, packed tightly together, are protons and neutrons. Protons carry a positive electrical charge. According to the laws of electricity and magnetism that we studied earlier, like charges repel each other with enormous force, especially when they are pressed as close together as subatomic particles are. If the electrical repulsion were the only force at play, every atomic nucleus with more than one proton would instantly fly apart. Yet many nuclei remain stable for billions of years.

Nature solves this problem through a completely different interaction called the strong nuclear force. This force is powerfully attractive between protons and neutrons, but only across unimaginably short distances, about the width of a single proton. At that microscopic range, the strong nuclear force completely overwhelms the electrical repulsion, gluing the nuclear particles tightly together. Neutrons play a crucial role here, acting like neutral spacers that add to the attractive strong force without adding any electrical repulsion, helping to keep the protons from tearing the nucleus apart. Alongside the strong force, nature operates three other fundamental forces: gravity, electromagnetism, and the weak nuclear force, which governs the specific interactions of subatomic particles that lead to radioactive transformations.

However, this delicate balance is fragile, especially in very large atoms where the number of protons grows high. When a nucleus becomes too large, or when it has an awkward imbalance of protons and neutrons, the strong force can no longer hold it together securely. The nucleus becomes unstable, or radioactive. To achieve stability, the unstable nucleus undergoes radioactive decay, spontaneously transforming into a different configuration and releasing energy and particles in the process. The energy released in every radioactive decay event is directly tied to a reduction in the total mass of the system, following the principle of mass-energy equivalence where energy equals mass times the speed of light squared.

Physicists group radioactive decay into three main types, each with distinct properties of penetration and ionization. The first is alpha decay, which happens in very heavy elements like uranium and radium. In this process, the nucleus ejects an alpha particle, which consists of two protons and two neutrons bound together, identical to the nucleus of a helium atom. Alpha particles have low penetrating power and can be stopped by a simple sheet of paper, but their heavy mass makes them highly ionizing if they interact with matter. By spitting out this package, the parent nucleus reduces its mass and charge, shifting closer to a stable state, and the original element transforms into a completely different element.

The second type is beta decay, which occurs when a nucleus has an unstable ratio of neutrons to protons, governed by the weak nuclear force. Standard college textbooks classify beta decay into three distinct forms. In beta-minus decay, one of the neutrons inside the nucleus transforms into a proton while emitting an electron and a nearly massless particle called an antineutrino. In beta-plus decay, a proton transforms into a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino. In the third form, known as electron capture, the nucleus pulls in an inner orbital electron and turns a proton into a neutron. Beta particles have moderate penetrating power, capable of passing through paper but typically stopped by a sheet of aluminum.

The third major type is gamma decay. Often, after an alpha or beta decay event, the remaining nuclear particles are left in an excited, high energy state, much like an electron in an excited Bohr orbit. To settle down into its lowest energy ground state, the nucleus sheds this excess energy by emitting a gamma ray, which is a packet of high frequency electromagnetic radiation without mass or charge. Gamma rays have very high penetrating power, requiring thick blocks of lead or concrete to stop them, though they ionize matter less densely than alpha or beta particles.

We measure the pace of this invisible transformation using a concept called half-life, which is the amount of time required for exactly half of a given sample of radioactive nuclei to undergo decay. Imagine you start with eight hundred grams of a radioactive isotope that has a half-life of ten years. After ten years pass, four hundred grams remain. After another ten years, two hundred grams remain. Four hundred grams is about the mass of a thick paperback book, giving us a concrete sense of the physical quantity remaining after one half-life. As we look ahead to our next lesson, we will see how unlocking the tremendous energy bound up within these nuclear transformations allows us to harness nuclear fission and fusion, shaping both our energy technology and our understanding of the cosmos.

## Key ideas
- The strong nuclear force binds protons and neutrons together in the nucleus, overcoming the powerful electrical repulsion between positive charges alongside the mediating effects of the other fundamental forces.
- Radioactive decay occurs when unstable nuclei with improper balances of particles spontaneously transform to reach a stable state, releasing energy linked directly to a loss of mass through mass-energy equivalence.
- The three primary types of radiation differ in their properties, with alpha particles stopped by paper, beta particles stopped by aluminum, and gamma rays requiring dense lead for shielding.
- Beta decay occurs in three forms, including beta-minus, beta-plus, and electron capture, all governed by the weak nuclear force.
- The half-life of a radioactive substance is the predictable duration of time required for half of the nuclei in a given sample to decay.
