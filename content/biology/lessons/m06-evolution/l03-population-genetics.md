# Microevolution and Population Genetics

Imagine walking through a dense forest and noticing that the wildflowers growing near the shaded path have darker petals than the exact same species blooming out in the bright meadow. In our last lesson, we saw the broad strokes of evolutionary history etched into fossils and anatomical structures. Today, we zoom in dramatically. We are looking at microevolution, which is evolution on a small scale, specifically the change in allele frequencies within a population over generations. An allele is simply a variant form of a gene, and tracking how common these variants are allows biologists to measure evolution as it happens.

To understand how populations change, population genetics uses a powerful baseline called the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. Imagine a theoretical population where no evolution is occurring at all. Allele frequencies remain completely stable generation after generation. For this frozen state to hold, a specific set of conditions must be met. First, there can be no mutations altering the gene pool. Second, no migration can introduce or remove alleles. Third, mating must be entirely random. Fourth, the population must be infinitely large to prevent random statistical fluctuations. Finally, natural selection must not favor any particular trait over another. In this ideal case the frequencies of genotypes follow a simple rule: if p and q are the frequencies of two alleles, the three genotypes appear in the proportions p squared, two p q, and q squared. Real populations, of course, almost always break at least some of these conditions. By comparing real-world genetic data to this static mathematical model, scientists can measure exactly how and why a population is evolving.

The first major driver of microevolution is genetic drift, which is a change in allele frequencies driven purely by chance rather than natural selection. Think of a small population of beetles living on an island, some green and some brown. If a heavy boot accidentally steps on the patch, crushing three brown beetles and leaving only green ones, the genetic makeup of the population shifts instantly. This is not because green beetles were superior in any survival sense, but simply because of bad luck. Genetic drift packs its biggest punch in small populations, where random events can easily wipe out rare alleles altogether.

Two classic scenarios illustrate how dramatic genetic drift can be. The first is the bottleneck effect, which happens when a natural disaster or human activity violently reduces a population to a tiny fraction of its former size. The few survivors carry only a fraction of the original gene pool, much like pulling a random handful of colored beads from a massive bag. The second scenario is the founder effect, which occurs when a small group of individuals breaks away from a larger population to colonize a new area, such as an island. Because these few founders carry only a sample of the parent population's genetic diversity, the new colony often looks very different genetically from the home population.

While genetic drift relies on random chance, gene flow is about connection and movement. Gene flow is the transfer of alleles into or out of a population due to the migration of fertile individuals or their gametes. Imagine a river separating two populations of frogs, one with bright yellow bellies and one with dull olive bellies. If a flood creates a temporary land bridge and a few yellow-bellied frogs hop across to join the olive group, they introduce new alleles. Gene flow tends to reduce differences between populations over time, effectively stirring the genetic pot across geographic boundaries.

Natural selection, which we met at the start of this module, is the other great force; unlike drift, it changes allele frequencies in a consistent direction. Finally, we must look at mutation, the ultimate source of all new genetic variation. A mutation is a random change in the nucleotide sequence of an organism's DNA. Most mutations are neutral, some are harmful, and a rare few provide a subtle advantage. While mutation rates for any single gene are generally very low, mutation is the foundational engine of evolution. Without it, genetic drift, gene flow, and natural selection would simply recycle existing variations, and life would lose its capacity to innovate.

By studying how mutations, migration, and random chance reshape gene pools, we unlock the mechanics of population-level change. These shifting allele frequencies lay the groundwork for major evolutionary leaps. Next, we will explore how these accumulated genetic shifts can build absolute barriers between groups, giving rise to entirely new species.

## Key ideas
- Microevolution is defined as a generational change in allele frequencies within a population.
- The Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium provides a mathematical baseline for a population that is not evolving, which requires the absence of mutation, migration, selection, and non-random mating, alongside an infinite population size.
- Genetic drift alters allele frequencies through random chance and has the strongest impact in small populations.
- Gene flow introduces or removes alleles when individuals migrate between populations.
- Mutation acts as the ultimate source of new genetic variation upon which other evolutionary mechanisms operate.
