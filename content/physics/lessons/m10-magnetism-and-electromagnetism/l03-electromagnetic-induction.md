# Electromagnetic Induction

Imagine sliding a small magnet back and forth through a tight loop of copper wire. In our last study, we saw how running an electric current through a wire creates a magnetic field, powering everyday motors and electromagnets. But nature works in reverse, too. If you move that magnet through the loop, something remarkable happens without any battery attached. A current suddenly starts flowing through the wire. This generation of electricity from changing magnetic fields is called electromagnetic induction. It is the invisible engine behind almost all the electrical power we use today, from household wall outlets to massive industrial power plants.

To understand why this happens, we must look closer at the invisible lines of magnetic force surrounding a magnet, which we call magnetic flux. Think of magnetic flux as the total amount of magnetic field passing through the area inside the wire loop. The exact mathematical value of this magnetic flux equals the strength of the magnetic field multiplied by the area of the loop, and then multiplied by the cosine of the angle between the magnetic field direction and the normal line pointing straight out from the surface of the loop. When the magnet sits completely still near the wire, a steady amount of field passes through the loop, and nothing happens. But the moment you push or pull the magnet, or rotate the loop, the amount of flux passing through the loop alters. A changing magnetic flux exerts a force on the electrons inside the wire, pushing them along the conductor and creating an induced voltage. The current in the circuit then depends on that induced voltage and the electrical resistance of the circuit.

This fundamental rule of nature was discovered in the eighteen thirties by the English experimentalist Michael Faraday and independently by the American physicist Joseph Henry. Faraday's law states that the magnitude of the induced voltage equals the rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop, which is the change in flux divided by the time interval. If you slide the magnet very slowly through the wire loop, the change happens gradually, and the induced voltage is tiny. If you shove the magnet through rapidly, the changing magnetic flux happens much faster, inducing a much larger voltage and current. Furthermore, adding more loops of wire to form a coil multiplies the effect, because each individual loop catches a share of the changing magnetic field.

Let us trace a quick example to see these numbers in action. Suppose you have a single circular loop of wire with an area of zero point five square meters, and you place it in a uniform magnetic field of two teslas. If you rotate the loop so that the magnetic flux passing through it drops from its maximum value of one weber down to zero in zero point one second, the average induced voltage equals the change in flux divided by that time interval. The change in flux is one weber. Dividing one weber by zero point one second yields an induced voltage of ten volts. A sensible check tells us that ten volts is about the voltage of a small electronics battery, which easily drives a measurable current through a standard copper wire when connected in a closed circuit.

There is another wonderful detail about this process, discovered by the Baltic German physicist Heinrich Lenz. Lenz figured out that the induced current always flows in a direction that creates its own secondary magnetic field, and this new field acts to oppose the original change that created it. If you push the north pole of a magnet toward a coil, the coil instantly generates its own north pole facing outward to push back against you. You can actually feel this resistance as a gentle physical pushing force on the magnet. This law of conservation in action ensures we never get something for nothing; the mechanical work you do pushing the magnet against that magnetic resistance is precisely what transforms into electrical energy in the circuit.

Understanding how moving magnetic fields spawn electric currents opens the door to creating continuous power. In our next study, we will build on this principle to see how mechanical rotation inside devices produces the electrical current that powers our modern world.

## Key ideas
- Electromagnetic induction is the process of generating an electric voltage and current in a conductor by changing the magnetic flux passing through it.
- Magnetic flux is calculated as the magnetic field multiplied by the area and the cosine of the angle between the field and the area normal.
- Faraday's law states that the magnitude of the induced voltage equals the rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop, or the change in flux divided by the time interval.
- Lenz's law states that an induced current always flows in a direction that creates a magnetic field opposing the original change, conserving energy.
