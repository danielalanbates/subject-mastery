# Gibbs Free Energy and Equilibrium

Imagine you are trying to push a heavy boulder up a hill. You know that gravity wants to pull it back down, while your muscles provide the energy to drive it upward. In chemistry, reactions face a similar tug of war. We have already explored entropy, which tells us that the universe tends toward disorder. But as you might have noticed, some reactions occur even if they seem to decrease the local entropy. To understand why, we need to balance two competing forces: the heat released or absorbed by the reaction, and the change in the system's disorder. We bring these together in a single, powerful concept called Gibbs free energy.

Gibbs free energy is the ultimate scorecard for a chemical reaction. It tells us the maximum amount of useful work we can extract from a process occurring at a constant temperature and pressure. We calculate this by taking the change in enthalpy, which represents the heat energy involved, and subtracting the product of the absolute temperature and the change in entropy. Think of it as a balance sheet. If the reaction releases a lot of heat, that helps drive it forward. If the reaction creates more disorder, that also helps. When these factors align, the free energy change is negative, and the reaction is spontaneous. It will proceed on its own without needing a constant input of energy from the outside.

Temperature acts as a dial that can shift this balance. Consider a reaction where the enthalpy change is positive, meaning the system absorbs heat, but the entropy change is also positive, meaning the system becomes more disordered. At low temperatures, the enthalpy term dominates, and the reaction stays put. However, as you turn up the heat, the entropy term grows larger. Eventually, the temperature becomes high enough that the entropy contribution overcomes the energy cost. The free energy change becomes negative, and the reaction suddenly becomes spontaneous. This is exactly how we decompose calcium carbonate into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. It requires high heat to overcome the energy barrier, but once the temperature is high enough, the entropy gain drives the reaction forward.

This relationship between free energy and spontaneity also gives us a window into the state of equilibrium. When a reaction is not yet at equilibrium, its instantaneous free energy change is defined by the standard free energy change plus the product of the gas constant, the absolute temperature, and the natural logarithm of the reaction quotient. As the reaction proceeds, the reaction quotient changes, and the free energy of the system moves toward a minimum. At the exact point of equilibrium, the free energy of the system has reached that absolute minimum, meaning the instantaneous free energy change is zero.

We can link this state of equilibrium directly to the standard free energy change. The standard free energy change is the value measured when all reactants and products are at a standard state. Chemists use two different conventions for standard pressure: one bar, which is the modern standard, or one atmosphere, which is the older convention. The standard free energy change equals negative R T times the natural logarithm of the equilibrium constant. This means that if the standard free energy change is very negative, the equilibrium constant will be very large, showing that the reaction favors the products. Conversely, if the standard free energy change is positive, the equilibrium constant will be very small, indicating that the reactants remain the dominant species at equilibrium.

Let us look at a simple example. Suppose we have a reaction with a standard free energy change of negative eleven point four kilojoules per mole at two hundred ninety-eight Kelvin. Because the value is negative, we know the reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions and will favor the products. By using the relationship between free energy and the equilibrium constant, we can calculate the value of the constant. First, divide the standard free energy change by the product of negative R and the temperature. If we use the gas constant of eight point three one four joules per mole Kelvin, we divide negative eleven thousand four hundred joules per mole by the product of negative eight point three one four and two hundred ninety-eight. This gives us four point six. Taking the exponential of four point six gives an equilibrium constant of approximately one hundred. This confirms that the products are significantly more abundant than the reactants when the system settles.

Understanding this energy landscape is essential for moving from static equations to the dynamic world of electron movement. By mastering how free energy drives chemical changes toward equilibrium, we prepare ourselves to see how these same principles govern the flow of electricity in chemical cells.

## Key ideas

- Gibbs free energy determines the spontaneity of a reaction by balancing the heat released or absorbed against the change in system disorder.
- Temperature can shift a reaction from non-spontaneous to spontaneous if the entropy change of the reaction is positive.
- The instantaneous free energy change depends on the reaction quotient, and it equals zero when the system reaches equilibrium.
- The standard free energy change is equal to the negative product of the gas constant, the temperature, and the natural logarithm of the equilibrium constant.
