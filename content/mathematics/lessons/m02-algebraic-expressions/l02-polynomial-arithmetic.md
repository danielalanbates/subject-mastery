# Polynomials and Their Arithmetic

Imagine walking into a tile workshop where every floor design is built from square tiles of different sizes. Some tiles are large squares, others are narrow rectangular strips, and some are tiny unit squares. If you want to know the total area of a custom floor, you add up the areas of all these individual pieces. In algebra, polynomials behave in much the same way. Building on our work with basic variables and expressions, we now study polynomials, which are finite sums of terms where each variable is raised to a whole number power.

A polynomial is made of individual parts called terms, separated by plus or minus signs. Consider an expression like four x cubed minus two x squared plus five x minus seven. Each part of that expression is a term. The number multiplying the variable in a term is its coefficient. In our example, the coefficient of the cubed term is four, and the coefficient of the linear term is five. The standalone number at the end, negative seven, is the constant term. The degree of a term is simply the exponent of its variable. For a multi-variable term, you add the exponents together. The degree of the entire polynomial is the highest degree found among all of its individual terms. Our example has a degree of three because the highest exponent is three.

Adding and subtracting polynomials is essentially a matter of organizing your tiles by size. You combine only like terms, which are terms that share the exact same variables raised to the exact same powers. Suppose you want to add the polynomial three x squared plus four x minus one to the polynomial two x squared minus x plus six. You group the x squared terms together to get five x squared, the x terms together to get three x, and the constants together to get plus five. Your final sum is five x squared plus three x plus five. When subtracting polynomials, remember to distribute the negative sign to every term inside the second set of parentheses before you combine your like terms.

Multiplying polynomials requires the distributive property, which we first encountered in arithmetic. If you multiply a single term by a sum, you multiply that term by each part of the sum individually. For instance, two x multiplied by the quantity three x plus four gives six x squared plus eight x. When multiplying two binomials, which are polynomials with two terms each, we multiply every term in the first binomial by every term in the second binomial by multiplying the first terms, then the outer terms, then the inner terms, and finally the last terms. To multiply the quantity x plus two by the quantity x plus three, you multiply the first terms to get x squared, the outer terms to get three x, the inner terms to get two x, and the last terms to get six. Combining the middle terms yields x squared plus five x plus six.

Certain multiplication patterns appear so frequently that mathematicians give them special names. In these patterns, the letters x and y represent any terms or expressions, not just single variables. The square of a sum, such as the quantity x plus y squared, always expands to the first term squared plus twice the product of both terms plus the second term squared. Test this with actual numbers by taking the quantity three plus two squared, which is five squared, or twenty-five. If you apply the formula, you get three squared, which is nine, plus two times three times two, which is twelve, plus two squared, which is four. Nine plus twelve plus four equals twenty-five. Similarly, the square of a difference, like the quantity x minus y squared, gives the first term squared minus twice the product plus the second term squared. Finally, the product of a sum and a difference, such as the quantity x plus y times the quantity x minus y, always results in the difference of two squares, which is x squared minus y squared. Recognizing these special products quickly will save you countless steps as we move forward.

As our algebraic toolset grows, we will soon want to reverse this entire multiplication process. In our next lesson, we will explore how to take a polynomial apart by factoring out common terms and grouping.

## Key ideas
- A polynomial is a sum of terms where each variable has a whole number exponent, and its degree is determined by its highest exponent term.
- Adding and subtracting polynomials is performed by combining the coefficients of like terms that share identical variable powers.
- Multiplying polynomials relies on the distributive property, ensuring every term in one expression multiplies every term in the other by taking products of first, outer, inner, and last terms.
- Special product formulas provide a fast way to square binomials and multiply a sum by a difference without full expansion.
