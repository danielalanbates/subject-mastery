# Positive Externalities and Public Goods

Imagine you decide to plant a magnificent flowering cherry tree in your front yard. You pay for it out of your own pocket, spend your Saturday afternoon digging in the dirt, and water it all summer. Naturally, you enjoy the lovely blossoms from your living room window. But your neighbors also enjoy the view as they walk down the sidewalk, and passing strangers smile at the burst of color. Passersby receive a benefit that you did not charge them for, and you do not capture the full value of your investment. In the language of economics, your tree has created a positive externality.

In our previous lesson, we looked at how negative externalities like pollution cause markets to overproduce harmful activities because firms do not pay the full social costs. Today, we turn the coin over to examine positive externalities and their close cousins, public goods. When a market activity generates widespread benefits that spill over to uninvolved third parties, private actors face a different kind of incentive problem. Because private individuals and firms look only at their own private costs and benefits, they tend to underproduce goods and services that generate rich external benefits for society.

Consider education, perhaps the most prominent example of a positive externality. When you pay tuition for college, you expect private benefits. You learn specific skills, earn a degree, and qualify for a higher salary. Those private returns motivate you to enroll. But your education also creates positive spillovers for the wider community. A better educated workforce fosters higher productivity, drives technological innovation, encourages civic participation, and lowers crime rates. Your neighbors and employers benefit from your education even though they did not chip in for your textbooks.

To see how this creates inefficiency, let us look at a simple numerical example. Suppose a community college course costs one thousand dollars to provide per student. Suppose the private financial return to the student is eight hundred dollars, which is less than the cost, so the student decides not to enroll. However, the course also generates four hundred dollars in wider social benefits for the local economy. If we add the private benefit of eight hundred dollars to the external benefit of four hundred dollars, the total social benefit is twelve hundred dollars. Because twelve hundred dollars exceeds the one thousand dollar cost, the class is socially efficient and ought to be run. Yet the private market leaves it unprovided.

To correct this underproduction, governments step in with policies such as subsidized public schooling, student grants, and tax credits. By lowering the out of pocket cost of education, public policy pushes the market closer to the efficient level where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost.

Alongside positive externalities lie public goods, which present an even starker challenge to private markets. Economists define public goods by two specific characteristics: they are non-excludable and non-rivalrous. Non-excludable means that once the good is provided, you cannot easily prevent people who have not paid for it from enjoying it. Non-rivalrous means that one person's use of the good does not reduce the amount available for anyone else. Streetlights and national defense are classic examples. If a city installs a streetlight, it lights the street for everyone, whether they paid local taxes or not.

The non-rival nature of public goods leads to a profound insight about efficiency, often called the Samuelson condition. Because the marginal cost of consuming an additional unit of a public good is zero once the good is produced, the efficient provision of a public good requires that the sum of marginal benefits across all individuals must equal the marginal cost of provision.

These traits also create what economists call the free rider problem. Because individuals know they cannot be excluded from enjoying a public good once it exists, they have a strong incentive to withhold their money and wait for someone else to pay. If everyone tries to be a free rider, no one voluntarily finances the good, and the private market produces zero units of it. Even if the total value to the community far exceeds the total cost, private profit seeking firms cannot make money selling a non-excludable good. Therefore, society relies on the government to collect taxes and provide public goods directly.

While mainstream economic theory agrees that public goods and positive externalities require government intervention, debates occasionally arise over the best delivery mechanism. Some economists favor direct government provision, while others advocate for voucher systems or public private partnerships that harness market competition to deliver these goods more efficiently. Yet on the core diagnosis, consensus holds firm: left entirely to private market forces, goods with massive positive spillovers and public characteristics will be drastically underproduced.

## Key ideas
- Positive externalities occur when private market activities generate uncompensated benefits for third parties, leading to market underproduction.
- Public goods are defined by being both non-excludable and non-rivalrous, meaning people cannot be easily barred from enjoying them once provided.
- The implication of non-rivalry is that the marginal cost of consuming an additional unit of a public good is zero, which underlies the Samuelson efficiency condition stating that the sum of marginal benefits across all individuals must equal marginal cost.
- The free rider problem arises when individuals withhold payment for public goods, hoping others will bear the cost instead.
- Government intervention through subsidies, public provision, and tax policy helps correct the market inefficiencies caused by underproduced public goods and positive spillovers.
