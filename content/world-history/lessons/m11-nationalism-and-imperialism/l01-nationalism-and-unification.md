# Nationalism and the Unification of States

Imagine you are living in a small town in central Europe in the early nineteenth century. You speak a specific dialect, you share a common history with your neighbors, and you identify with a local prince. Yet, your home is just one tiny piece of a fragmented puzzle, caught in the shadow of distant, powerful empires. By the end of the century, this patchwork of identities was swept away by a powerful new force called nationalism. This ideology argued that people who share a language, culture, and history should live together in a single, self-governing state.

In our last lesson, we explored how the Industrial Revolution and new political ideologies began to restructure society. Now, we turn to how those changes fueled the rise of the nation-state. Nationalism was not just a feeling of pride in one’s country; it was a political project. It demanded that the boundaries of the state match the boundaries of the people. This was a radical departure from the old model, where monarchs ruled over vast, diverse territories regardless of who lived there.

The foundation for this change was laid at the Congress of Vienna in 1815. After the defeat of Napoleon, European leaders reorganized the continent. They established a loose confederation of German states and left Italy divided into several smaller entities, many under the influence of the Austrian Empire. While the Congress aimed to restore stability and monarchical order, it inadvertently created the very fragmentation that nationalists would later seek to overcome.

Economic factors played a crucial role in this transition. In the German lands, Prussia led the creation of the Zollverein, or customs union, in 1834. By eliminating internal tariffs and standardizing trade policies among member states, the Zollverein fostered economic integration. It proved that German unity offered tangible benefits, making political unification seem logical to many Germans.

The two most dramatic examples of this transformation were the unifications of Italy and Germany. Throughout the mid-nineteenth century, Italian patriots sought to knit their disparate regions into one kingdom. Leaders like the revolutionary Giuseppe Mazzini, the soldier Giuseppe Garibaldi and Count Camillo di Cavour, chief minister of Piedmont-Sardinia, harnessed the spirit of nationalism to challenge foreign control. The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in 1861, and the capture of Rome in 1870 completed unification.

Germany followed a similar, though more militaristic, path. Otto von Bismarck, the Prussian chief minister, saw that nationalism could be a tool for Prussian dominance. Through carefully calculated wars against Denmark in 1864, Austria in 1866 and France in 1870, he consolidated the German states under a single empire. In an 1862 speech he declared that the great questions of the day would be decided not by speeches and majority votes but by iron and blood. The proclamation of the German Empire in 1871 fundamentally shifted the balance of power in Europe, creating a new industrial giant in the heart of the continent.

While nationalism brought people together in Italy and Germany, it had the opposite effect on the multi-ethnic empires of the era. Consider the Austrian Empire, later known as the Austro-Hungarian Empire, and the Ottoman Empire. These states were home to dozens of different ethnic and linguistic groups, including Hungarians, Czechs, Serbs, and Greeks. Nationalism acted as a solvent, eating away at the foundations of these sprawling, diverse realms. As these groups began to define themselves as distinct nations, they questioned why they should remain subjects of a distant emperor in Vienna or Istanbul.

This destabilization created a volatile environment. As minority groups demanded autonomy or independence, central authorities often reacted with repression. This friction turned the Balkans, in particular, into what many historians called a powder keg, where competing nationalist aspirations threatened to spark wider conflicts. The idea of the nation-state, which promised self-determination for some, became a source of existential dread for the multi-ethnic empires that had governed for centuries.

It is important to remember that nationalism was not a static, harmless force. It transformed the map of the world by replacing traditional loyalties to a throne or a religion with a primary loyalty to the nation. This created a new kind of citizen, one who felt a personal stake in the success of their state. However, it also fostered a competitive, us-versus-them mentality. As states unified and grew more powerful, they began to look outward, measuring their strength against their neighbors and seeking to extend their influence far beyond their own borders. By the late nineteenth century, the success of the nation-state model had fundamentally changed global politics, setting the stage for a new era of international competition.

## Key ideas

- Nationalism is the ideology that people who share a common language, culture, and history should be united within a single, sovereign nation-state.
- The Congress of Vienna in 1815 left Italy and the German states fragmented, creating the political conditions that nineteenth-century nationalists sought to overturn.
- Economic integration, such as the Prussian-led Zollverein, provided a practical foundation for German unification by linking the interests of separate states.
- The unifications of Italy and Germany demonstrated how nationalist sentiment could consolidate fragmented regions into powerful new states.
- Nationalism destabilized multi-ethnic empires by encouraging ethnic minorities to seek independence, creating significant geopolitical tension in regions like the Balkans.
