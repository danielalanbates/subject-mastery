# Humanistic and Social-Cognitive Approaches

Imagine you are looking at a garden. If you want a plant to thrive, you do not just study its past traumas or its hidden, dark roots. Instead, you provide the right conditions—sunlight, water, and space—and trust that the plant has an innate tendency to grow toward the light. This is the core metaphor behind the humanistic approach to personality. While our last lesson explored the psychodynamic tradition, which focused heavily on unconscious conflicts and the baggage of childhood, humanistic psychologists in the mid-twentieth century felt that this view was too pessimistic. They argued that psychology should instead focus on the human potential for growth and the importance of our conscious, subjective experience.

Carl Rogers was a central figure in this movement. He believed that every individual possesses an actualizing tendency, an innate drive to reach their full potential. For Rogers, personality is not a fixed set of conflicts, but a process of becoming. He argued that to grow, people need an environment that provides genuineness, acceptance, and empathy. He called this unconditional positive regard. When we feel that we are valued even when we make mistakes, we are more likely to develop a healthy self-concept. A healthy personality, in Rogers' view, occurs when our real self—who we actually are—is closely aligned with our ideal self—the person we wish to become. When these two selves are far apart, people often experience significant anxiety and distress.

Abraham Maslow offered a similar, optimistic view. He studied healthy, high-achieving individuals rather than focusing on pathology. Maslow proposed that human needs are organized in a hierarchy, starting with deficiency needs, such as physiological and safety requirements, and moving up toward growth needs, culminating in self-actualization. While Maslow’s theory is famous for its intuitive appeal, it is important to note that the scientific evidence for a strict, universal hierarchy is limited. Modern researchers find that people often pursue higher-level goals even when their basic needs are not perfectly met, and the path to self-actualization is rarely as linear as the classic pyramid suggests.

As the century progressed, researchers began to favor more empirical methods, leading to the rise of the social-cognitive perspective. This approach bridges the gap between behavioral learning theories and internal cognitive processes. Albert Bandura was a pioneer here, shifting the focus to how our environment and our internal thought processes interact. He introduced the concept of reciprocal determinism, which suggests that our personality is shaped by the constant back-and-forth between our behaviors, our internal cognitive factors, and our environmental context.

A major contribution of Bandura’s work is observational learning, or modeling. Through his famous Bobo doll experiments, Bandura demonstrated that people learn by watching others. We do not need to experience every consequence ourselves to change our behavior; we can acquire new responses simply by observing the actions of others and the outcomes they receive. This process is a foundational element of the social-cognitive perspective.

Building on this, Bandura emphasized self-efficacy. This refers to your internal belief in your own ability to succeed in specific situations. If you have high self-efficacy regarding your academic skills, you are more likely to persist through a difficult assignment rather than giving up. This is not just positive thinking; it is a measurable cognitive state that influences how we set goals and how we handle failure. Research consistently shows that self-efficacy is a strong predictor of performance across many domains.

Julian Rotter added another layer to this perspective with his concept of locus of control. This describes our belief about whether the outcomes of our lives are controlled by our own actions or by external forces like luck, fate, or powerful others. People with an internal locus of control believe they are the masters of their own destiny. Those with an external locus of control believe that life happens to them, rather than because of them. While an internal locus of control is generally associated with better mental health outcomes, research suggests that the reality is nuanced. Sometimes, recognizing the limits of our control is actually a healthy, adaptive response to uncontrollable stressors.

Both the humanistic and social-cognitive schools shifted psychology away from the deterministic views of the early twentieth century. Humanistic theories provide a compelling framework for understanding the drive for personal growth, though they have been criticized for being difficult to test scientifically. Social-cognitive theories are more grounded in experimental evidence, offering a clear view of how our thoughts and environments shape who we are. By moving beyond the internal dramas of the psychodynamic tradition, these perspectives help us see personality as a dynamic, changing system. As we transition to our next lesson, we will see how psychologists attempt to categorize these complex, individual differences into broad, stable traits that describe the consistency of human behavior over time.

## Key ideas
- Humanistic psychology, led by Rogers and Maslow, emphasizes the innate human drive toward growth and the importance of a positive self-concept.
- Maslow distinguished between deficiency needs and growth needs, though empirical evidence does not support a rigid, universal hierarchy of these needs.
- Bandura’s social-cognitive perspective highlights observational learning and self-efficacy as core drivers of human behavior and motivation.
- Rotter’s locus of control theory explains how our perception of control—whether internal or external—shapes how we navigate life’s challenges.
- While humanistic theories are valued for their focus on human potential, social-cognitive approaches are generally better supported by contemporary experimental research.
