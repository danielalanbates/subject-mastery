# Price Elasticity of Demand

Imagine you run a local coffee shop and decide to raise the price of your signature latte by twenty percent. You know from the law of demand that sales will drop, but by how much? Will you lose just a few loyal regulars, or will a stampede of customers flee to the diner across the street, destroying your daily revenue? Knowing the direction of a price change is not enough in economics; you need to measure the exact degree of consumer responsiveness. Building on our previous look at how markets settle through supply and demand, we now need a tool that tells us how buyers react when a price shifts.

That tool is called price elasticity of demand. In plain terms, it is a numerical measure of how much the quantity demanded of a good changes when its price changes. Economists calculate this by dividing the percentage change in the quantity demanded by the percentage change in the price. If consumers drastically alter their buying habits following a price nudge, we say the demand is elastic. Think of a rubber band stretched wide with very little effort. If, however, consumers keep buying nearly the exact same amount even after a massive price hike, we call the demand inelastic, much like a thick piece of wire that refuses to bend.

Why do some goods stretch like rubber while others stay rigid? Four primary factors determine price elasticity of demand. The first is the availability of close substitutes. Goods with many alternatives, such as a specific brand of cereal, have elastic demand because buyers can easily switch if prices rise. The second is whether the item is a luxury or a necessity. Necessities like basic food have inelastic demand, whereas luxuries like luxury cruises have elastic demand. The third factor is the proportion of income spent on the good. A salt purchase takes up a tiny fraction of a household budget, making its demand inelastic, while buying a house consumes a massive share, making buyers very sensitive to price swings. The fourth factor is the time horizon. Consumers need time to find alternatives, so demand is usually more elastic over the long run than in the short run.

Let us look at a simple worked example with round numbers. Suppose a neighborhood movie theater charges ten dollars per ticket, and it sells one thousand tickets on a typical weekend. The owners decide to raise the price by ten percent, bringing the ticket price to eleven dollars. Because moviegoers have plenty of other entertainment options, attendance drops by twenty percent, meaning they now sell only eight hundred tickets. To find the price elasticity of demand, we divide the negative twenty percent change in quantity by the positive ten percent change in price. That gives us a raw calculation of negative two.

Economists usually drop the negative sign when discussing elasticity because the law of demand ensures that price and quantity move in opposite directions, so the number is understood to be negative. An elasticity value greater than one means demand is elastic, showing that consumers are sensitive to price changes. In our movie theater example, the elasticity of two tells us that the percentage drop in sales was twice as large as the percentage rise in price. Because the drop in volume outweighed the higher price per ticket, the theater's total revenue actually fell: ten thousand dollars became eight thousand eight hundred. This reveals a vital business rule: when demand is elastic, raising prices hurts total revenue, while lowering prices can boost it.

On the flip side, consider a product with few or no substitutes, such as a life-saving prescription medication. If a pharmacy raises the price of this medicine by ten percent, patients will pay it because their health depends on it, causing the quantity demanded to fall by only two percent. Dividing negative two percent by positive ten percent gives us an elasticity of zero point two. Because this number is less than one, demand is inelastic, meaning consumers are relatively unresponsive to price changes. For inelastic goods, raising the price increases total revenue because the extra money collected from each unit sold makes up for the few lost customers.

When the percentage change in quantity matches the percentage change in price exactly, the elasticity equals one, a condition known as unit elasticity. While pure unit elasticity is rare in the real world, it serves as a helpful boundary line between elastic and inelastic behaviors. Understanding where a product falls on this spectrum helps businesses set profitable prices and helps governments predict the revenue impacts of taxes. Yet price is not the only factor that dictates how consumers react, and other market forces can twist these measurements in surprising ways. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will explore other types of elasticity, looking at how consumer choices shift in response to changes in consumer income and the prices of related goods.

## Key ideas
- Price elasticity of demand measures how much consumer buying responds to changes in the price of a good or service.
- It is calculated by dividing the percentage change in quantity demanded by the percentage change in price.
- Demand is elastic when consumers are highly responsive to price changes, resulting in an elasticity value greater than one.
- Demand is inelastic when consumers show little responsiveness to price changes, yielding an elasticity value of less than one.
- Key determinants of price elasticity include the availability of substitutes, whether a good is a necessity or luxury, the proportion of income spent, and the time horizon.
