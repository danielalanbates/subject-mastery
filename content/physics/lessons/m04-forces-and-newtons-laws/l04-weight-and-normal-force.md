# Weight and Normal Force

Imagine standing on a bathroom scale while holding a heavy suitcase. The number on the display jumps upward, not because you have suddenly grown larger, but because you are pressing harder against the mechanism beneath your feet. In our everyday language, we often use the words mass and weight interchangeably. We say a person weighs seventy kilograms. In the language of physics, however, these two concepts are fundamentally distinct. Building on our recent look at how forces pair up and push against one another, we must now separate the intrinsic amount of matter in an object from the environmental pull of gravity acting upon it.

Mass is an intrinsic property of an object. It represents the quantity of matter contained within that object and serves as a measure of its inertia, which is its resistance to any change in motion. Your mass remains exactly the same whether you are standing on the surface of the Earth, floating in the microgravity of orbit, or resting on the surface of the moon. It is measured in kilograms. Weight, on the other hand, is a force. It is the gravitational force exerted on an object by a planet or another massive body. Because weight is a force, it is measured in newtons.

To find the magnitude of an object's weight, you multiply its mass by the local acceleration due to gravity. Using nine point eight meters per second squared for the gravitational acceleration near the Earth's surface, an object with a mass of ten kilograms experiences a downward gravitational pull of ninety-eight newtons. If you take that same ten-kilogram object to the moon, where the gravitational acceleration is roughly one-sixth as strong, its mass remains ten kilograms, but its weight drops to about sixteen newtons. Weight depends entirely on where you are in the universe.

When you stand completely still on a floor, gravity pulls you downward toward the center of the Earth with a force equal to your weight. Yet you do not accelerate downward through the floorboards. Why? Because the floor pushes back upward against you. This upward push is known as the normal force. The word normal in this scientific context does not mean ordinary or usual. It comes from geometry, where normal means perpendicular. The normal force is always directed perpendicular to the surface of contact, pushing outward from the surface to support any object resting upon it.

Consider a textbook resting flat on a wooden table. Gravity pulls the book downward with a force of twenty newtons. The book does not fall because the table exerts an upward normal force of twenty newtons on the book. These two forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, balancing each other out so that the net force on the book is zero. This situation is a classic application of equilibrium. Notice that these two forces do not form an action and reaction pair from the third law of motion. Gravity acting on the book and the normal force from the table act on the same object, whereas action and reaction pairs always act on two different interacting objects.

Bathroom scales and laboratory scales do not measure your gravitational weight directly. Instead, they measure the contact force between your feet and the scale, which equals the normal force pushing up on you and is commonly referred to as your apparent weight. When you stand completely still, this normal force equals your true weight. But the normal force equals weight only when there is no vertical acceleration. On an inclined surface, or when an object accelerates downward, the normal force can be less than the true weight. In free fall, with no contact force at all, the normal force drops to zero, and you experience apparent weightlessness.

Now let us walk through a quick calculation involving an elevator. Suppose a person with a mass of sixty kilograms steps into an elevator that remains stationary. Their true weight is sixty kilograms multiplied by nine point eight meters per second squared, which gives five hundred eighty-eight newtons downward. The floor of the elevator pushes up with a normal force of five hundred eighty-eight newtons, balancing gravity. If the elevator suddenly begins to accelerate upward at two meters per second squared, the normal force must adjust to satisfy the condition that the net force equals mass times acceleration.

To find this new normal force in the accelerating elevator, we use the second law of motion. The net upward force is the normal force minus the downward weight. Setting this equal to sixty kilograms times two meters per second squared gives a required net force of one hundred twenty newtons. Adding the person's true weight of five hundred eighty-eight newtons to this required net force yields a total upward normal force of seven hundred eight newtons. This is why you feel heavier, or feel your knees compress, when an elevator accelerates upward from the ground floor. The scale beneath you reads this higher normal force as your apparent weight.

Understanding how surfaces push back against objects opens the door to exploring what happens when surfaces slide past one another. ## Key ideas
- Mass is an intrinsic measure of an object's matter and inertia, measured in kilograms, and it never changes based on location.
- Weight is the gravitational force acting on an object, measured in newtons, and it varies depending on the local gravitational acceleration.
- A scale measures apparent weight, which is the normal force, not the gravitational force directly.
- The normal force can be less than weight when an object accelerates downward or is on an inclined surface, and it becomes zero in free fall.
- The normal force equals weight only when there is no vertical acceleration, and otherwise adjusts to satisfy the net force equals mass times acceleration.
