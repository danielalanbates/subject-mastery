# Formation of the Canon

Imagine you are curating a library intended to define the identity of an entire people for thousands of years. You have hundreds of scrolls, letters, and historical accounts at your disposal. How do you decide which ones carry the weight of divine authority and which ones are merely helpful reflections? This is the challenge of the biblical canon. The word canon comes from a Greek term meaning a measuring rod or standard. When we talk about the formation of the canon, we are exploring the historical process by which certain texts were recognized as the authoritative rule of faith for the Jewish and Christian communities.

The Old Testament canon did not emerge overnight. The Jewish Tanakh, or Hebrew Bible, consists of twenty-four books divided into the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings. While the Law and the Prophets were widely settled by the time of Jesus, the Writings remained somewhat more fluid. The early church inherited the Greek translation of these scriptures, known as the Septuagint. This collection included additional books that were not part of the standard Hebrew Tanakh.

These additional books, often called the deuterocanonical books, include works such as Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Baruch, and the books of the Maccabees. The Eastern Orthodox tradition includes these and even more, such as First Esdras, the Prayer of Manasseh, and Psalm one hundred fifty-one. Roman Catholic tradition formally defined these deuterocanonical books as part of the canon during the Council of Trent in 1546. In contrast, Protestant reformers sought to align their Old Testament canon with the twenty-four books of the Hebrew Tanakh, resulting in a thirty-nine-book collection. They viewed the deuterocanonical books as valuable for historical and moral instruction but not as authoritative for establishing church doctrine.

The New Testament canon followed a similar path of recognition rather than invention. The early church did not gather in a boardroom to vote these books into existence. Instead, they recognized the authority of writings that were already circulating in their worship life. By the late fourth century, the church reached a broad consensus. Documents like the Muratorian fragment and Athanasius’s Festal Letter written in the year three hundred sixty-seven helped solidify this list. Later, the regional Councils of Hippo and Carthage in the late fourth century formally affirmed the twenty-seven books we recognize today.

The church generally applied three criteria for inclusion. First, a book needed to be rooted in the apostolic tradition, meaning it was written by an apostle or someone in their immediate circle, such as Mark or Luke. Second, the message had to align with the rule of faith, which was the core teaching passed down from the beginning. Third, the book had to be widely utilized in the churches across the Mediterranean world, showing that it had proven its value in the life of the faithful.

It is important to remember that Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant churches hold to the same twenty-seven-book New Testament; only a few ancient churches, such as the Ethiopian Orthodox, recognize a wider collection. The differences are confined to the Old Testament and reflect different historical decisions about the boundaries of the ancient Jewish collection. Whether one follows the Catholic, Orthodox, or Protestant tradition, the process reflects a deep commitment to preserving the message that the community believed was inspired by the Spirit of God.

This process of formation is quite distinct from the physical process of copying and preserving those texts. Once the community recognized these books as authoritative, the task shifted to the painstaking work of scribes. They had to ensure that the words were transmitted accurately through centuries of hand-copying. This transition from recognizing the canon to the physical reality of the manuscripts is where we find the next stage of our study. We must move from the question of which books belong in the Bible to the question of how those specific words have reached us today through the long history of manuscript transmission and the rigorous work of textual criticism.

## Key ideas

- The biblical canon is the result of a historical process of recognition by faith communities rather than an arbitrary selection of texts.
- The Jewish Tanakh contains twenty-four books, while Christian traditions vary in their Old Testament counts due to the inclusion or exclusion of the deuterocanonical books.
- The New Testament canon was solidified by the late fourth century through the consensus of the church, as reflected in early lists and regional councils.
- Criteria for New Testament inclusion focused on apostolic origin, consistency with the rule of faith, and widespread usage in Christian worship.
