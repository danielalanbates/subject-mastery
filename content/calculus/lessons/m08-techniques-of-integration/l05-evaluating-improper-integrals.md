# Evaluating Improper Integrals

Imagine painting a fence that stretches on forever. Even if the planks go on infinitely into the distance, could the total amount of paint required still be finite? This question takes us beyond standard definite integrals, where our intervals of integration are closed and bounded, and our functions are well-behaved. In our last lesson, we looked at numerical methods like Simpson's Rule to approximate tricky definite integrals. Today, we confront a different kind of challenge entirely: evaluating integrals that stretch out toward infinity or blow up to infinity right in the middle of our domain. These are called improper integrals, and they reveal a surprising mathematical truth, which is that an infinite region can sometimes have a completely finite area.

To see why this happens, think about the function one over x squared, for any positive x. As x grows larger and larger, the graph of this function hugs the horizontal axis tighter and tighter. Suppose we want to find the total area under this curve starting at the number one and heading outward forever. We cannot simply plug infinity into the fundamental theorem of calculus because infinity is a concept, not a number. Instead, we use limits, which we first explored back when we studied limits at infinity and asymptotes. We replace the infinite upper bound with a temporary variable, let us call it b, and then we take the limit of our result as b approaches infinity.

Let us walk through this specific calculation step by step. We set up our definite integral from one to b of one over x squared, d x. First, we find the antiderivative of one over x squared, which is negative one over x. Next, we evaluate this antiderivative at our limits, substituting b and one. This gives us the quantity negative one over b, minus the quantity negative one over one, which simplifies to one minus one over b. Now, we take the limit of this expression as b grows without bound toward infinity. As b becomes enormous, the fraction one over b shrinks toward zero, leaving us with a final answer of exactly one. The area of this infinitely long region is finite, precisely equal to one.

Not every improper integral behaves so politely. Consider what happens if we change our function slightly to just one over x, again integrating from one to infinity. The antiderivative of one over x is the natural logarithm of the absolute value of x. When we evaluate this from one to b, we get the natural logarithm of b minus the natural logarithm of one, which is just the natural logarithm of b because the natural logarithm of one is zero. As b approaches infinity, the natural logarithm of b also grows without bound, heading straight to infinity. Because this limit does not exist as a finite number, we say that this improper integral diverges, while our first example converged.

The second major type of improper integral occurs when our function has a vertical asymptote hidden inside our interval of integration. Imagine integrating the function one over the square root of x from zero to one. At zero, our function shoots upward toward infinity. To handle this discontinuity, we must approach the dangerous endpoint from a safe distance using a limit. We replace zero with a small positive variable, evaluate the integral from that variable to one, and then take the limit as our variable approaches zero from the right. The antiderivative of one over the square root of x is two times the square root of x. Evaluating this from our small variable to one gives us two times the square root of one, minus two times the square root of our variable. As our variable approaches zero, that second term vanishes, leaving us with a convergent value of two.

Mastering improper integrals requires patience with both limits and antiderivatives. Whether we are stretching our domain out to the stars or squeezing past a vertical asymptote where a function blows up, the strategy remains the same: replace the troublesome spot with a variable, compute the standard definite integral, and then take the limit. As we step into the world of differential equations, these limiting processes will allow us to model continuous change, fading forces, and long term behaviors that stretch across time without end.

## Key ideas
- Improper integrals involve either infinite limits of integration or functions with vertical asymptotes inside the integration interval.
- We evaluate improper integrals by replacing the troublesome bound or discontinuity with a variable and taking a limit.
- An improper integral converges if its limit evaluates to a finite number, and diverges if the limit fails to exist or equals infinity.
- The integral of one over x squared from one to infinity converges to one, while the integral of one over x from one to infinity diverges.
