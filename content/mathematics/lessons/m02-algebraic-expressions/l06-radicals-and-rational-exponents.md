# Radicals and Rational Exponents

Imagine you are standing at a workbench, and you need to cut a piece of wood whose area is precisely ten square units. If the piece is a square, how long is each side? It cannot be an integer, because three times three is nine, and four times four is sixteen. To name that exact side length, we need a mathematical tool that undoes squaring. That tool is the square root. Now, we turn our attention to radicals and fractional exponents, expanding how we write and simplify algebraic quantities.

A radical expression consists of a radical sign, a number or expression underneath called the radicand, and sometimes a small raised number in the crook of the sign called the index. When no index is written, it is understood to be a two, representing the square root. The principal nth root is the primary real root of a number. When working with real numbers, even-index radicals require non-negative radicands, because no real number multiplied by itself an even number of times can yield a negative result. For instance, the principal square root of nine is three, not negative three.

Simplifying radicals relies on breaking numbers down and pulling out factors that match the index. Consider the square root of eighteen. We can rewrite eighteen as the product of nine times two. Because nine is a square number, its square root is three. We can split the square root of the product into the product of the square roots, giving us the square root of nine times the square root of three, which simplifies to three times the square root of two. When we multiply or divide two radicals, we combine their radicands, provided they share the same index. We can also add and subtract like radicals, which are radical terms that share the exact same index and radicand, by combining their outer coefficients just like standard variable terms.

Sometimes an algebraic expression contains a radical in the denominator of a fraction, which we remove through rationalizing the denominator. If we have one divided by the square root of three, we multiply both the numerator and the denominator by the square root of three. The numerator becomes the square root of three, and the denominator becomes three, because the square root of three times the square root of three is three. For denominators containing binomial radicals, such as the quantity two plus the square root of three, we multiply both the numerator and denominator by the conjugate, which is the same expression with the middle sign reversed, yielding the quantity two minus the square root of three.

Roots and exponents are two sides of the same coin, and we can translate freely between radical form and rational exponent form. The square root of a variable, say x, can be written as x raised to the power of one half. The cube root of x squared can be written as x raised to the power of two over three, where the numerator of the fraction is the power inside the radical and the denominator is the index of the root. Converting between radical and exponent forms yields equivalent expressions, but with even indices, taking an even power of a root requires absolute values, such as the principal square root of x squared equalling the absolute value of x.

The full set of exponent laws applies equally to rational exponents. When multiplying terms with the same base, we add the exponents. When dividing terms with the same base, we subtract the exponent of the denominator from the exponent of the numerator. Raising a power to a power requires multiplying the exponents. A negative rational exponent indicates the reciprocal of the base raised to the corresponding positive power. Whenever we work with even roots or rational exponents, we must remain mindful of domain restrictions to ensure our resulting values stay within the realm of real numbers.

## Key ideas
- A radical expression uses an index and a radicand to find the principal nth root, requiring non-negative radicands for even-index radicals to produce real results.
- Simplifying a radical involves factoring out perfect powers from the radicand, and like radicals with identical indices and radicands can be added or subtracted by combining their coefficients.
- Rationalizing denominators with single radicals or binomial radicals using conjugates eliminates radical expressions from the bottom of a fraction.
- Radical and rational exponent forms are fully interchangeable, and converting between them follows strict rules such as the principal square root of x squared equalling the absolute value of x.
- The full set of exponent laws governs rational exponents, allowing us to multiply, divide, raise powers, and handle negative exponents while respecting domain restrictions for even roots.
