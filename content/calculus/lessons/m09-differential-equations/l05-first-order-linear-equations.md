# First Order Linear Equations

Imagine trying to keep track of a bank account balance where money enters as a steady monthly salary, but leaves at a rate that depends on the current balance, perhaps because of a variable interest rate or a dynamic fee structure. In our previous look at population models, we saw how logistic growth curves handle changing environments, but what happens when an external driving force acts directly on a changing system? That is the territory of first order linear differential equations. They show up whenever a rate of change is proportional to the current state plus some additional forcing function that depends only on the independent variable, like time.

A first order differential equation is called linear when the unknown function and its derivative appear only to the first power, without any products of the function with itself or with its derivative. Standard form places the derivative of y with respect to x plus a coefficient function times y on the left side of the equation, equal to a separate function of x on the right side. If that right side function is zero, the equation is homogeneous, meaning no external force is at play. But when a nonzero function sits on the right side, solving the equation by separation of variables usually fails, because in general the terms refuse to factor neatly.

To solve this puzzle, mathematicians introduced a brilliant device called the integrating factor. Think of it as a mathematical multiplier that transforms the left side of our equation into an exact derivative through the magic of the product rule for differentiation. Suppose we multiply every term in our standard equation by a special positive function of x, which we will call mu. If we choose mu cleverly, the entire left side of our modified equation collapses into the derivative of the product of mu and y with respect to x.

What is the exact recipe for this integrating factor? It turns out that mu must be equal to e raised to the power of the integral of our coefficient function of x, d x. When you take the derivative of that exponential expression with respect to x, the chain rule brings down the coefficient function itself, which provides precisely the mechanism needed to make the product rule work in reverse. Once you multiply the entire differential equation by this integrating factor, the left side becomes a single derivative expression, and the right side becomes the product of the integrating factor and our original forcing function.

Let us walk through a concrete example step by step to see how this machinery operates. Consider the differential equation where the derivative of y with respect to x plus two y equals four, with x greater than zero. Here, our coefficient function multiplying y is the constant two, and our forcing function on the right side is the constant four. To find our integrating factor, we integrate two with respect to x, which gives two x, and then we raise e to that power. Thus, our integrating factor mu is e raised to the two x power.

Next, we multiply every term in our original differential equation by e raised to the two x power. The left side becomes e raised to the two x times the derivative of y, plus two times e raised to the two x times y. By our design, this entire expression is equal to the derivative of the product of e raised to the two x and y with respect to x. The right side of our equation is now four times e raised to the two x. Now, we integrate both sides of the equation with respect to x. The left side simply strips away the derivative, leaving e raised to the two x times y. The right side requires a simple integration, yielding two times e raised to the two x, plus our constant of integration c.

To finish solving for y, we divide both terms on the right side by our integrating factor, e raised to the two x. This leaves us with y equals two plus c times e raised to the negative two x. We can check our work easily. If we take this resulting expression for y and differentiate it, the constant two disappears, and the derivative of c times e raised to the negative two x is negative two c times e raised to the negative two x. Substituting this back into the original left side, y prime plus two y, we get negative two c times e raised to the negative two x plus two times the quantity two plus c times e raised to the negative two x, which simplifies neatly to four, confirming our solution is correct. This method unlocks a massive class of physical and economic models, paving the way for us to shift our gaze next toward understanding infinite sequences and their limits.

## Key ideas
- A first order linear differential equation features the unknown function and its derivative to the first power, coupled with coefficient functions of the independent variable.
- The integrating factor is an exponential function derived from the integral of the coefficient of y, designed to force the product rule of differentiation in reverse.
- Multiplying the entire standard equation by the integrating factor transforms the left side into a single exact derivative, making integration possible.
- Dividing the resulting integrated equation by the integrating factor yields the explicit general solution, which includes a constant determined by initial conditions.
