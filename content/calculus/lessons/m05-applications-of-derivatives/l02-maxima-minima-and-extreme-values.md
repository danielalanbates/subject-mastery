# Maxima, Minima and Extreme Values

Imagine you are designing a sleek metal can to hold a fixed volume of soda. You want to use the absolute minimum amount of aluminum to save money and reduce waste. Or picture a pilot plotting a flight path who needs to find the exact altitude where air resistance drops to its lowest possible point. In both situations, you are hunting for peaks and valleys, the high points and low points that govern how systems behave. Building directly on our recent work with tangent lines, calculus gives us a precise lens to spot these extreme values without needing to test every single possibility by hand.

The invention of this powerful branch of mathematics was achieved independently by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in the seventeenth century, sparking a bitter priority dispute across the academic world that lasted for decades. Newton introduced fluxions and used dot notation to represent rates of change, while Leibniz introduced differential ratios and the fraction style notation we use for derivatives today. Together, their foundational work unlocked the mathematics of continuous change.

To talk about these highs and lows, we need clear vocabulary. An absolute maximum is the single largest value a function reaches across its entire domain, while an absolute minimum is the absolute lowest. Sometimes a point is not the highest in the entire universe of the function, but it is the highest in its immediate neighborhood, sitting proudly at the top of a local hill. We call that a local maximum, and its counterpart at the bottom of a local dip is a local minimum. Together, these are known as extrema.

How do we find them? Think about standing on a smooth, rolling hill. At the very peak, your feet rest on a spot that is momentarily flat. If you take a step forward, you start going down; if you step backward, you were going up. At that exact peak, the slope of the hill is zero. In the language of calculus, where slopes are given by derivatives, a local maximum or local minimum can only occur where the derivative is zero or where the derivative fails to exist. We call these special locations critical numbers. Notice that endpoints of an interval are not classified as critical numbers, and they must always be evaluated separately when we search for absolute extrema.

Once we find our critical numbers, we often need to classify them to see whether they are local peaks or valleys. The first derivative test examines the sign of the derivative to the left and right of a critical number, seeing whether the function changes from increasing to decreasing, or vice versa. Alternatively, the second derivative test evaluates the concavity at the critical number, where a positive second derivative signals a local minimum and a negative second derivative signals a local maximum.

Let us watch this work in a concrete example. Consider the function f of x equals three x cubed minus nine x, defined on the closed interval from negative three to positive three. First, we find the derivative, f prime of x, which equals nine x squared minus nine. To find our critical numbers, we set this derivative equal to zero. Solving nine x squared minus nine equals zero gives us x squared equals one, meaning our critical numbers are x equals one and x equals negative one. Both of these sit comfortably inside our starting interval.

Now, where are the highest and lowest values on the whole interval? This is where the extreme value theorem becomes our greatest tool. The theorem guarantees that if a function is continuous on a closed interval, it must attain both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum somewhere on that interval. To find them, we only need to check three places: the critical numbers inside the interval, and the two endpoints of the interval.

Let us evaluate our function at these key spots. At our left endpoint, negative three, evaluating three times negative three cubed minus nine times negative three gives us negative fifty-four. At our right endpoint, positive three, the function evaluates to positive fifty-four. Now let us check our critical numbers. At x equals one, the function evaluates to negative six. At x equals negative one, the function evaluates to positive six. Comparing all four results, our absolute maximum is positive fifty-four at the right endpoint, and our absolute minimum is negative fifty-four at the left endpoint. Meanwhile, the second derivative, eighteen x, is negative at negative one and positive at one, so our local maximum lives at negative one with a height of six, and our local minimum sits at positive one with a height of negative six.

Understanding how these peaks and valleys connect across an interval opens the door to deeper truths about motion and change. As we move into our next lesson, we will explore the mean value theorem, which connects the average rate of change of a function over an interval to the instantaneous rate of change at a single point hidden inside it.

## Key ideas
- An absolute extremum is the highest or lowest value a function reaches across its entire domain, while local extrema are peaks and valleys relative to their immediate neighborhoods.
- Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently developed calculus in the seventeenth century, introducing distinct notations that sparked a bitter priority dispute.
- Critical numbers occur where the derivative of a function is equal to zero or where the derivative does not exist, though endpoints are evaluated separately and are not classified as critical numbers.
- The first and second derivative tests allow mathematicians to classify critical points as local maxima or minima by examining slopes and concavity.
- The extreme value theorem guarantees that any continuous function on a closed interval will attain both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum, which are found by checking both critical numbers and endpoints.
