# The Bohr Model and Line Spectra

Have you ever watched a fireworks display and wondered why the sparks glow in such specific, vibrant colors? A deep crimson red, a brilliant emerald green, or a piercing royal blue. Each of these colors is a signature, a chemical fingerprint left by the elements inside the firework. When we heat these elements, they do not glow with a smooth, continuous rainbow of light like the sun or a white-hot lightbulb filament. Instead, they emit very specific, discrete bands of color. This phenomenon is known as a line spectrum, and it contains the secret to understanding how electrons behave inside an atom.

In our previous lesson, we learned that light travels as waves and carries energy in discrete packets called photons. If we pass the light from an excited gas through a prism, we see only a few sharp lines of color, separated by darkness. This means the atoms are only releasing light at specific, set energy levels. If atoms could release any amount of energy, we would see a blurry, continuous smear of all the colors of the rainbow. The fact that we see distinct lines tells us that the energy of an electron inside an atom is quantized.

In nineteen thirteen, Niels Bohr proposed a model to explain this behavior, specifically using the hydrogen atom. Bohr imagined the atom like a tiny solar system. He suggested that electrons orbit the nucleus at fixed distances, much like planets orbiting a star. These fixed paths are called energy levels or shells. Bohr theorized that an electron cannot exist between these levels; it must either be in one shell or another. When an atom absorbs energy, an electron jumps from a lower energy level to a higher one. This is an unstable, excited state. Almost immediately, the electron falls back down to its original, lower energy level. When it falls, it releases that exact difference in energy as a photon of light.

Think of it like a ladder. You can stand on the first rung or the second rung, but you cannot stand in the air between them. If you jump from the second rung to the first, you release a specific amount of potential energy. The color of the light we see corresponds to the size of that jump. A large jump releases high-energy light, like violet or blue, while a smaller jump releases lower-energy light, like red. Because each element has a unique arrangement of energy levels, each element produces its own unique set of jumps and, consequently, its own unique line spectrum.

Let us look at a quick example. If an electron in a hydrogen atom moves from the third energy level down to the second, it releases a specific photon of red light. If it moves from the fourth level down to the second, the energy difference is greater, so it emits a photon of blue-green light. By measuring these exact wavelengths of light, Bohr was able to calculate the energy of these levels with remarkable accuracy for hydrogen. He had successfully applied the concept of quantization to the structure of the atom.

However, science often moves forward by discovering the limits of a successful theory. While the Bohr model worked beautifully for hydrogen, which has only one electron, it failed miserably for any atom with two or more electrons. The model could not account for the complex repulsions between multiple electrons, and the math fell apart as soon as the system became more complicated. It turns out that electrons do not move in neat, circular orbits like planets. They are far more mysterious and behave more like blurry clouds of probability.

The Bohr model was eventually replaced by the more sophisticated framework of quantum mechanics. Even though the idea of "orbits" was discarded, Bohr’s core insight remained: the energy of an electron in an atom is indeed quantized. He gave us the vital starting point for understanding that electrons exist in specific states. This set the stage for us to move beyond simple circles and explore the complex, three-dimensional shapes where electrons actually reside. In our next lesson, we will dive into those shapes and the quantum numbers that define the modern view of the atom.

## Key ideas

- Elements emit light at specific, discrete wavelengths when heated, creating a unique line spectrum that acts as a chemical fingerprint.
- The Bohr model explains these spectra by proposing that electrons exist in fixed, quantized energy levels around the nucleus.
- Light is emitted when an electron drops from a higher energy level to a lower one, releasing energy equal to the difference between those states.
- The Bohr model is limited because it only accurately predicts the behavior of the single-electron hydrogen atom and fails for more complex atoms.
- Modern quantum mechanics replaced Bohr's circular orbits with a more accurate description of electrons as existing in defined, three-dimensional regions of space.
