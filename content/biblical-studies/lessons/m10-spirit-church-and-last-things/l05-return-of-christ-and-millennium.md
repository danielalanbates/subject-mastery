# The Return of Christ and the Millennium

Imagine looking at a distant mountain range through the haze of a morning mist. Two peaks that look right next to each other from your viewpoint might actually be separated by a vast, hidden valley miles wide. Christian eschatology, the theological study of the end times, often views the first and second comings of Jesus Christ in much that same way. In our last lesson, we explored what happens to individual believers at death and during the intermediate state while awaiting the ultimate restoration of the cosmos. Today, we turn our gaze from personal destiny to the grand canvas of cosmic history, focusing specifically on the return of Christ and the famous thousand-year period mentioned in the twentieth chapter of the Book of Revelation, verses one through six.

That mysterious period, known as the millennium, has generated some of the most vibrant and careful debates in church history. When Revelation speaks of binding Satan and believers reigning with Christ for a thousand years, how should we read it? Are we looking at a literal, future political kingdom on earth, or is this symbolic language describing the current spiritual reign of Christ through His church? It is worth noting that the millennium is not explicitly addressed in the historic ecumenical creeds, such as the Apostles' Creed and the Nicene Creed. These historic texts affirm that Christ will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead, but they leave the exact timing and nature of the millennium open to theological discussion. Christian theologians across the centuries have answered these questions through three major frameworks, known as premillennialism, amillennialism, and postmillennialism. Each view reads scripture with deep reverence, yet arranges the sequence of biblical prophecies quite differently.

The first major view is premillennialism. Adherents of this tradition believe that Jesus Christ will return physically to earth before the millennium begins. In this perspective, the present age will experience a worsening of moral crisis and tribulation, culminating in the dramatic, visible arrival of Christ. Once He returns, He will establish a literal earthly kingdom, binding Satan and reigning from Jerusalem for a thousand years of peace and righteousness. Historic premillennialism, held by early church fathers such as Justin Martyr and Irenaeus and by modern scholars such as George Eldon Ladd, sees this earthly reign as the literal fulfillment of Old Testament prophecies. A variation known as dispensational premillennialism, popularized in the nineteenth century, adds a pretribulation rapture of the church, drawing on passages like First Thessalonians chapter four, verses sixteen and seventeen, before a seven-year period of tribulation on earth. Across these variations, the unifying core of premillennialism is simple: Christ comes first, and then the thousand-year kingdom follows.

The second view is amillennialism, which translates literally to "no millennium," though a better description is realized millennialism. Amillennialists argue that the thousand-year period in Revelation is a symbolic number representing the entire present church age, the vast stretch of time between the ascension of Christ and His final return. This framework emphasizes an already-not-yet tension, meaning the kingdom of God is already inaugurated in the hearts of believers and through the ministry of the church, but not yet fully consummated in visible power. In this view, Satan is currently bound in the sense that his power to deceive the nations is restrained, and believers right now reign spiritually with Christ in the heavenly realms. Shaped profoundly by the fifth-century theologian Saint Augustine, amillennialism became the traditional majority view of Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Lutheran, and Reformed theologians for centuries. Christ will make His single, visible return at the very end of this age, bringing history to its climax with the general resurrection, the final judgment, and the eternal state of the new heavens and new earth.

The third framework is postmillennialism, which teaches that Christ will return after the golden age of the millennium. Postmillennialists are remarkably hopeful about the power of the gospel. They believe that through the steady expansion of the Christian message and the work of the Holy Spirit, the world will gradually become more Christianized, bringing about a prolonged era of peace, justice, and Christian ethics that fulfills the imagery of the millennium. Christ does not need to rule an earthly kingdom in person during this time because His church, empowered by grace, transforms culture. This view enjoyed immense popularity during the nineteenth-century missionary expansion, though its cultural optimism declined significantly in the wake of the devastating world wars of the twentieth century. Only after this long, flourishing era of Christian influence will Christ return physically, closing the age with the final judgment and the eternal state.

Navigating these distinct interpretations requires both humility and intellectual charity. Faithful, scripture-loving Christians have landed in each of these camps, agreeing on the central creeds of the faith while differing on the timing and mechanics of prophetic fulfillment.

## Key ideas
- Christian eschatology interprets the return of Christ and the prophetic texts of Revelation, particularly chapter twenty, verses one through six, through three major frameworks known as premillennial, amillennial, and postmillennial views.
- Because the millennium is not addressed in the historic ecumenical creeds, these frameworks represent theological opinions rather than dogmas of the Christian faith.
- Premillennialism holds that Christ will return physically before a literal thousand-year earthly reign, a view that includes both historic church father traditions and nineteenth-century dispensational variations featuring a pretribulation rapture.
- Amillennialism, shaped profoundly by Saint Augustine and embraced widely in Catholic, Orthodox, and classical Protestant theology, interprets the millennium symbolically as the present church age, emphasizing an already-not-yet tension in the reign of Christ.
- Postmillennialism anticipates that the global spread of the gospel will gradually bring about an era of Christian flourishing and peace, with Christ returning after this millennial golden age to inaugurate the final judgment and the eternal state.
