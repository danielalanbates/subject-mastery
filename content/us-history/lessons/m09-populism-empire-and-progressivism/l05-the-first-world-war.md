# The United States in World War One

In the summer of 1914, most Americans viewed the unfolding conflict in Europe as a distant tragedy that had little to do with them. President Woodrow Wilson, building on the Progressive tradition of focusing on domestic reform, urged his fellow citizens to be neutral in thought as well as in action. The United States was a nation of immigrants with deep cultural ties to both the Allied and Central Powers, and the administration feared that choosing sides would tear the American social fabric apart. Yet, as the war dragged on, the reality of global interdependence made isolation difficult. The sinking of the British ocean liner Lusitania by a German submarine in 1915, which claimed over one hundred American lives, began to shift public opinion. By early 1917, Germany’s decision to resume unrestricted submarine warfare—targeting any ship approaching Britain—coupled with the interception of the Zimmermann Telegram, a German proposal for an alliance with Mexico against the United States, made entry into the war appear both inevitable and necessary to protect American interests.

When Congress declared war in April 1917, the United States was largely unprepared for a global struggle. The government had to mobilize the economy and the military on an unprecedented scale. Through the War Industries Board, federal officials took control of raw materials, production schedules, and prices, effectively creating a command economy to ensure that ships, munitions, and supplies reached the front lines. To fund this massive effort, the government sold Liberty Bonds and significantly increased income taxes. Meanwhile, the Committee on Public Information launched a massive propaganda campaign to sell the war to the American people. This campaign often blurred the line between patriotism and conformity, fostering a climate of suspicion that led to the passage of the Espionage and Sedition Acts. These laws criminalized dissent, allowing the government to silence critics and imprison those who spoke out against the war effort, including the Socialist leader Eugene Debs.

The war effort also transformed the lives of American women. With millions of men deployed, women entered the industrial workforce in record numbers, taking jobs in munitions factories, shipyards, and on farms. Others served in the military as nurses or clerical workers, while thousands more volunteered for the Red Cross or led food conservation efforts at home. This visible contribution to the national cause strengthened the argument for women’s suffrage. By 1920, the persistent activism of the suffrage movement, combined with the undeniable role women played in the wartime mobilization, helped secure the ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment, guaranteeing women the right to vote.

While the government cracked down on dissent, the war effort simultaneously triggered a profound demographic shift. As production needs soared and the supply of European immigrant labor dried up, industrial centers in the North and Midwest faced a desperate shortage of workers. This labor vacuum accelerated the Great Migration, the movement of hundreds of thousands of African Americans from the rural South to urban centers like Chicago, Detroit, and New York. Seeking to escape the violence and systemic disenfranchisement of the Jim Crow South, these migrants hoped for better wages and greater social freedom. While they found employment in factories and meatpacking plants, they also encountered new forms of racial tension and residential segregation in their new homes.

On the battlefield, the American Expeditionary Forces, led by General John J. Pershing, arrived in France in growing numbers throughout 1918. American troops played a decisive role in halting the final German offensives. The American Expeditionary Forces then launched the Meuse-Argonne Offensive, a massive and grueling campaign that helped break the German lines and forced the end of the conflict in November 1918.

When the fighting ended, President Wilson sought to translate his wartime rhetoric into a lasting peace. He arrived in Europe with his Fourteen Points, a vision for a new international order based on self-determination, free trade, and a global organization to mediate disputes. This organization, the League of Nations, became the centerpiece of his postwar diplomacy. However, Wilson’s idealism collided with the hard-edged nationalism of the European victors and the fierce political opposition of the United States Senate. Many senators, led by Henry Cabot Lodge, feared that joining the League would surrender American sovereignty and drag the nation into future foreign conflicts without congressional approval. Despite a grueling cross-country speaking tour to rally public support, Wilson could not overcome the resistance. The Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles, and the United States never joined the League of Nations.

## Key ideas

- The United States moved from formal neutrality to active participation in 1917, driven by German unrestricted submarine warfare and perceived threats to American security.
- The entry of the American Expeditionary Forces into the war provided the manpower necessary to break the stalemate on the Western Front, culminating in the decisive Meuse-Argonne Offensive.
- Women’s essential contributions to the wartime industrial and military effort provided critical momentum for the successful campaign to ratify the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920.
- The Great Migration of African Americans to Northern cities fundamentally reshaped the nation’s demographic and social landscape, though it also introduced new forms of racial conflict.
- The Senate’s rejection of the League of Nations reflected a deep divide over the role of the United States in international affairs and a retreat from the globalist idealism that defined the war effort.
