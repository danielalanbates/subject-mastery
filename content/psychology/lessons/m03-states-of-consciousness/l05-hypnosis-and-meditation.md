# Hypnosis and Meditation

Imagine a stage performer who invites a volunteer to the front, speaks in a rhythmic, soothing voice, and suddenly, the person acts as though they have forgotten their own name. This is a classic piece of theater. But is this what scientists mean when they talk about hypnosis? In our last lesson, we explored how psychoactive drugs alter our neurochemistry to change our perception of reality. Today, we turn to two other methods for shifting our conscious experience: hypnosis and meditation.

Hypnosis is a social interaction in which one person, the hypnotist, suggests to another that certain perceptions, feelings, thoughts, or behaviors will spontaneously occur. It is crucial to dispel a common myth immediately: hypnosis is not a form of mind control. A person under hypnosis cannot be forced to perform acts against their will or violate their deeply held moral values. The power of hypnosis lies in the subject’s own openness to suggestion. This susceptibility is a relatively stable trait, which researchers can measure using standardized tools like the Harvard Group Scale of Hypnotic Susceptibility.

Scholars remain divided on whether hypnosis is a unique, altered state of consciousness. Social influence theory suggests that hypnosis is not a distinct state at all. Instead, these researchers argue that the hypnotized person is simply an actor playing a role, highly motivated to fulfill the expectations of the hypnotist. Conversely, the divided consciousness theory, championed by researchers like Ernest Hilgard, proposes that hypnosis induces a genuine, split state of consciousness. Hilgard observed that even when hypnotized people were told they felt no pain, their brains still processed the sensory input. It is likely that both social factors and altered attention play a role.

What can hypnosis actually do? It is effective for pain management. Meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials indicate that for highly suggestible individuals, hypnotic suggestion can produce a modest reduction in the need for pain medication. Hypnosis can also facilitate anesthesia, motor inhibition, and the experience of hallucinations during the session. However, hypnosis is not a magic bullet. It does not increase physical strength, nor is it a reliable tool for memory recovery. In fact, hypnotic techniques often lead to the creation of false memories, where individuals confidently describe events that never happened. Consequently, many courts are cautious about accepting testimony obtained through these methods.

If hypnosis relies on external suggestion, meditation is a practice of internal regulation. Meditation encompasses various techniques, including focused attention, where one concentrates on a single object; open monitoring, which involves observing all experiences without judgment; and loving-kindness practices. These methods aim to improve attention regulation and self-regulation.

Research into meditation has grown significantly. Randomized controlled trials suggest that consistent practice can lead to a modest reduction in symptoms of anxiety and stress. Some studies correlate long-term practice with changes in the brain, such as reduced activity in the default mode network—the system active when the mind wanders—and changes in the amygdala, an area involved in emotional processing. However, we must be careful with these findings. Many brain-imaging studies are cross-sectional, meaning they compare long-term meditators to non-meditators at a single point in time. This makes it difficult to determine if meditation caused these brain changes or if people with certain brain structures were simply more likely to take up meditation in the first place.

Ultimately, both hypnosis and meditation highlight the plasticity of human consciousness. Our experience of the world is not a fixed, objective feed of data. It is a construct, shaped by our expectations, our focus, and the way we direct our cognitive resources. By shifting our attention—whether through the guided suggestions of another or the disciplined practice of the self—we can change how we process pain, stress, and our own internal narrative. Having explored how we can consciously influence our mental states, we are now ready to examine the very first step of our interaction with the world: how our senses detect the raw signals of light and sound.

## Key ideas

- Hypnosis is a social interaction characterized by heightened suggestibility, but it does not grant the hypnotist control over the subject’s behavior or moral agency.
- The scientific debate over hypnosis centers on whether it is a unique, divided state of consciousness or a result of social expectations and role-playing.
- While hypnosis can be a useful tool for managing pain, it is unreliable for memory retrieval and often results in the creation of false, fabricated recollections.
- Meditation practices, such as mindfulness, utilize attention regulation to improve emotional outcomes, though many reported brain changes require further longitudinal research to confirm a causal link.
