# Nuclear Stability and Radioactive Decay

Imagine holding a tiny, dense core at the center of an atom. You know from our previous look at atomic structures that atoms like carbon form stable bonds in chains and rings. But down inside the nucleus, the rules of physics shift from electron sharing to a much more intense game of forces. The nucleus is packed with positively charged protons and neutral neutrons. Since like charges repel, you might wonder why the nucleus does not simply fly apart. The answer lies in the strong nuclear force, a powerful attraction that acts only over incredibly short distances. It binds protons and neutrons together, overcoming the electromagnetic repulsion between the protons.

For an atom to remain stable, it needs the right balance of neutrons to protons. Think of this as a structural requirement. In small atoms, a one to one ratio of neutrons to protons is usually ideal. As atoms get larger, however, the repulsion between the many protons grows. To compensate, the nucleus requires an increasing number of neutrons to provide extra strong nuclear force without adding more charge-based repulsion. When a nucleus has too many or too few neutrons to maintain this balance, it becomes unstable or radioactive. It will eventually undergo radioactive decay, transforming its composition to reach a more stable state.

One common way this happens is through alpha decay. An alpha particle is essentially a helium nucleus, consisting of two protons and two neutrons. When a heavy, unstable nucleus emits an alpha particle, its mass number decreases by four and its atomic number decreases by two. For instance, consider uranium two three eight, which has a mass number of two three eight and an atomic number of nine two. When it undergoes alpha decay, it loses four units of mass and two units of charge, transforming into thorium two three four. In balancing this nuclear equation, we ensure the total mass number and total atomic number are the same on both sides of the reaction.

Sometimes a nucleus has too many neutrons. In this case, it might undergo beta decay. During this process, a neutron inside the nucleus converts into a proton and an electron. The proton stays in the nucleus, increasing the atomic number by one, while the high-energy electron is ejected as a beta particle. Because the mass of an electron is negligible compared to a proton or neutron, the mass number of the atom remains unchanged. If we look at carbon one four, which has six protons and eight neutrons, one neutron turns into a proton, turning the carbon atom into nitrogen one four. The mass number stays at one four, but the atomic number rises from six to seven.

Conversely, if a nucleus has too many protons, it might undergo positron emission. A positron is the antimatter counterpart to an electron; it has the same mass but a positive charge of positive one. During positron emission, a proton inside the nucleus converts into a neutron and a positron. The positron is ejected, and the atomic number decreases by one while the mass number stays the same. Finally, we have gamma decay. This does not change the identity of the element at all. Instead, it is the way an excited nucleus releases excess energy. Gamma rays are high-energy electromagnetic waves emitted when a nucleus settles into a lower, more stable energy state.

To balance any nuclear equation, you simply act like an accountant. The sum of the mass numbers on the left must equal the sum of the mass numbers on the right. Simultaneously, the sum of the atomic numbers on both sides must also match. If you are given an unknown decay product, you just subtract the known particle’s mass and atomic number from the parent nucleus to find the identity of the new element. For example, if you start with an isotope of polonium with a mass number of two one zero and an atomic number of eight four, and it emits an alpha particle, you subtract four from the mass and two from the atomic number. You are left with a mass number of two zero six and an atomic number of eight two, which identifies the product as lead two zero six.

These transformations are not instantaneous for every atom; they happen over specific intervals of time. Understanding these decay processes allows us to predict how long certain radioactive isotopes will persist in our environment. As we move forward, we will explore how these predictable rates of decay provide us with a natural clock for measuring the age of ancient materials.

## Key ideas

- The strong nuclear force acts over short distances to hold protons and neutrons together against the electrostatic repulsion of the protons.
- Nuclear stability depends on the neutron to proton ratio, which requires more neutrons to stabilize larger, more highly charged nuclei.
- Radioactive decay occurs through the emission of alpha particles, beta particles, positrons, or gamma rays to reach a more stable configuration.
- Balancing nuclear equations requires that the sum of mass numbers and the sum of atomic numbers remain equal on both sides of the reaction.
