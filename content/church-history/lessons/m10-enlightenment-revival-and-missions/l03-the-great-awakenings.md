# The Great Awakenings

Imagine standing in a crowded, dusty field in the American colonies during the middle of the seventeen hundreds. You are surrounded by hundreds of people, many of whom have traveled for days on foot or horseback. At the center, a man is preaching with an intensity that seems to vibrate through the air. He is not reading from a prepared manuscript in the traditional, monotone style of the day. Instead, he speaks directly to the hearts of his listeners, calling them to an immediate, personal encounter with God. This scene was not an anomaly; it was the hallmark of a seismic shift in religious life known as the First Great Awakening.

In our last lesson, we explored how John Wesley and the Methodists brought a disciplined, heartfelt piety to the Church of England. That same spirit of personal conversion crossed the Atlantic, finding fertile ground in the American colonies. The First Great Awakening, peaking in the seventeen forties, was led by figures like the rigorous theologian Jonathan Edwards and the dramatic itinerant preacher George Whitefield. Edwards, a brilliant mind in Massachusetts, argued that true religion consisted in holy affections, or deep-seated emotional responses to the beauty of God. Whitefield, meanwhile, traveled from colony to colony, using his powerful voice and oratorical skill to captivate massive outdoor audiences. They challenged the prevailing religious establishment, suggesting that church membership and outward morality were insufficient without a transformative, inward rebirth.

This movement reshaped the American landscape by prioritizing individual experience over formal, institutional tradition. It contributed to the decline of state-supported churches and spurred the rapid growth of denominations like the Methodists and Baptists. Because the focus was on the soul’s direct relationship with God, the Awakening fostered a spirit of independence. If a person could stand before God without the mediation of a traditional, state-sanctioned church structure, they were arguably more prepared to question other forms of authority as well. These revivals also spurred educational initiatives, leading to the founding of colleges such as Princeton, Dartmouth, and Brown.

After a period of relative quiet during the American Revolution, the religious fervor returned with even greater intensity around 1800. This era is known as the Second Great Awakening. If the first movement was defined by the preaching of the pulpit, the second was defined by the frontier camp meeting. These were multi-day outdoor gatherings in the expanding American West, where families would camp in wagons and hear preachers from various denominations hold forth from dawn until dusk.

The most famous figure of this second wave was Charles Finney. Unlike the earlier revivalists who believed that only God could initiate a spiritual awakening, Finney utilized what he called new measures. He believed that if a preacher used the right techniques, such as the anxious bench where those seeking conversion could sit at the front of the room, the church could effectively encourage a revival. Finney’s approach was pragmatic and highly structured. He turned revivalism into a profession, emphasizing human agency and the moral responsibility of the believer to choose God.

The impact of these revivals on public life was profound. The Second Great Awakening provided the moral fuel for a variety of social reforms. Believers who were energized by their personal faith began to look outward, seeking to improve the world around them. This led to a surge in activity regarding prison reform, the temperance movement, and, most significantly, the growing abolitionist movement that sought to end the scourge of slavery. The revivals also increased the participation of women and African Americans, laying the groundwork for independent Black churches and women's reform societies. The revivals taught that a changed heart should result in a changed society.

By the middle of the eighteen hundreds, the American church had been permanently altered. The emphasis on individual conversion and the desire for social transformation became central pillars of the evangelical identity. However, these movements also created new tensions. Critics argued that the focus on emotional experience and human agency undermined the intellectual depth and sacramental dignity of older church traditions. Despite these disagreements, the legacy of the Great Awakenings remains visible today in the way many churches prioritize personal faith and active engagement with the wider world. Having seen how these revivals mobilized individuals to address societal needs, we now turn our attention to how that same zeal for the gospel began to spread across the globe.

## Key ideas

- The First Great Awakening, led by figures like Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield, emphasized the necessity of a personal, emotional conversion experience over formal religious observance.
- The Awakenings contributed to the decline of state-supported churches and the rise of voluntary denominations, while also fueling the founding of major colleges.
- The Second Great Awakening shifted the focus of revivalism to the American frontier, utilizing large-scale camp meetings to reach a rapidly expanding population.
- Charles Finney’s revivalist methods championed human agency and practical techniques, marking a departure from earlier views that saw revival as a purely sovereign act of God.
- These revivals played a crucial role in shaping American public life by motivating Christians to pursue social reforms and increasing the leadership roles of women and African Americans.
