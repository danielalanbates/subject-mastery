# Inflation and Purchasing Power

Imagine walking into your favorite coffee shop and noticing that a simple latte costs five dollars this year, compared to four dollars last year. Nothing about the coffee has changed. The barista uses the exact same beans, the cup is the same size, and the shop is in the same location. Yet your money buys less coffee than it did twelve months ago. This is inflation in action. In our last lesson, we looked at how economists measure unemployment to gauge the health of the labor market. Today, we turn to the second major indicator of macroeconomic stability: inflation, and how it quietly reshapes our purchasing power and the cost of borrowing.

Inflation is a general, sustained increase in the overall price level of goods and services across the economy, rather than a spike in the price of just one item. When inflation occurs, the purchasing power of your money declines. Purchasing power is simply the quantity of goods and services you can actually buy with a single unit of currency. If prices rise by five percent over a year, but your income stays completely flat, your real purchasing power has dropped by five percent. You are poorer in terms of what your paycheck can secure, even though the number printed on your paycheck hasn't changed.

To track this shifting landscape, governments rely on statistical measures, the most prominent of which is the Consumer Price Index, which people often call the CPI. Imagine a massive, hypothetical basket of goods and services that a typical urban household buys each month, including housing, food, clothing, medical care, and transportation. Government statisticians price this basket every single month. The consumer price index measures the weighted average price of this basket relative to a designated base year. If the basket cost one hundred dollars in the base year and costs one hundred and twenty dollars today, the index has risen to one hundred and twenty, indicating a twenty percent cumulative rate of inflation over that period.

While the consumer price index captures the cost of living for consumers, economists also look at the Producer Price Index, which measures price changes from the perspective of businesses buying raw materials and intermediate goods. When producer prices rise, those costs are usually passed down the chain to consumers before long, making the producer index a helpful early warning signal for future consumer inflation.

Inflation also transforms the meaning of interest rates, dividing them into two distinct categories. When you take out a car loan or look at a savings account, the bank quotes you a nominal interest rate. This is the raw percentage increase in money terms. However, if inflation is running at three percent, and your savings account pays a nominal rate of two percent, your money is actually losing ground in real terms. To capture this reality, economists use the real interest rate, which is roughly calculated as the nominal interest rate minus the inflation rate. When the real interest rate is negative, the purchasing power stored in your savings account shrinks over time, no matter how faithfully the bank deposits your nominal interest earnings.

Different schools of economic thought offer differing perspectives on what drives this engine of price increases. Monetarists, following the tradition of Milton Friedman, emphasize that inflation is fundamentally a monetary phenomenon, driven when the central bank grows the money supply faster than the economy's output of goods and services. Keynesian economists look more broadly at aggregate demand, arguing that if total spending in the economy outpaces the economy's productive capacity, a demand-pull inflation takes hold. Supply shocks, such as a sudden spike in global oil prices, can likewise trigger cost-push inflation, forcing prices higher across nearly every sector of commerce.

Understanding how inflation erodes purchasing power and distorts financial contracts is essential for making sense of broader economic health. As price levels shift unpredictably, they alter the rhythm of economic activity, setting the stage for the recurring economic fluctuations that define modern market systems.

## Key ideas
- Inflation is a general, sustained increase in the overall price level that reduces the purchasing power of money over time.
- The Consumer Price Index measures changes in the cost of a standard basket of goods and services purchased by a typical urban household.
- The real interest rate adjusts the nominal interest rate by subtracting the rate of inflation, revealing the true change in purchasing power for savers and borrowers.
- Economists debate the primary drivers of inflation, with monetarists focusing on money supply growth and Keynesians examining aggregate demand pressures.
