# Derivative Tests and Graph Shape

Imagine driving down a winding mountain road at dusk. Even when you cannot see far ahead, the curve of the highway tells you everything you need to know. If the steering wheel turns steadily in one direction, the road bends downward in a bowl shape. If you must turn the wheel the other way to stay in your lane, the road arches upward like a dome. In calculus, we read the shape of a graph in much the same way. In our last lesson, the Mean Value Theorem gave us global insights into how functions change over an interval. Today, we zoom in on local behavior by examining concavity and inflection points using second derivatives.

The historical development of these powerful tools is rooted in one of the most famous intellectual rivalries in scientific history. In the seventeenth century, Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently invented the core ideas of calculus. Isaac Newton developed his method of fluxions, thinking of variables as flowing quantities changing over time, while Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz focused on sums and differences of infinitesimal quantities. A bitter and protracted dispute arose between them and their followers over who deserved priority of discovery. Fortunately for modern students, both brilliant men left us enduring gifts. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz introduced the differential notation, such as d y over d x, and the integral sign, while Isaac Newton introduced the dot notation for derivatives with respect to time, which remains common in physics today.

To understand graph shape, we must look beyond whether a function is increasing or decreasing. A curve can rise while bending downward, or fall while bending upward. We capture this bending behavior through concavity. A function is concave up on an open interval if its tangent lines lie completely below the graph. Think of a cup holding water. Conversely, a function is concave down if its tangent lines lie above the graph, like an umbrella shedding rain. We can also identify concavity through the first derivative, because a function is concave up when its first derivative is an increasing function, and concave down when its first derivative is a decreasing function. Since tangent lines are given by the first derivative, the rate at which those tangent slopes change is controlled by the second derivative.

Here is the core test for concavity. If the second derivative of our function is positive for every value in a given interval, the slopes of the tangent lines are constantly increasing. That means the curve bends upward, making it concave up. If the second derivative is negative throughout the interval, the tangent slopes are decreasing, so the curve bends downward, making it concave down. This gives us a reliable procedure for mapping the curvature of any twice differentiable function without plotting hundreds of points.

Where concavity changes from upward to downward, or the reverse, we find a very special location called a point of inflection. Picture a flexing spring or the exact place where a valley transitions into a hill. At a point of inflection, the graph passes right through its tangent line. For this transition to happen, the second derivative must equal zero or fail to exist, and the concavity must genuinely switch signs on either side of that location. It is vital to note that a point where the second derivative is zero, but the sign of the second derivative does not change, is not an inflection point. For example, the function f of x equals x to the fourth power has a second derivative of twelve x squared. At x equals zero, this second derivative is zero, yet the function remains concave up on both sides of zero because x squared is never negative. Therefore, zero is not an inflection point for that function. Just as the first derivative helps us find flat peaks and valleys, the second derivative reveals the exact places where the bending of the curve reverses.

Let us trace this with a concrete example. Consider the function f of x equals the quantity x cubed minus three x squared, defined for all real numbers. First, we take the derivative to find the velocity of change, giving f prime of x equals three x squared minus six x. Taking the derivative a second time yields the acceleration of change, giving f double prime of x equals six x minus six. To find potential inflection points, we set our second derivative equal to zero. Solving six x minus six equals zero gives us x equals one.

We now test the intervals on either side of x equals one. If we pick a test value to the left, such as zero, our second derivative becomes six times zero minus six, which equals negative six. Because negative six is less than zero, the function is concave down on the interval from negative infinity to one. If we pick a test value to the right, such as two, our second derivative becomes six times two minus six, which equals positive six. Because positive six is greater than zero, the function is concave up on the interval from one to positive infinity. Since the concavity changes from downward to upward at x equals one, that point is a genuine inflection point. Substituting one back into our original function gives the coordinates one and negative two for this inflection point on the graph.

By combining the first derivative test for local extrema with the second derivative test for concavity, we gain total mastery over curve sketching and qualitative analysis. We can predict precisely where a function reaches its peak, where it bottoms out, and how it bends between those landmarks. As we move forward, we will harness these analytical tools to evaluate limits and understand complex functions more deeply.

## Key ideas
- Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently developed calculus amidst a bitter priority dispute, each introducing notation that we still use today.
- Concavity describes how a curve bends, with upward concavity resembling a cup where tangent lines lie below the graph and downward concavity resembling a dome where tangent lines lie above it.
- A function is concave up on an interval where its second derivative is strictly positive, and concave down where its second derivative is strictly negative.
- A point of inflection occurs where a graph changes its concavity and passes through its tangent line, requiring a true sign change in the second derivative rather than just a zero value.
- To locate inflection points, we find where the second derivative equals zero or is undefined, and verify that concavity changes sign across that point.
