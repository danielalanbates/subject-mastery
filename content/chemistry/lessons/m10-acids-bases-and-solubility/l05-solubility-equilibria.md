# Solubility Equilibria

Imagine you have a glass of water and you begin adding table salt, one spoonful at a time. At first, the salt disappears into the water, creating a clear solution. Eventually, however, you reach a point where no more salt can dissolve. The solid crystals simply sit at the bottom of the glass. This is not because the process of dissolving has stopped, but because it has reached a state of dynamic equilibrium. While some salt ions are leaving the solid crystal to enter the water, an equal number of ions are returning from the water to the solid state. This balance is the foundation of solubility equilibria.

In our previous discussion of acid base chemistry, we focused on how ions react to neutralize one another. Here, we shift our focus to how ions interact with their own solid forms. For sparingly soluble salts, we use a specific equilibrium constant called the solubility product constant. This constant represents the product of the concentrations of the ions in a saturated solution, with each concentration raised to the power of its stoichiometric coefficient from the balanced dissolution equation. If you have a solid like silver chloride, it exists in equilibrium with its dissolved silver and chloride ions. The solubility product constant is simply the concentration of silver ions multiplied by the concentration of chloride ions.

Calculating how much of a solid can dissolve requires us to determine its molar solubility, which is the number of moles of solute that dissolve in one liter of water. Let us walk through a calculation using lead two chloride. When lead two chloride dissolves, it produces one lead ion with a charge of positive two and two chloride ions. Suppose the solubility product constant for lead two chloride is four times ten to the negative fifth. We define molar solubility as x. The concentration of lead ions is x, and the concentration of chloride ions is two x. The solubility product constant equals the concentration of lead ions multiplied by the concentration of chloride ions squared. Substituting our variables, the constant equals x times the square of two x. The square of two x is four x squared, so the product simplifies to four x cubed. To solve for x, we set four times ten to the negative fifth equal to four x cubed. Dividing both sides by four, we get one times ten to the negative fifth equals x cubed. Taking the cube root of both sides, we find that x is approximately zero point zero two two moles per liter. This value represents the molar solubility of lead two chloride.

Sometimes, a solution already contains one of the ions involved in the equilibrium. This leads to the common ion effect. If you try to dissolve silver chloride in a solution that already contains dissolved sodium chloride, the presence of extra chloride ions shifts the equilibrium toward the solid side according to Le Chatelier’s principle. Because the solution is already crowded with chloride, the silver chloride becomes significantly less soluble than it would be in pure water. This is a vital concept in analytical chemistry, as it allows us to selectively precipitate specific ions out of a mixture by controlling the concentration of common ions.

We can also predict whether a precipitate will form when we mix two solutions by using the reaction quotient. In this context, we call the reaction quotient the ion product. We calculate the ion product exactly like the solubility product constant, using the current concentrations of the ions in the mixture rather than the equilibrium concentrations. If the ion product is less than the solubility product constant, the solution is unsaturated, and no solid will form. If the ion product equals the solubility product constant, the solution is exactly saturated. If the ion product is greater than the solubility product constant, the system is supersaturated, and a precipitate must form to bring the ion concentrations back down to the equilibrium limit.

This quantitative approach to solubility helps us understand everything from the formation of kidney stones to the way minerals deposit in caves. By mastering the balance between solid and dissolved states, we gain control over chemical separations and the purification of substances. Understanding these equilibria also prepares us to look at the broader energy landscape of chemical systems. As we move forward, we will investigate the concept of entropy and the nature of spontaneity, which dictate why certain reactions prefer to proceed in one direction over another, bridging the gap between simple equilibrium and the complex energy transformations that drive our universe.

## Key ideas

- The solubility product constant is an equilibrium expression that describes the balance between a solid salt and its dissolved ions in a saturated solution.
- Molar solubility is determined by solving for the concentration of the dissolved species using the solubility product constant and the stoichiometry of the dissolution reaction.
- The common ion effect explains that adding an ion already present in a saturated solution will decrease the solubility of the salt by shifting the equilibrium toward the solid.
- By comparing the ion product to the solubility product constant, we can mathematically predict whether a precipitate will form when two solutions are mixed.
