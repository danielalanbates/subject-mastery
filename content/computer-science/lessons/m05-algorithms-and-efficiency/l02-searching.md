# Searching Ordered and Unordered Data

Imagine you are standing in a massive, chaotic library where the books have been tossed onto the shelves in no particular order. You are looking for a specific title by a specific author. Because the books are scattered randomly, you have no choice but to start at the very first shelf, pick up the first book, and check the title. If it is not the one you want, you put it back and move to the next. You repeat this process, book by book, until you finally hold the right volume in your hands. This is the essence of a linear search. It is simple, reliable, and entirely indifferent to the state of the data, but it is also incredibly slow if you have to check thousands of items.

In our previous lesson, we discussed what makes an algorithm good, focusing on clarity and correctness. Now, we must consider how the organization of our data dictates the efficiency of our search. When data is unordered, a linear search is often the only option. In the worst-case scenario, the item you are looking for is the very last one in the collection, or perhaps it is not there at all. In that case, you have to inspect every single item. If you have ten books, you might make ten guesses. If you have a million books, you might make a million guesses. The effort required grows in direct proportion to the number of items.

However, life becomes much easier when our data is sorted. Think back to that library, but this time, imagine the books are arranged alphabetically by author. You no longer need to check every single book. You can use a more sophisticated approach called a binary search. The logic here is to divide and conquer. You go to the middle of the shelves. You look at the author name there. If the name you want comes before the name you see, you know the book must be in the first half of the library. You can immediately ignore the entire second half.

By making one simple comparison, you have effectively cut your workload in half. Now, you repeat the process with the first half of the shelves. You go to the middle of that section, check the name, and again, you either find your book or discard half of the remaining options. You keep halving the search space until you narrow it down to the single book you need. Even if you have a million books, you can find any specific one in about twenty guesses. This is because every time you make a guess, you are eliminating half of the remaining possibilities.

Let us walk through a quick example with a sorted list of eight numbers: one, three, five, seven, nine, eleven, thirteen, and fifteen. Suppose we are looking for the number eleven. We look at the middle of the list, which gives us seven and nine. We choose nine. Since eleven is greater than nine, we discard everything to the left. We are left with eleven, thirteen, and fifteen. We pick the middle of those, which is thirteen. Since eleven is less than thirteen, we discard the thirteen and fifteen. Finally, we are left with eleven. We found it in just three steps. If we had used a linear search, it might have taken us six steps.

The beauty of binary search lies in its logarithmic efficiency. While linear search requires more work as the list grows, binary search scales gracefully. It is the difference between walking every inch of a road to find a lost key and using a map to zoom in on the exact house where you left it. However, this power comes with a strict requirement: the data must be sorted before you begin. If the books are out of order, the binary search will fail because it relies on the assumption that everything to the left is smaller and everything to the right is larger.

Choosing between these methods is a fundamental part of algorithm design. We must weigh the cost of sorting our data against the time we save during future searches. As we move forward, we will begin to formalize these observations into a mathematical notation that allows us to quantify exactly how much work an algorithm performs as our input size scales toward infinity.

## Key ideas

- A linear search inspects every item in a collection one by one, which is necessary for unordered data but becomes very slow as the collection grows.
- A binary search requires sorted data and functions by repeatedly dividing the search area in half, allowing it to find items much faster than a linear search.
- The number of guesses in a binary search is logarithmic, meaning the effort increases very slowly even as the amount of data grows exponentially.
- Deciding which search algorithm to use depends on whether the data is already sorted and how many times we expect to search through that data in the future.
