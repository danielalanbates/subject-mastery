# Slope as a Rate of Change

Imagine you are driving up a mountain road on a steep incline. As you travel forward along the horizontal asphalt, your car also climbs higher into the air. Mathematics captures this dual movement with a single number called slope. In our last lesson, we measured the straight-line distance between two points on the coordinate plane and found their exact midpoint. Today, we step beyond static locations to examine how one variable changes relative to another. Slope measures the steepness and direction of a line, telling us precisely how much the vertical output changes for each single-unit increase in the horizontal input.

To picture how this works, think of walking along a straight path drawn on a grid. We call the horizontal movement the run and the vertical movement the rise. Slope is simply the ratio of the rise to the run, expressed as a fraction. If you walk forward by three units and climb upward by six units, your rise is six and your run is three. Dividing six by three gives a slope of two. This means that for every one step you take to the right, your height increases by two units. The input grows by one, and the output grows by two.

A fundamental property of any straight line is that the slope is constant for any two points you choose. Whether you check the steepness near the start of the path or far down the road, the ratio of the vertical change to the horizontal change never varies. For any two points on a line, with coordinates for the first point and coordinates for the second point, the slope equals the change in output divided by the change in input. Stated as a formula, the slope equals the quantity the second vertical output minus the first vertical output, divided by the quantity the second horizontal input minus the first horizontal input.

The sign of this number tells us the direction of the travel. When a line rises as you move from left to right, the slope is positive, representing growth or upward progress. When a line falls as you scan from left to right, the slope is negative, reflecting a decline. Consider a path where you walk forward four units while dropping two units vertically. The rise is negative two, and the run is four, giving a slope of negative one over two. For each one-unit step to the right, your output decreases by one-half.

Special lines have extreme behaviors that reveal the limits of this ratio. A completely flat horizontal floor has no rise at all. If the rise is zero and the run is five, zero divided by five gives a slope of zero. Walking along a horizontal line means your output never changes no matter how far you travel. Conversely, a vertical wall has no run. If you try to calculate the slope of a vertical line with a rise of six and a run of zero, you encounter division by zero. Because division by zero is undefined, a vertical line has an undefined slope, representing a motion that is impossible to walk along as a function of the horizontal input.

In the physical world, slope is not just a geometric feature on paper; it is a rate with meaningful units. Suppose you track a cyclist's journey on a graph where the horizontal input measures hours and the vertical output measures miles. If the line connects the point zero hours, zero miles to the point two hours, one hundred miles, the slope equals the quantity one hundred miles minus zero miles, divided by the quantity two hours minus zero hours. This simplifies to fifty miles per hour. The slope is a genuine rate of change, telling us the speed of the cyclist. The numerator units of miles are divided by the denominator units of hours, yielding miles per hour.

Let us test this with another concrete example. Suppose a water tank leaks over time, and a graph shows the remaining gallons versus the elapsed minutes. At minute two, fifty gallons remain. At minute six, thirty gallons remain. The change in the output gallons is thirty minus fifty, which is negative twenty gallons. The change in the input minutes is six minus two, which is four minutes. Dividing negative twenty by four gives a slope of negative five. The rate of change is negative five gallons per minute, meaning the tank loses five gallons with every passing minute.

Grasping slope as a rate of change transforms a static line into a dynamic story about how variables interact. Now that we can measure the steepness and speed of change for any straight path, we are ready to write algebraic equations that capture these relationships completely. In our next lesson, we will combine a point and a slope to build the full equations of lines.

## Key ideas
- Slope is defined as the rise divided by the run, measuring the vertical change for a given horizontal change, and remains constant for any two points chosen on a line.
- The slope formula states that slope equals the quantity the second vertical output minus the first vertical output, divided by the quantity the second horizontal input minus the first horizontal input.
- A positive slope indicates a rising line, a negative slope indicates a falling line, a zero slope represents a horizontal line, and an undefined slope represents a vertical line.
- Slope acts as a rate of change with combined units, such as miles per hour or gallons per minute, showing how an output changes for each one-unit increase in the input.
