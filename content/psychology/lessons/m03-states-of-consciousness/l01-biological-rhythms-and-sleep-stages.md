# Biological Rhythms and Sleep Stages

Have you ever noticed how your energy levels follow a predictable script throughout the day? You might feel sharp by mid-morning, experience a slump after lunch, and feel a surge of alertness in the early evening. This is not just a matter of your schedule or caffeine intake. It is the result of your body’s internal timekeeper, a biological rhythm that keeps you in sync with the twenty-four-hour rotation of the Earth.

In our last module, we explored the endocrine system, which regulates bodily functions through hormones. Those chemical messengers operate within a framework set by your circadian rhythm. This is a roughly twenty-four-hour cycle of physiological processes. Deep in the middle of your brain, in a region of the hypothalamus called the suprachiasmatic nucleus, sits your master clock. Research using animal models and human clinical studies provides strong evidence that this cluster of cells acts as the primary pacemaker for daily rhythms. This nucleus receives direct input from your eyes. When sunlight hits your retinas, the suprachiasmatic nucleus signals your brain to suppress the production of melatonin, the hormone that promotes sleep. As daylight fades, the signal changes, and your brain begins to release melatonin, nudging you toward rest.

Scientists often use the two-process model to explain sleep regulation. This model suggests that your sleep timing is determined by the interaction between two systems. First, there is the circadian Process C, which is your internal clock. Second, there is the homeostatic Process S, which tracks your sleep pressure—the biological need for sleep that builds up the longer you stay awake. When these two processes align, you experience stable sleep.

When we disrupt this rhythm, we feel the consequences. Evidence from longitudinal studies on shift workers and experimental studies on jet lag shows a clear correlation between misalignment of these rhythms and impairments in cognitive performance, mood, and physical health. While we infer that this misalignment causes the resulting disorientation, we must distinguish between the rhythm disruption itself and the secondary effects of sleep deprivation. For example, traveling across time zones forces your physiological processes—like body temperature regulation and hormone release—to operate out of phase with the local sun.

Once we drift off, we do not simply power down. Instead, we enter an active state of consciousness that cycles through distinct stages. We measure these stages using an electroencephalogram, or EEG, which records electrical activity through waves. When you are awake, your brain produces fast, low-amplitude beta waves. As you relax, these shift into slower, more regular alpha waves.

Sleep is divided into non-REM and REM sleep. Non-REM sleep consists of three stages. In stage one, or N1, you are in a light, transitional sleep characterized by theta waves. In stage two, or N2, your brain waves slow further, marked by the appearance of sleep spindles and K-complexes, which are distinct bursts of neural activity. By stage three, or N3, you reach deep, slow-wave sleep, dominated by high-amplitude delta waves. During this time, your heart rate and breathing drop to their lowest levels, and it becomes very difficult to wake you up.

After about ninety minutes of moving through these non-REM stages, your brain activity shifts dramatically. You enter REM sleep, or rapid eye movement sleep. During this stage, your brain waves become fast and active, resembling the waves of a person who is wide awake. Yet, your body is essentially paralyzed, with your muscles relaxed to the point of inactivity. This is the stage where vivid dreaming is most likely to occur.

These stages do not happen just once. A full sleep cycle lasts about ninety minutes. As the night progresses, the composition of these cycles changes. You spend more time in slow-wave sleep during the first half of the night, while the REM periods grow longer and more frequent as morning approaches. This rhythmic architecture is essential for how we process information and regulate our emotions. ## Key ideas

- Circadian rhythms are internal biological cycles regulated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which interacts with homeostatic sleep pressure to determine sleep timing.
- EEG research identifies distinct sleep stages, including N1 theta waves, N2 sleep spindles and K-complexes, and N3 slow-wave delta activity.
- Evidence from clinical and experimental studies shows that disrupting circadian rhythms leads to significant cognitive and physiological impairment.
- Sleep follows a ninety-minute cycle, with the proportion of slow-wave sleep and REM sleep shifting as the night progresses.
