# Modeling with Word Problems

Imagine you are standing at a train station, watching two trains pull away from each other on parallel tracks. One is crawling along at forty miles per hour while the other races ahead at sixty miles per hour. When will they be two hundred miles apart? Translating a moving, breathing story like that into a clean, precise mathematical equation is one of the most powerful skills in all of algebra. In our last lesson, we mastered solving linear and rational equations once they were neatly handed to us. Today, we step back one crucial stage to build those equations ourselves from descriptions of real-world scenarios.

To bridge the gap between human language and mathematical symbols, we follow a reliable four-step method: name the unknown, write the relationship, solve, and check. First, we name our unknown quantity clearly using a variable like letter m or letter n, and if there is a second unknown, we express it in terms of that same letter rather than introducing a mess of new ones. Second, we translate the words into an equation by looking for key verbal clues. Words like total or sum mean addition, difference means subtraction, and phrases like is equal to or yields translate directly into an equals sign.

Let us apply this first to a classic distance, rate, and time problem. Suppose two cyclists start from the same town and ride in opposite directions. The first cyclist pedals at ten miles per hour, and the second pedals at fifteen miles per hour. When will they be seventy miles apart? We begin by naming our unknown: let t be the number of hours they ride. The distance the first cyclist travels is ten times t, and the distance the second cyclist travels is fifteen times t. The relationship we write is that the sum of their two distances must equal seventy miles. So, ten times t plus fifteen times t equals seventy.

Now we solve that linear equation step by step. Combining our like terms on the left side, we get twenty-five times t equals seventy. To isolate t, we divide both sides by twenty-five, which gives us seventy over twenty-five, or two point eight hours. Finally, we check our work by substituting that answer back into the original story. If the first cyclist rides for two point eight hours at ten miles per hour, they cover twenty-eight miles. If the second rides for two point eight hours at fifteen miles per hour, they cover forty-two miles. Adding twenty-eight and forty-two gives us exactly seventy miles, so our solution makes complete sense.

This same four-step rhythm guides us through mixture and percent problems, where we track quantities of pure substances mixed into solutions. Imagine a pharmacist who has a ten percent saline solution and wants to mix it with a forty percent saline solution to create three hundred milliliters of a twenty percent solution. We name our unknown: let m be the volume of the ten percent solution in milliliters. That means the volume of the forty percent solution must be the quantity three hundred minus m. The relationship is based on the total amount of pure salt: ten percent of m plus forty percent of the quantity three hundred minus m must equal twenty percent of three hundred.

We solve this by writing decimals for our percents: zero point one m plus zero point four times the quantity three hundred minus m equals zero point two times three hundred, which is sixty. Expanding the parentheses gives zero point one m plus one hundred twenty minus zero point four m equals sixty. Combining our like terms leaves negative zero point three m plus one hundred twenty equals sixty. Subtracting one hundred twenty from both sides leaves negative zero point three m equals negative sixty. Dividing both sides by negative zero point three gives us two hundred milliliters for m, meaning we need two hundred milliliters of the ten percent solution and one hundred milliliters of the forty percent solution.

Sometimes we do not need to build an equation from scratch, but rather rearrange an existing formula to solve for a single desired quantity. If you know the formula for the perimeter of a rectangle, which is twice the length plus twice the width equals the total perimeter, you can treat it just like any linear equation to isolate width in terms of perimeter and length. Subtract twice the length from both sides, then divide both sides by two: the width equals the quantity perimeter minus twice the length, all divided by two. For a perimeter of thirty and a length of ten, the width is thirty minus twenty, which is ten, divided by two, giving five; and indeed twice ten plus twice five is thirty. Whether you are charting moving trains, mixing chemical solutions, or rearranging scientific laws, the secret lies in turning language into algebra with care. As we move into our next lesson, we will leave linear relationships behind to explore how to solve quadratic equations by factoring and square roots.

## Key ideas
- Modeling word problems relies on a four-step method: name the unknown, write the relationship, solve, and check that the answer makes sense.
- Distance, rate, and time problems are solved by equating the sum or difference of individual distances to a total distance.
- Mixture and percent problems require tracking the amount of a pure substance across different concentrations to maintain equality.
- Rearranging a formula involves treating the desired variable as the primary unknown while isolating it using standard algebraic operations.
