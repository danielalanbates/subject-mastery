# Angular Momentum and Equilibrium

Imagine holding a bicycle wheel by its axle handles while sitting on a spinning stool. If someone hands you that wheel spinning rapidly, and you suddenly tilt it sideways, something remarkable happens. Your entire stool begins to rotate beneath you in the opposite direction. You have just felt the stubborn persistence of rotational motion, a phenomenon governed by angular momentum. In our last lesson, we explored how torque changes the speed of a spinning object. Today, we look at why a spinning object resists any attempt to change the direction of its spin, and how objects manage to remain completely at rest and balanced.

Angular momentum is the rotational counterpart to the linear momentum we studied earlier. For any spinning body, the amount of angular momentum depends on two things. It depends on the mass distribution, which is the rotational inertia, and on how fast the body is spinning, which is the rotational speed. The fundamental law of conservation of angular momentum states that if no outside twisting force acts on a system, the total angular momentum of that system must remain constant. This is why a figure skater pulls her arms inward to spin faster. By shrinking her rotational inertia, her rotational speed must increase to keep the total angular momentum unchanged. Just as the net force on an object equals the rate of change of its linear momentum, the net torque on a spinning object equals the rate of change of its angular momentum.

The direction of the angular momentum points along the axis of rotation. To find this direction, you can curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the spin, and your thumb will point along the axis to indicate the direction of the angular momentum vector. Because changing that direction requires a torque, a rapidly spinning object resists tilting. If you try to tilt the spin axis of a gyroscope, the applied torque is perpendicular to the angular momentum, which causes the axis to rotate sideways in a motion called precession rather than simply pushing back or falling immediately. Under the idealized model of a rigid body on a frictionless pivot, a top experiences gravity, but its rapid spin and precession keep it upright for a time before friction slows it down and it falls. This directional stability is why spinning wheels are used in navigational compasses on ships and spacecraft, pointing steadily toward a fixed orientation regardless of how the vehicle rolls or pitches.

While spinning objects maintain their orientation, stationary objects achieve stability through a different principle known as static balance. For an object to be in complete static equilibrium, two strict conditions must be met. First, the vector sum of all external forces acting on the object must be zero, meaning it has no tendency to accelerate linearly in any direction. Second, the sum of all torques acting on the object about any pivot point must also be zero, meaning it has no tendency to start rotating.

Let us walk through a practical example of static balance. Imagine a uniform wooden plank weighing fifty newtons and measuring four meters long, resting horizontally with its ends supported by two bathroom scales. If you place a heavy box weighing one hundred newtons exactly one meter from the left end, what do the two scales read? To solve this, we apply the rules of equilibrium. We choose the left scale as our pivot point. The weight of the plank acts downward at its center, which is two meters from the left end. The box acts downward one meter from the left end. Taking torques about the left end, the box creates a clockwise twisting effect of one hundred newtons multiplied by one meter, which equals one hundred newton meters. The plank creates a clockwise twisting effect of fifty newtons multiplied by two meters, which equals one hundred newton meters.

Adding these two clockwise torque contributions gives a total clockwise torque of two hundred newton meters about the left pivot. To balance this, the right scale must push upward with a force that creates an opposing counterclockwise torque. Since the right scale is four meters away from our pivot, we divide the two hundred newton meters of torque by the four meter distance, telling us the right scale reads fifty newtons. For a sense check, the left scale reads more because the heavy box is closer to it. To find the reading on the left scale, we use our first condition of equilibrium, which states that total upward force must equal total downward force. The total downward weight is one hundred fifty newtons. With the right scale supporting fifty newtons, the left scale must support the remaining one hundred newtons. This step by step balance of forces and torques allows engineers to design bridges and buildings that remain rigidly stable against all loads.

As we look toward our next journey, we will lift our gaze from spinning wheels and balanced beams to the grand scale of the cosmos, exploring how gravity orchestrates the sweeping orbital paths of planets and moons.

## Key ideas
- Angular momentum combines rotational inertia and rotational speed, and its rate of change equals the net torque applied to the system.
- The right hand rule determines the vector direction of angular momentum, and applying a perpendicular torque to a spinning object causes the axis to undergo precession.
- Static equilibrium requires both that the net force on an object is zero and that the net torque about any pivot point is zero.
- Solving equilibrium problems involves balancing opposing torques around a chosen pivot to find unknown support forces.
