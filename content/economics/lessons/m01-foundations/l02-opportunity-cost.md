# Opportunity Cost and Decision Making

Imagine it is a sunny Saturday and you have fifty dollars in cash. You could spend the afternoon at a music festival, where the ticket costs fifty dollars, or you could work an extra shift that would earn you sixty dollars. If you decide to go to the festival, most people would say it costs fifty dollars, the price of the ticket. An economist would say it costs more. Going means giving up two things: the fifty dollars, which you could have spent on something else, and the sixty dollars you could have earned at work. Together they make the opportunity cost of the festival: one hundred ten dollars.

This distinction follows from the basic idea of scarcity. Because our time, money, and other resources are limited, we must constantly choose how to allocate them. Every choice means giving up something else, and the value of what we give up is the opportunity cost.

Opportunity cost is defined as the value of the next best alternative that must be sacrificed when a decision is made. It is not the value of all the other possibilities you could have pursued, only the one you would have chosen if the selected option were unavailable. For example, suppose you have three activities to consider: studying for an exam, sleeping, or watching a movie. If you decide to study, the opportunity cost is not both sleeping and watching a movie. It is whichever of those two you would have preferred most in the absence of studying. In other words, the opportunity cost is the single best alternative you forgo.

Two important concepts often get confused with opportunity cost: explicit costs and sunk costs. Explicit costs are the monetary payments you actually make, such as the fifty-dollar ticket or the rent you pay for an apartment. Sunk costs are expenses that have already been incurred and cannot be recovered, like a non-refundable concert ticket you bought last week. Because sunk costs cannot be changed by any future action, they should not influence your current decision. If you already spent thirty dollars on a ticket that you cannot sell, that thirty dollars is a sunk cost. When you decide whether to attend the concert, you should ignore the sunk cost and focus on the opportunity cost of the time you will spend at the event.

Opportunity cost is not limited to money. It can be measured in any unit that reflects the value you place on alternatives. For instance, if you value an hour of leisure at ten dollars, then spending that hour working overtime has an opportunity cost of ten dollars in lost leisure. Similarly, choosing to eat a healthy salad instead of a cheeseburger may involve an opportunity cost measured in taste satisfaction, even though the monetary price difference is small.

Economists also speak of marginal opportunity cost. This is the cost of obtaining one additional unit of something, holding everything else constant. Imagine you are deciding whether to study an extra hour for a test. The marginal opportunity cost of that hour is the value of the next best use of that hour—perhaps a half-hour of sleep plus a half-hour of watching a favorite show. By comparing the marginal benefit of the extra study time with its marginal opportunity cost, you can decide whether the additional hour is worthwhile.

A larger illustration of opportunity cost involves the decision to attend college. Suppose tuition, books, and fees amount to fifteen thousand dollars per year. That figure is an explicit cost because you must write a check or take out a loan to pay it. Yet, if you were to work full-time instead of studying, you could earn about thirty thousand dollars a year. That foregone salary is an implicit cost, reflecting the value of your time and skills in the labor market. Adding the explicit and implicit costs gives the opportunity cost of one year of college: forty-five thousand dollars, not just the fifteen thousand in tuition. Notice that everyday living costs such as food are usually left out, because you would pay for them whether or not you studied. Recognizing this helps you compare the benefits of education—future earnings, knowledge, personal growth—with the true cost of the alternative use of your time.

The way economists treat profit illustrates the same principle. A small business generates one hundred thousand dollars in revenue and incurs sixty thousand dollars in explicit expenses for rent, supplies, and wages. An accountant would report a profit of forty thousand dollars. However, if the owner left a job that paid fifty thousand dollars to run the business, that foregone salary is an implicit cost. Including it, the economic profit becomes negative ten thousand dollars, indicating that, from an economic standpoint, the owner would have been better off staying at the previous job. This example shows why economists always consider both explicit and implicit costs when evaluating decisions.

Everyday situations also involve opportunity cost. Picture yourself waiting in a long line for a free sandwich. The sandwich itself costs nothing, but the time you spend standing in line has value. If you value your time at fifteen dollars an hour and you wait for thirty minutes, the opportunity cost of the free sandwich is roughly seven and a half dollars. By assigning a monetary value to time, you can see that “free” items are rarely costless.

Understanding opportunity cost prepares you for the next analytical tool in economics: the budget constraint. The budget constraint maps the combinations of goods and activities you can afford, given your income and the prices you face, while implicitly accounting for the opportunity costs of each choice. In the upcoming lesson, we will see how this idea turns the intuitive sense of trade-offs into a simple, precise way of reasoning about choices.

## Key ideas
- Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative that must be given up when a choice is made.
- Sunk costs are past expenditures that cannot be recovered and should be ignored in current decisions.
- Opportunity cost includes both monetary and non-monetary values, and can be measured marginally for incremental choices.
- The full opportunity cost of a choice combines explicit out-of-pocket payments with implicit costs such as foregone earnings or leisure.
- Recognizing opportunity cost is essential for constructing and interpreting the budget-constraint framework.
