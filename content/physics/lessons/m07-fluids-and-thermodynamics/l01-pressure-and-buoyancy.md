# Pressure and Buoyancy

Imagine diving to the bottom of a deep swimming pool. As you go deeper, you feel a tightening sensation pressing against your eardrums. That discomfort is your first direct experience with fluid pressure. In our previous module, we looked at how gravity orchestrates planetary orbits across vast empty spaces. Now, we turn our attention from the vacuum of space to the substances that flow around us, specifically liquids and gases. Fluids shape our world, from the air pressing on our skin to the vast oceans that cover our planet. Understanding how these substances behave requires us to look closely at two interconnected concepts: how fluids exert pressure, and why objects either float or sink.

To understand fluid pressure, we must look at what is happening on a microscopic scale. A fluid is made of countless tiny particles, whether molecules of water or atoms of nitrogen and oxygen, constantly moving and colliding with everything they touch. When you submerge an object in water, or when air surrounds your body, these countless microscopic collisions sum up to a steady, pushing force. Pressure is simply that force distributed over an area. More formally, pressure equals the force applied divided by the surface area over which it acts. Because fluids can flow, this pressure does not just push downward. It pushes equally in all directions at any given point, pressing against the sides of a container, the hull of a submarine, or your own skin.

As you go deeper into a fluid, the pressure increases. Think about a tall stack of heavy books. The book at the very bottom feels the weight of every single book stacked on top of it, while the book near the top feels almost nothing. A fluid behaves in the exact same way. The water at the bottom of the ocean is compressed by the immense weight of all the water above it. This means that fluid pressure increases directly with depth. It also depends on the density of the fluid and the local acceleration due to gravity, which is nine point eight meters per second squared.

This increase in pressure with depth leads directly to one of the most striking phenomena in physics: buoyancy. When you submerge an object in water, the pressure at the bottom of the object is greater than the pressure at the top because the bottom is deeper. This difference in pressure creates a net upward push on the object. This upward force exerted by a fluid is called the buoyant force. The ancient Greek scholar Archimedes realized that the magnitude of this buoyant force is equal to the weight of the fluid that the object pushes aside, or displaces. This principle explains why a massive steel ship can float easily on the ocean. Even though steel is much denser than water, the ship is hollow and filled with air, so its overall shape displaces a huge volume of water. The weight of that displaced water creates a massive upward buoyant force that supports the heavy ship.

Let us walk through a quick example to see how this works in numbers. Suppose you submerge a solid metal block with a volume of zero point one cubic meters entirely in water. The density of water is one thousand kilograms per cubic meter. First, we find the mass of the displaced water by multiplying its volume by its density, which gives us one hundred kilograms. Next, we find the weight of that displaced water by multiplying its mass by the acceleration due to gravity of nine point eight meters per second squared. This gives us a buoyant force of nine hundred eighty newtons pushing upward on the block. If the downward pull of gravity on the block itself is less than nine hundred eighty newtons, the block will float to the surface. If the block is heavier than nine hundred eighty newtons, it will sink, though it will still feel lighter underwater than it does in the open air.

Fluids are not static, however. They can swirl, rush, and flow in complex patterns that govern everything from the wind in our atmosphere to the blood moving through our veins. Next, we will build on these static principles to explore fluid dynamics and flow, seeing what happens when liquids and gases start moving.

## Key ideas
- Fluid pressure is the force exerted by the microscopic collisions of fluid particles distributed over a surface area, pressing equally in all directions at any given depth.
- Fluid pressure increases with depth because of the weight of the fluid pressing down from above.
- The buoyant force is an upward push exerted on any object placed in a fluid, caused by the difference in pressure between the top and bottom of the object.
- According to Archimedes principle, the magnitude of the buoyant force equals the weight of the fluid displaced by the submerged object.
