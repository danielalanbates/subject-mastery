# Measurement and Significant Figures

Imagine you are baking a cake. If the recipe calls for two cups of flour, you likely reach for a standard measuring cup. If you accidentally use a coffee mug or a cereal bowl, your cake might turn out dry or collapse entirely. In chemistry, measurement is our primary tool for understanding the physical world. Unlike a simple kitchen recipe, our measurements must account for the inherent limits of our tools. Today we focus on how we quantify matter, ensuring that our data is both reliable and honest.

To maintain consistency, scientists worldwide use the International System of Units. This system is built upon seven base units, such as the meter for length, the kilogram for mass, and the second for time. Because we often work with quantities much larger or smaller than these base units, we use metric prefixes to adjust the scale. For example, a millimeter is one-thousandth of a meter, and a kilometer is one thousand meters. By attaching these prefixes, we can express any measurement using a manageable number.

No measurement is ever truly perfect. Every time you record a value, there is a degree of uncertainty. This leads us to the distinction between accuracy and precision. Accuracy refers to how close a measurement is to the true or accepted value. Precision refers to how close a series of measurements are to one another. Think of a dartboard. If you hit the bullseye every time, you are both accurate and precise. If you hit the same spot near the outer edge consistently, you are precise, but not accurate. If your darts are scattered all over the board, you lack both. We often express this uncertainty as an interval, such as twelve point three plus or minus zero point one centimeters.

To communicate this uncertainty, we use significant figures. These are the digits in a measurement that carry meaning, including all the certain digits plus one final digit that is estimated. When you look at a ruler with millimeter markings, you can confidently report the centimeters and the millimeters, but you must estimate the fraction between the last two marks. That estimated digit is significant.

Counting significant figures follows a few simple rules. Non-zero digits are always significant. Zeros between non-zero digits are always significant. Leading zeros, such as those in zero point zero five, are just placeholders and do not count. Trailing zeros are significant only if there is a decimal point in the number. For instance, five hundred has one significant figure, but five hundred point zero has four. When dealing with very large or small numbers, we use scientific notation to clarify these figures. For example, six point zero two times ten to the twenty-third clearly shows three significant figures. Note that exact numbers, such as counted items or defined conversion factors, have infinite significant figures and do not limit the precision of a calculation.

When you use measurements in calculations, your answer cannot be more precise than your least precise measurement. For addition and subtraction, the result should have the same number of decimal places as the measurement with the fewest decimal places. If you add twelve point one grams to zero point five two grams, your calculator gives you twelve point six two, but you must round that to twelve point six because your first measurement was only certain to the tenths place. For multiplication and division, the result is limited by the measurement with the fewest total significant figures. If you multiply two point zero centimeters by three point zero zero centimeters, the result is six point zero square centimeters, keeping two significant figures because two point zero has the fewest.

When rounding, if the digit to be dropped is exactly five, we often round to the nearest even number to avoid systematic bias. Always remember to keep your digits through the intermediate steps of a calculation and only round at the very end to avoid rounding errors. By respecting these rules, you ensure that your scientific claims reflect the true quality of your data. We are now ready to move on to the next step, which is learning how to transform those numbers from one unit to another using conversion factors.

## Key ideas

- The International System of Units uses base units and metric prefixes to provide a consistent, global standard for scientific measurement.
- Accuracy describes how close a measurement is to the true value, while precision describes the consistency of repeated measurements.
- Significant figures communicate the uncertainty of a measurement by including all known digits plus one final estimated digit, with scientific notation used to clarify precision in large or small values.
- Calculations must be rounded to reflect the precision of the least certain value, using the nearest even number rule when dropping a five to minimize bias.
- Exact numbers like counted items or defined conversion factors possess infinite significant figures and do not restrict the precision of a calculation.
