# The Athanasian Creed

Imagine trying to describe a symphony to someone who has never heard sound, or explaining three dimensions to a traveler from a flat plane. Human language strains to its absolute breaking point when we try to speak about the inner life of God. In our last lesson, we saw how the Council of Chalcedon mapped out how Jesus could be both fully God and fully human. But what about the wider architecture of the Trinity itself? How do Christian thinkers safeguard the truth that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are distinct persons, yet one single God, without tipping into polytheism or collapsing into a blur?

To find an answer, we turn to one of the most remarkable and intricate texts in the history of Christian thought: the Athanasian Creed, known in Latin by its opening word, "Quicunque vult," which means, "Whoever desires." Unlike the Nicene Creed, which emerged from an imperial council, the Athanasian Creed is a Latin text that likely arose in southern Gaul, modern-day France, around the late fifth or early sixth century. Although it was falsely attributed for centuries to Saint Athanasius of Alexandria because of its fierce defense of orthodoxy against heresy, scholars today recognize that it was written anonymously. In the Western church, it quickly gained immense authority, becoming one of the three great ecumenical statements alongside the Apostles' and Nicene creeds. Eastern Orthodox churches, however, do not regard it as ecumenical, primarily because of its inclusion of the filioque clause regarding the procession of the Holy Spirit.

The creed opens with a striking, uncompromising assertion about the necessity of catholic faith, using the word catholic in its original sense of universal and historic Christianity. It also includes severe anathematic or damnatory clauses declaring that salvation depends on holding this historic faith intact. The text then plunges immediately into the grammar of the Trinity with mathematical precision and poetic rhythm. The text commands us to worship one God in Trinity, and Trinity in unity, neither confusing the persons nor dividing the divine substance. Here, Latin theological vocabulary reaches its zenith. The Father is uncreated, the Son is uncreated, and the Holy Spirit is uncreated. The Father is infinite, the Son is infinite, and the Holy Spirit is infinite.

Yet we do not have three uncreated beings or three infinites. We have one uncreated and one infinite God. The creed walks a razor-thin theological tightrope. If you say the Father, Son, and Spirit are merely three masks or modes of a single person wearing different hats, you fall into the ancient error of modalism. If you say they are three separate gods working in close cooperation, you fall into polytheism or tritheism. The Athanasian Creed blocks both paths, insisting that the Godhead is one in essence or substance, but three in subsistence or personal relations.

To make this concrete, the text uses careful relational definitions. The Father is made of none, neither created nor begotten. The Son is of the Father alone, not made nor created, but begotten. The Holy Spirit is of the Father and of the Son, neither made nor created nor begotten, but proceeding. These distinctions preserve the eternal relational order within the Godhead. The Father is unoriginate, the Son is eternally begotten of the Father, and the Spirit eternally proceeds from the Father and the Son. This Western addition of the phrase "and the Son," known as the filioque clause, later became a major point of division between Western and Eastern traditions.

Crucially, the creed insists on absolute co-equality. In this Trinity, none is before or after another, none is greater or less than another, but the whole three persons are co-eternal together and co-equal. No single person of the Trinity outranks the others in deity or eternity. The Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God, yet they are not three gods, but one God. The language is deliberately paradoxical, designed to stretch human reason past its breaking point and to humble our intellect before the mystery of the divine nature.

The second half of the creed shifts from the Trinity to the Incarnation, reinforcing the Chalcedonian boundaries we explored last time. It insists that our Lord Jesus Christ is both God and man, equal to the Father touching His Godhead, and inferior to the Father touching His manhood. Just as the rational soul and flesh make one man, so God and man make one Christ. This dual focus ensures that the doctrine of God and the doctrine of salvation remain intimately bound together, because the God who saves us is none other than the triune God incarnate. In many Western traditions, such as the Anglican Book of Common Prayer and historic Lutheran confessions, the creed is used liturgically on Trinity Sunday to celebrate this profound mystery.

As we move from these ancient Latin formulations into the post-Reformation era, we will see how later Protestant and Catholic communities adapted and reinterpreted these historic definitions in their own doctrinal confessions.

## Key ideas
- The Athanasian Creed is an anonymous Latin text from the late fifth or early sixth century that articulates the historic Christian doctrine of the Trinity and the Incarnation.
- The creed explicitly teaches that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are co-equal, co-eternal, and share one single divine substance.
- It includes severe anathematic clauses declaring that salvation depends on holding this orthodox faith.
- While revered in Western traditions and used liturgically on Trinity Sunday in some denominations, the creed is not recognized as ecumenical by Eastern Orthodox churches due to the filioque clause.
- The text combines strict trinitarian monotheism with precise relational distinctions, guarding against both modalism and polytheism.
