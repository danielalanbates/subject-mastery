# Sequences and Their Limits

Imagine a hypothetical world where gravity is stronger, and a falling object drops four meters in the first second, sixteen meters by the second, and thirty-six meters by the third, following a rule of the square of the time multiplied by four. We can list these falling distances in order, creating an infinite ordered list of numbers where every position corresponds to a specific point in time. In calculus, we call such an ordered list of numbers an infinite sequence. Unlike the functions we have studied that accept every real number along a continuous line, a sequence is a function whose domain is only the counting numbers one, two, three, and onward. Every entry in this list has a position, and we index them using a subscript letter like n, so the terms are written as the first term, the second term, the third term, and continuing forever. Textbooks usually name the general term with the first letter of the alphabet and a subscript n; we will simply call it the general term, or b sub n, to keep the audio clear.

In our previous journey through calculus, we tracked how continuous quantities change smoothly over time, building functions that solved rates of change. Now, we shift our lens from continuous flow to discrete stepping stones. We want to know what happens to the terms of a sequence as our index n marches off toward infinity. Does the list of numbers settle down and approach a single, steady destination, or does it bounce around wildly or grow without bound? This question of ultimate destination brings us to the central concept of our lesson, which is the limit of a sequence.

To understand how a sequence behaves in the long run, we take the limit of the general term as n approaches infinity. If the terms grow closer and closer to a single finite real number, we say that the sequence converges to that limit. If the numbers fail to settle down, oscillating forever between different values or growing larger than any finite number, we say that the sequence diverges. This feels very similar to finding limits of continuous functions as variables grow large, which we explored when studying limits at infinity and horizontal asymptotes. The main difference here is that our variable n only takes on discrete integer steps rather than sliding smoothly across all real numbers. In fact, limit laws for sequences mirror those for functions, meaning that the limit of a sum, difference, product, constant multiple, or quotient of convergent sequences is simply the sum, difference, product, constant multiple, or quotient of their individual limits.

To make this precise, we use the formal epsilon N definition of the limit of a sequence, which is directly analogous to the epsilon delta definition we used earlier for continuous functions. We say that the limit of the general term as n approaches infinity equals a real number L if, for every positive number epsilon, there exists a corresponding cutoff, a whole number we call capital N, such that for every index n beyond that cutoff, the absolute value of the difference between the general term and L is strictly less than epsilon. This means that if you choose any tiny error margin epsilon, all terms of the sequence beyond a certain point will fall comfortably inside that narrow band.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Suppose we have a sequence whose general term is defined as the fraction where the numerator is n, and the denominator is the quantity n plus one. Let us write out the first few terms to build our intuition. For n equals one, the term is one half. For n equals two, the term is two thirds. For n equals three, the term is three quarters. As n grows larger, the numerator and denominator both grow without bound, which at first glance might look like a complicated struggle.

To find the limit as n approaches infinity, we can use our familiar algebraic tools. We divide every term in both the numerator and the denominator by the highest power of n present, which in this case is simply n. The numerator becomes one, and the denominator becomes the quantity one plus the reciprocal of n. Now, as n approaches infinity, the reciprocal of n shrinks toward zero. This leaves us with one divided by one plus zero, which evaluates simply to one. Because the terms of our sequence settle down toward the finite number one, the sequence converges.

Sometimes we can determine convergence without computing the exact limit value, thanks to the Monotone Convergence Theorem. This powerful theorem states that every bounded monotonic sequence must converge. A sequence is monotonic if it is entirely non-decreasing or entirely non-increasing, and it is bounded if all its terms stay between some finite lower and upper limits. Whenever a list of numbers marches steadily in one direction without flying off to infinity, it is guaranteed to hit a wall and settle down.

Let us look at a contrasting example where things do not settle down. Consider a sequence where the general term is negative one raised to the power of n. Writing out the terms, we get negative one, positive one, negative one, positive one, alternating forever without settling on any single value. Because this sequence never approaches a single limiting number, the limit does not exist, and the sequence diverges. Understanding whether these ordered lists converge or diverge lays the essential groundwork for our next step, where we will study infinite series and geometric series.

## Key ideas
- An infinite sequence is a function whose domain is the counting numbers, producing an ordered list of terms indexed by a variable such as n.
- A sequence converges if its terms approach a single finite real number as n approaches infinity, a behavior governed by limit laws analogous to those for functions and made rigorous by the formal epsilon N definition.
- The Monotone Convergence Theorem guarantees that any sequence that is both monotonic and bounded will converge to a finite limit.
- A sequence diverges if its terms fail to approach a single finite limit by growing without bound or oscillating endlessly.
