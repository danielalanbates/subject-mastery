# Order of Operations and the Properties of Arithmetic

Imagine walking into a room where someone hands you a piece of paper with a math expression written across it: five plus two times three. If you add five and two first, you get seven, and multiplying by three gives twenty-one. But if you multiply two and three first to get six, and then add five, you get eleven. Which answer is correct? In our last lesson, we walked the real number line and saw how numbers relate in order and magnitude, but mathematics cannot tolerate ambiguity. To ensure that an expression has one and only one correct value, mathematicians established a universal agreement known as the order of operations.

This agreed convention tells us precisely how to read and evaluate any arithmetic string. We evaluate groupings first, such as parentheses and brackets, working from the inside out. Next, we evaluate exponents. After clearing exponents and groupings, we perform multiplication and division from left to right. Finally, we perform addition and subtraction, also from left to right. Returning to our earlier puzzle of five plus two times three, the rules of the road require us to multiply first, yielding six, and then add five, leaving an unambiguous final result of eleven.

Yet the order of operations is merely a traffic signal; the deeper engine of arithmetic relies on structural properties that govern how numbers behave. The first of these is commutativity, which tells us that the order of addition or multiplication does not change the result. If we take four plus seven, or seven plus four, we land on eleven. Similarly, three times five equals five times three, both giving fifteen. Notice that subtraction and division are not commutative; taking five minus two is very different from taking two minus five.

Next comes associativity, which dictates how we group terms when we have a string of identical operations. When adding three numbers, such as two plus three plus four, it does not matter whether we first add two and three to get five, and then add four, or first add three and four to get seven, and then add two. In both cases, the sum is nine. Multiplication shares this property. Associativity allows us to drop grouping symbols entirely when working with purely additive or purely multiplicative expressions, simplifying our calculations.

The true glue connecting addition and multiplication is the distributive property. Consider three times the quantity four plus five. If we evaluate inside the grouping first, four plus five is nine, and three times nine is twenty-seven. Alternatively, we can distribute the factor of three to each term inside, computing three times four, which is twelve, plus three times five, which is fifteen. Adding twelve and fifteen gives twenty-seven. The distributive property allows us to multiply a sum by breaking it into manageable pieces, a technique that forms the bedrock of all algebra.

We also rely heavily on identities and inverses. The additive identity is zero, because adding zero to any number leaves that number unchanged. The multiplicative identity is one, because multiplying any number by one leaves it unchanged. Inverses undo operations. The additive inverse of a number is its opposite, such as negative four for four, which brings us back to zero. The multiplicative inverse is its reciprocal, such as one-half for two, which brings us back to one.

These structural rules allow us to justify every step of simplifying a multi-step arithmetic expression. Suppose we want to simplify the expression two plus three times the quantity four plus one minus five. First, using the distributive property, we multiply three into the quantity four plus one, rewriting the expression as two plus twelve plus three minus five. Next, by the commutative property of addition, we can rearrange the terms to group the constants, writing two plus twelve plus three minus five as two plus twelve plus three minus five grouped in a specific order. Applying the associative property of addition, we add two and twelve to get fourteen, fourteen and three to get seventeen, and finally subtract five to arrive at twelve. Together, these properties turn chaotic calculations into orderly logical chains. As we move next into fractions, decimals, percents, and ratios, these foundational rules will travel with us every step of the way.

## Key ideas
- The order of operations establishes a universal sequence—groupings, exponents, multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction—to ensure every arithmetic expression has a single, unambiguous value.
- The commutative properties of addition and multiplication allow us to change the order of terms without altering the final result.
- The associative properties of addition and multiplication show that the grouping of terms does not affect the outcome of a purely additive or multiplicative expression.
- The distributive property bridges multiplication and addition by allowing a factor to be distributed across each term inside a grouping.
- Identities and inverses, combined with the commutative, associative, and distributive properties, justify every step of simplifying multi-step arithmetic expressions.
