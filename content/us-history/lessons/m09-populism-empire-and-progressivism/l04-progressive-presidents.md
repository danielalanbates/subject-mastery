# The Progressive Presidents

Imagine a high-speed locomotive roaring across the American landscape at the turn of the twentieth century. For decades, the industrial giants of the Gilded Age had been driving this train, often prioritizing profit over the safety of their passengers and the health of the tracks. Muckraking journalists, settlement house workers, and local activists had spent years shouting from the sidelines. They demanded that the government step into the engineer’s cab. By the early 1900s, those demands finally reached the White House, transforming the presidency from a reactive office into a powerful engine for national reform.

Theodore Roosevelt, who ascended to the presidency in 1901 following the assassination of William McKinley, fundamentally changed the nature of executive power. Roosevelt brought an irrepressible energy to Washington. He believed in what he called the Square Deal, a term that captured his vision of a fair playing field for all Americans. For Roosevelt, this meant balancing the needs of business, labor, and the public. He did not want to destroy all corporations, but he was determined to regulate them. He famously used the Sherman Antitrust Act to break up massive monopolies, earning himself the nickname of the trust-buster. He believed that if a company grew too large and abused its power, the federal government had a duty to intervene.

Beyond the economy, Roosevelt was a pioneer of the conservation movement. He saw the rapid industrialization of the country and realized that the nation’s natural beauty and resources were not inexhaustible. Using his executive authority, he protected millions of acres of land, creating national forests, bird sanctuaries, and national monuments. He worked closely with conservationists like John Muir, though they sometimes disagreed on the extent to which land should be preserved versus managed for resource use. Roosevelt’s legacy here was the creation of a lasting federal commitment to environmental stewardship, setting aside vast tracts of wilderness for future generations to enjoy.

When Roosevelt left office in 1909, he hand-picked his successor, William Howard Taft. Taft was a different kind of leader. A former judge, he possessed a more legalistic and cautious temperament than his predecessor. Taft actually continued many of the progressive programs Roosevelt started. He initiated even more aggressive antitrust lawsuits than Roosevelt had. However, Taft lacked Roosevelt’s flair for public persuasion. He struggled to manage the growing fractures within the Republican Party. When he seemed to side with the conservative, pro-business wing of his party on issues like tariffs and land management, he alienated the progressive wing that had supported Roosevelt. This rift eventually led to a dramatic political explosion in the election of 1912.

The 1912 election was a three-way contest that showcased the different philosophies of progressivism. Roosevelt, feeling that Taft had betrayed the cause, ran as a third-party candidate for the Progressive Party, often called the Bull Moose Party. This split the Republican vote, allowing the Democratic candidate, Woodrow Wilson, to win the presidency. Wilson brought a different vision to the White House, which he termed the New Freedom. While Roosevelt’s progressivism accepted the existence of large corporations but sought to regulate them, Wilson’s New Freedom initially focused on destroying monopolies entirely to restore competition for small businesses.

Wilson’s early years in office were remarkably productive. He pushed for the Underwood Tariff to lower taxes on imported goods. He secured the creation of the Federal Reserve System to stabilize the nation’s banking. He also oversaw the establishment of the Federal Trade Commission to monitor unfair business practices. Wilson’s approach was rooted in the idea that the federal government should act as a referee, ensuring that no single interest group could dominate the national economy. Through these legislative victories, the federal government became a permanent fixture in the daily lives of Americans, tasked with maintaining economic stability and corporate accountability.

However, it is important to note that the progressivism of these presidents was not universal in its scope. Their reforms often focused on the urban middle class and industrial workers, but they frequently ignored or even worsened the conditions for African Americans. Wilson, in particular, allowed the re-segregation of federal offices in Washington. The Progressive era was a time of immense growth in federal power and humanitarian concern, but it was also a period of significant exclusion. The push for a more equitable society was often restricted by the racial and social prejudices of the time.

By the second decade of the twentieth century, these domestic concerns began to shift. The focus on cleaning up the cities and regulating the trusts was increasingly interrupted by the gathering storm clouds in Europe. As Wilson sought to balance his ambitious domestic agenda with the realities of a globalized world, the United States found itself drawn toward a conflict that would test the limits of its newfound international influence.

## Key ideas

- Theodore Roosevelt’s Square Deal aimed to provide a balanced approach to government by regulating trusts, protecting consumers, and conserving the nation’s natural resources.
- William Howard Taft continued many progressive policies, such as antitrust litigation, but his cautious approach and political disagreements caused a deep rift within the Republican Party.
- Woodrow Wilson’s New Freedom program sought to restore economic competition by curbing the power of large monopolies through the creation of the Federal Reserve and the Federal Trade Commission.
- While the Progressive presidents expanded the role of the federal government in the economy and society, their reforms often failed to address, and sometimes ignored, the systemic inequalities facing African Americans.
