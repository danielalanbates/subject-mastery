# The Birth of Pentecostalism

Imagine a dusty, unassuming building in the heart of Los Angeles in 1906. It was a former African Methodist Episcopal church on Azusa Street, now serving as a warehouse. Inside, the walls were bare, the seating was makeshift, and the atmosphere was electric with an intensity that defied the social norms of the era. People from vastly different racial, economic, and denominational backgrounds gathered here for daily services that stretched for hours. They were not there for liturgy or formal preaching in the traditional sense. They were there to seek a direct, personal encounter with the Holy Spirit, characterized by what they described as the gift of tongues and miraculous healing. This was the spark that ignited the global Pentecostal movement.

To understand how this movement emerged, we must look at the late nineteenth-century Holiness movement. Many Christians at the time felt that the church had become spiritually stagnant, overly focused on intellectual debates or social formality. In our last lesson, we discussed the rise of liberal theology, which sought to reconcile faith with modern science and historical criticism. In reaction to this perceived cooling of religious fervor, many believers sought a "second blessing." They taught that after initial conversion, a Christian could experience a distinct, transformative work of grace—a sanctification that purified the heart and empowered the believer for service.

By the early 1900s, some within the Holiness tradition began to push these ideas further. They argued that the "baptism in the Holy Spirit" was not merely about personal holiness, but was evidenced by the ability to speak in tongues, or glossolalia, just as the apostles did on the Day of Pentecost. This belief became the theological cornerstone of what we now call Pentecostalism. The movement found its most famous catalyst in William Seymour, an African American preacher who had been influenced by the Holiness teachings of Charles Fox Parham. Seymour’s leadership at the Azusa Street Revival was remarkable not only for its spiritual intensity but for its radical embrace of racial integration during a time of intense segregation in the United States.

The revival was not a quiet, orderly affair. Observers described a scene of fervent prayer, spontaneous singing, and diverse groups of people worshiping together with a joy that many found startling. The news of what was happening in Los Angeles spread rapidly. Visitors from across the country and around the world traveled to Azusa Street, experienced the services, and returned home to plant their own congregations. Because the movement emphasized immediate, personal experience over rigid institutional hierarchy, it proved remarkably easy to export. It moved quickly from the streets of California to the rural South, and soon across the oceans to Europe, Latin America, and sub-Saharan Africa.

As the movement matured, it began to organize into formal denominations, such as the Assemblies of God. These groups retained the core Pentecostal emphasis on the gifts of the Spirit, such as prophecy, healing, and tongues. By the middle of the twentieth century, the influence of these ideas began to spill over into older, more established denominations. This gave rise to the charismatic movement, where members of Catholic, Orthodox, and mainline Protestant churches began to embrace Pentecostal practices while remaining within their own traditions. Unlike the early Pentecostals, who often formed separate churches, charismatics sought to bring this new spiritual vitality into the heart of their existing denominations.

The growth of Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity is perhaps the most significant religious development of the twentieth century. From a small, marginalized gathering in Los Angeles, it has exploded into a global phenomenon, claiming hundreds of millions of adherents today. It reshaped the landscape of world Christianity by shifting the focus from Western, institutionalized structures to a more experiential, grassroots faith. This emphasis on the immediate power of the Spirit offered a compelling alternative to both the secularism of the modern world and the perceived dryness of traditional church structures.

As the movement expanded, it inevitably collided with the rigid boundaries of established theology. While some celebrated this new wave of fervor, others viewed it with deep suspicion, leading to intense debates over the nature of the Holy Spirit and the authority of scripture. These tensions would eventually contribute to the broader, often bitter, intellectual and cultural battles between conservative and liberal factions within the church.

## Key ideas

- The Pentecostal movement grew out of the nineteenth-century Holiness tradition, which emphasized a second, transformative experience of grace for believers.
- William Seymour led the Azusa Street Revival in 1906, which served as the primary catalyst for the spread of Pentecostalism as a global, multiracial, and experiential movement.
- Pentecostalism is defined by the belief that the baptism of the Holy Spirit is evidenced by miraculous gifts, particularly speaking in tongues.
- The charismatic movement later extended these spiritual practices into established Catholic, Orthodox, and mainline Protestant denominations, significantly altering the global religious landscape.
