# Fission, Fusion and Cosmology

When you look up at the night sky, you are looking at a vast engine fueled by mass itself. Every second, our Sun converts four million tons of matter into pure radiant energy. In our last lesson, we explored how unstable atomic nuclei undergo radioactive decay, shedding particles and energy to find stability. Today, we scale that nuclear view up to the most powerful reactions in the universe, examining how heavy nuclei split, how light nuclei fuse, and how these subatomic processes shaped the entire cosmos from its fiery birth.

The conversion of mass into energy is governed by the most famous relation in physics, stating that energy equals mass multiplied by the speed of light squared. Because the speed of light is an enormous number, even a tiny speck of mass contains a staggering amount of potential energy. To understand why certain reactions release this energy, physicists look at a fundamental curve known as the binding energy per nucleon. Nuclei near iron fifty-six sit at the peak of this curve, meaning they are the most tightly bound and stable. Nuclei much heavier than iron can split to move up the curve, while nuclei much lighter than iron can combine, or fuse, to also climb toward that stable peak. Both fission and fusion release energy because the final products are more tightly bound than the initial reactants.

Nuclear fission is one way to unlock this energy store. In fission, a very heavy, unstable nucleus, such as a specific isotope of uranium, absorbs a stray neutron and splits into two smaller daughter nuclei, releasing several high-speed neutrons and a massive burst of energy. Imagine a drop of water stretching out, pinching in the middle, and violently snapping into two droplets. That is the behavior of a fissioning nucleus. The total mass of the resulting fragments and free neutrons is slightly less than the mass of the original uranium atom plus the incoming neutron. That missing mass has not vanished; it has been converted directly into kinetic energy and gamma rays. If those newly freed neutrons strike other heavy nuclei, they can trigger a self-sustaining chain reaction, releasing continuous power.

The opposite process, nuclear fusion, powers the stars and forges many of the elements heavier than helium, up to iron. Instead of splitting a heavy atom, fusion forces two light atomic nuclei, such as isotopes of hydrogen named deuterium and tritium, to merge together. In deuterium-tritium fusion, the two nuclei react to produce a helium-4 nucleus and a high-speed free neutron, releasing a tremendous amount of energy in the process. Because like charges repel each other electrically, you must push these nuclei together with incredible speed and heat to overcome their mutual repulsion. Once they get close enough, the strong nuclear force takes over, pulling them together.

Let us walk through a simple calculation of stellar fusion energy. Suppose four individual protons, which are hydrogen nuclei, fuse to form one helium-four nucleus in the core of a star. If we measure the mass of the four separate protons, we find it is slightly greater than the mass of the resulting helium nucleus. The difference is about zero point seven percent of the original mass. If we take one gram of hydrogen and put it through this fusion process, we multiply that missing mass by the square of the speed of light, which is about nine times ten to the sixteenth square meters per second squared. The resulting energy is roughly six times ten to the eleventh joules, which is enough electricity to power an average American home for more than fifteen years.

This mass-energy conversion is why stars shine for billions of years. In the core of our Sun, gravitational pressure creates the intense heat and density required to sustain continuous hydrogen fusion. When stars exhaust the hydrogen in their cores, they begin fusing heavier elements in successive stages, forging carbon, oxygen, and iron, until the largest stars explode in spectacular supernovae, scattering these newly minted elements across space.

Scaling this up to the largest possible canvas brings us to cosmology, the study of the origin, evolution, and ultimate fate of the universe. By looking at how galaxies move away from one another, astronomers realized that the universe is expanding. If you run that expansion backward in time, classical general relativity leads us toward a singularity, a point of infinite density. However, physicists understand that this model is a limit of classical gravity where quantum gravity effects become dominant and our equations break down, meaning the absolute beginning remains an open question beyond classical theory. Scientists refer to this early hot, dense phase as the Big Bang.

In the earliest fractions of a second, the universe was an unimaginably hot soup of pure energy and fundamental particles. As the cosmos expanded and cooled, the temperature dropped enough for quarks to combine into protons and neutrons. Within the first three minutes, simple fusion reactions occurred across the entire universe, turning about a quarter of the mass of ordinary matter into helium while most of the rest remained hydrogen, in a process known as nucleosynthesis. Millions of years later, this gas would condense under gravity to ignite the very first stars.

Another major pillar supporting this cosmological model is the cosmic microwave background radiation. This is a faint glow of ancient thermal radiation filling every direction in space, left over from a time when the universe cooled enough for neutral atoms to form and let light travel freely for the first time. Physics has taken us on a remarkable journey across this entire curriculum, from measuring everyday motion and forces to unraveling the strange realms of quantum mechanics, relativity, and the birth of the cosmos itself.

## Key ideas
- The binding energy per nucleon curve explains that both the fission of very heavy nuclei and the fusion of very light nuclei release energy by moving toward the stable iron peak.
- Nuclear fission splits heavy unstable nuclei into smaller fragments, releasing energy and triggering chain reactions.
- Nuclear fusion combines light nuclei such as deuterium and tritium into heavier helium and free neutrons, releasing immense energy and powering stars.
- Cosmology applies physical laws to trace the expansion and evolution of the universe back to the early hot phase of the Big Bang, supported by evidence like primordial nucleosynthesis and the cosmic microwave background.
