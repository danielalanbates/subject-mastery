# Jim Crow and the New South

Imagine walking down a city street in the American South during the late nineteenth century. You see signs on water fountains, waiting rooms, and train cars. They are marked with two words: "White" and "Colored." This was not merely a social custom; it was the law of the land. As the federal government retreated from its commitment to protecting the rights of formerly enslaved people after the collapse of Reconstruction, a new social and legal order emerged. Historians call this the era of Jim Crow, a term derived from a derogatory minstrel show character, which came to represent a pervasive system of state-sanctioned racial hierarchy and segregation.

Segregation was only one pillar of this new order. To maintain white supremacy, Southern states systematically stripped Black citizens of the right to vote. They implemented mechanisms like poll taxes, which required payment to cast a ballot, and literacy tests, which were intentionally designed to be impossible for Black applicants to pass while exempting illiterate white voters through "grandfather clauses." By the turn of the twentieth century, these disenfranchisement tactics had effectively removed the vast majority of Black men from the political process, undoing the promise of the Fifteenth Amendment.

The legal enforcement of this system reached the Supreme Court in the 1896 case of Plessy versus Ferguson. Homer Plessy, a man of mixed racial heritage, had been arrested for sitting in a "whites-only" railroad car in Louisiana. The Court ruled against him, establishing the "separate but equal" doctrine. They argued that as long as facilities for both races were equal in quality, the state could legally mandate their separation. In reality, the facilities provided for Black Americans were consistently inferior, underfunded, and dehumanizing. The Court’s decision provided a constitutional veneer for a system that was inherently unequal.

Beyond the courtroom and the polling booth, the New South was defined by the threat of extralegal violence. Lynching—the public, extrajudicial murder of Black individuals by white mobs—became a tool of domestic terror. It was used to enforce racial boundaries, punish those who sought economic independence, and intimidate entire communities. These acts were often carried out with the silent approval, or even the active participation, of local law enforcement, creating an atmosphere of constant fear.

In the face of this systemic oppression, Black leaders and activists developed different strategies for survival and resistance. Ida B. Wells, a journalist and educator, courageously challenged the myth that lynchings were responses to crimes. In her investigative writing, she documented that these murders were actually calculated efforts to suppress Black economic and political progress. Her work brought the horrors of lynching to a national and international audience, demanding accountability and federal intervention.

Other leaders focused on different paths toward advancement. Booker T. Washington, the founder of the Tuskegee Institute, advocated for a strategy of "accommodation." He urged Black Americans to focus on vocational training, economic self-sufficiency, and moral character. In his famous 1895 Atlanta Compromise speech, he suggested that Black people should accept segregation for the time being, focusing instead on building their own businesses and schools. He believed that proving their economic value to the nation would eventually earn them their civil rights.

W. E. B. Du Bois, a sociologist and intellectual, offered a starkly different critique. He argued that accommodation only emboldened white supremacists. In his 1903 work, The Souls of Black Folk, he contended that Black Americans could not trade their fundamental human rights for economic opportunity. He called for full political equality, the right to vote, and higher education for the "talented tenth" of the Black community. Du Bois believed that the struggle for civil rights required constant agitation rather than silent acceptance.

These debates between Washington and Du Bois laid the intellectual foundations for the civil rights activism of the next century. While the era of Jim Crow seemed to lock the nation into a permanent state of racial division, the internal resistance of Black communities ensured that the fight for equality never truly ceased. Even as the South sought to rebuild its economy around industrialization and the plantation system, the struggle over the meaning of freedom remained the most significant tension in American life. As we turn our attention toward the American West, we will see how these same questions of land, power, and identity played out on the expanding frontier.

## Key ideas
- The Jim Crow era established a legal system of segregation and disenfranchisement that effectively stripped Black Americans of the political and social rights they had gained during Reconstruction.
- The 1896 Plessy versus Ferguson Supreme Court decision institutionalized "separate but equal" as the law of the land, despite the reality of systemic inequality.
- Lynching served as a brutal, extralegal mechanism of domestic terror used to enforce white supremacy and suppress Black economic and political advancement.
- Black leaders debated the best path forward, with Booker T. Washington advocating for economic self-sufficiency and vocational training, while W. E. B. Du Bois and Ida B. Wells demanded immediate political equality and an end to racial violence.
