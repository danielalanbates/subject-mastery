# Batteries, Fuel Cells and Corrosion

Think about the last time you reached for your phone or a remote control, only to find it dead. You likely felt a flicker of annoyance, but have you ever considered the invisible, relentless chemical dance happening inside that device? In our last session, we explored how galvanic cells harness spontaneous redox reactions to generate electricity. Today, we are moving that theory into the real world. We will look at how we package those reactions into portable power sources, why they eventually fail, and how we can stop the destructive, spontaneous redox process we call corrosion.

A battery is a self-contained galvanic cell. We distinguish between primary and secondary batteries. Primary batteries are designed for single use; once the reactants are consumed, the reaction reaches equilibrium and the battery is dead. Common examples include alkaline batteries, which use a zinc anode and a manganese dioxide cathode. Secondary batteries, such as lead-acid batteries in cars or lithium-ion batteries in phones, are rechargeable. In these systems, we apply an external electrical current to reverse the chemical reaction, effectively regenerating the original reactants.

Regardless of the type, a battery’s voltage often drops during use. This happens for three main reasons. First, as the battery runs, reactants are used up and products build up. The reaction quotient climbs toward the equilibrium constant, so the reaction has less free energy to release and the voltage falls, reaching zero when the cell reaches equilibrium. Second, internal resistance within the battery components limits the flow of ions. Third, we encounter overpotential, which refers to the extra voltage required to drive the reaction at a specific rate. Activation overpotential relates to the energy barrier of the electron transfer at the electrode surfaces, while concentration overpotential occurs when the reactants cannot reach the electrode surface fast enough to keep up with the current demand.

Fuel cells differ from batteries because they are open systems. Rather than containing a fixed amount of chemicals, they receive a continuous supply of reactants from an external source. In a common hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell, hydrogen gas is oxidized at the anode and oxygen gas is reduced at the cathode to form water. These systems often rely on catalysts, such as platinum, to speed up the reaction rates at the electrodes. Major types include proton exchange membrane fuel cells, which are favored for vehicles due to their compact size, and solid oxide fuel cells, which operate at high temperatures and offer high efficiency for stationary power generation. Because the reactants are constantly replenished, a fuel cell produces power as long as the fuel supply continues.

Now, let us turn to a less helpful redox process: corrosion. Corrosion is essentially an unintended galvanic cell. When iron rusts, one region of the metal acts as the anode, losing electrons, while oxygen from the air gains those electrons at another region of the surface that acts as the cathode. Moisture in the environment serves as the electrolyte, completing the circuit. Over time, the iron is consumed, turning into hydrated iron three oxide, which we know as rust. Because this process is spontaneous, it is thermodynamically favored.

We can fight corrosion by manipulating these redox conditions. One common method is galvanization, where we coat iron with a thin layer of zinc. Zinc is more easily oxidized than iron, so it acts as a sacrificial anode, giving up its electrons first to protect the iron core. We also use cathodic protection by attaching a more active metal, such as magnesium, to steel structures like pipelines or ship hulls. The magnesium corrodes in place of the steel, sacrificing itself to preserve the structural integrity of the main object. Other strategies include applying protective coatings like paint or plastic to block oxygen and water, or using chemical inhibitors that form a thin, protective film on the metal surface. By understanding the flow of electrons, we can turn a destructive natural process into a manageable one.

In our next lesson, we will see what happens when we force a non-spontaneous reaction to occur by pumping electrical energy back into a system.

## Key ideas

- Batteries are galvanic cells categorized as primary, which are single-use, or secondary, which are rechargeable through the reversal of chemical reactions.
- Voltage drop in batteries occurs due to shifts in reactant concentrations, internal resistance, and overpotential effects that impede the flow of charge.
- Fuel cells are open systems that provide continuous power by consuming external reactants, often utilizing catalysts like platinum to facilitate efficient redox cycles.
- Corrosion is an unwanted spontaneous redox reaction that can be mitigated through protective coatings, chemical inhibitors, or cathodic protection using sacrificial anodes.
