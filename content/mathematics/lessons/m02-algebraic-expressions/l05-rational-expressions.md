# Rational Expressions

Imagine baking a cake and scaling the recipe up to feed a crowd. You multiply every ingredient by three, turning fractions like one-half into three-sixths. We do something very similar in algebra when we work with rational expressions. In our previous lessons, we learned how to break polynomials apart into neat products of simpler factors. Now, we take those polynomials and stack them into fractions. A rational expression is simply a fraction where both the numerator and the denominator are polynomials. Just as a numerical fraction represents the division of two integers, a rational expression represents the division of two polynomial expressions.

Before we do any arithmetic with these expressions, we must look at what values are allowed. Because division by zero is undefined in mathematics, any value of the variable that makes the denominator equal to zero is strictly forbidden. These forbidden values form the excluded domain of the expression. For instance, if your denominator is the quantity x minus three, then x cannot equal three, because three minus three gives zero. When we simplify or transform rational expressions later, these original restrictions must stay with the expression through every step.

Simplifying a rational expression relies on a fundamental rule of fractions: we can cancel common factors, but we can never cancel common terms. Think about a numerical fraction like fifteen over twenty. Fifteen is three times five, and twenty is four times five. The common factor of five cancels out, leaving three over four. Notice that we cancel factors because they are joined by multiplication. We cannot do this with terms joined by addition or subtraction. In the expression the quantity x plus five over the quantity x plus two, you cannot cross out the x because x is a term, not a factor multiplied across the entire numerator and denominator.

To see how factoring helps us simplify, let us look at an example. Suppose we have a numerator of x squared minus nine, and a denominator of x squared plus five x plus six. Using our earlier factoring skills, we recognize the numerator as a difference of squares, which factors into the quantity x plus three times the quantity x minus three. The denominator is a quadratic trinomial that factors into the quantity x plus two times the quantity x plus three. Now both the top and bottom are written as products of factors. We spot the common factor of the quantity x plus three in both numerator and denominator.

We must note that x cannot equal negative three, because that would have made our original denominator zero. With that restriction in place, we cancel the matching factor of the quantity x plus three from the top and bottom. What remains is the simplified rational expression: the quantity x minus three divided by the quantity x plus two. This new expression behaves identically to our original one, except for that single excluded point where x equals negative three.

Multiplying and dividing rational expressions follows the exact same rules you learned for ordinary fractions, applied now to polynomials. To multiply two rational expressions, you factor every numerator and denominator completely, find any domain restrictions where any denominator equals zero, and then cancel any factors that appear in both a numerator and a denominator. Finally, you multiply the remaining numerators across and the remaining denominators across. Division works by multiplying the first expression by the reciprocal of the second expression, remembering to restrict any values that make either the divisor's numerator or denominator zero.

Adding and subtracting rational expressions requires a common denominator, just like combining numerical fractions. If the denominators are already identical, you simply combine the numerators over that shared denominator while keeping the denominator unchanged. If the denominators differ, you must find a common denominator by factoring each denominator first and building the least common multiple of those factors. Once both fractions share that common denominator, you adjust each numerator, expand, and combine like terms across the top. As we move next into radicals and rational exponents, you will see how these fraction rules and polynomial tools extend to roots and fractional powers.

## Key ideas
- A rational expression is a fraction whose numerator and denominator are both polynomials.
- Any value of the variable that makes the denominator equal to zero must be excluded from the domain of the expression.
- You may cancel common factors in a rational expression, but you can never cancel individual terms joined by addition or subtraction.
- Adding and subtracting rational expressions requires finding a common denominator by factoring each denominator completely.
