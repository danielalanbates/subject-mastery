# Acid-Base Titrations

Imagine you have a clear, colorless liquid in a beaker, and you know it is an acid, but you have no idea how concentrated it is. Perhaps it is a sample of vinegar from a local farm, or a cleaning solution that has been diluted. How do you find out exactly how many moles of acid are dissolved in that volume? You cannot simply look at it to tell. In the laboratory, we use a technique called titration. Titration is a precise analytical method used to determine the unknown concentration of a solution by reacting it with a second solution of known concentration, called a titrant.

To perform a titration, you place a specific volume of the unknown acid into a flask. You then slowly add a base of known concentration from a long, graduated glass tube called a burette. As you add the base drop by drop, it reacts with the acid in the flask. If you are titrating a strong acid with a strong base, the reaction is essentially a neutralization. You continue adding the base until you reach the equivalence point. This is the exact moment when the number of moles of base added is stoichiometrically equal to the number of moles of acid originally present in the flask.

You might wonder how you know exactly when that point is reached. Since both the acid and the base are colorless, we rely on a chemical indicator. An indicator is a substance that changes color at a specific acidity or pH level. You add a few drops of the indicator to your flask at the start. As you add the base, the solution remains one color, perhaps clear. The moment you cross the equivalence point, the pH of the solution shifts dramatically, and the indicator changes to a new color, such as a vibrant pink. This color change signals the endpoint of the titration.

The change in pH as you add base tells us a great deal about the substances involved. For a strong acid titrated with a strong base, the pH starts very low and stays relatively flat as you add base. As you approach the equivalence point, the pH rises very sharply. This reflects the fact that just a single drop of base can cause a massive change in the hydrogen ion concentration when the acid is almost entirely neutralized. Once you pass the equivalence point, the pH levels off again as the solution becomes increasingly basic.

If you are titrating a weak acid with a strong base, the process looks different. Because the weak acid does not fully dissociate, the starting pH is higher than it would be for a strong acid. Also, because the weak acid and its conjugate base form a buffer system, the pH rises more gradually at first. You might recall that buffers resist changes in pH. This makes the pH change for a weak acid appear more sloped before it eventually reaches the steep rise at the equivalence point.

Let us walk through a simple calculation. Suppose you have twenty milliliters of hydrochloric acid with an unknown concentration. You find that it takes thirty milliliters of zero point one moles per liter sodium hydroxide to reach the equivalence point. Because the reaction is a one to one ratio of acid to base, you know the moles of base added equal the moles of acid present. To find the moles of base, you multiply the volume in liters, which is zero point zero three, by the concentration, zero point one moles per liter. This gives you zero point zero zero three moles of base. Since the mole ratio is one to one, you have zero point zero zero three moles of acid in your original twenty milliliter sample. Dividing those moles by the volume of the acid in liters, zero point zero two, gives you a concentration of zero point one five moles per liter.

Choosing the right indicator is vital. You want an indicator that changes color exactly at the pH of the equivalence point. For a strong acid and strong base, the equivalence point is at a pH of seven. However, for a weak acid and strong base, the equivalence point is basic, meaning the pH is greater than seven. If you chose an indicator that turned color at a pH of four, you would stop your titration far too early. Matching the indicator range to the expected equivalence point ensures your measurement is accurate. Having mastered how we measure the concentration of dissolved substances through titration, we are now ready to explore how solids behave when they reach their limits of dissolution in water.

## Key ideas

- Titration is an analytical technique used to determine the unknown concentration of a solution by reacting it with a known volume and concentration of a titrant.
- The equivalence point is the exact moment during a titration when the moles of titrant added are stoichiometrically equivalent to the moles of the substance being analyzed.
- A pH indicator is selected based on its color-change range, which must align with the pH value expected at the equivalence point of the specific reaction.
- Titration of strong acids shows a rapid change in pH at the equivalence point, whereas weak acids show a more gradual rise due to the buffering effect of the acid-base conjugate pair.
