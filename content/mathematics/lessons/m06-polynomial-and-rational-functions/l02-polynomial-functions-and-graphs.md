# Polynomial Functions and Their Graphs

Imagine watching a roller coaster climb a massive peak, plunge into a deep valley, and then curve smoothly back upward into the sky. Its path is continuous, sweeping, and free of sharp corners or sudden breaks. In our last lesson, we studied quadratic functions, which trace out a simple, symmetric bowl shape called a parabola. Today, we expand that view to polynomials of any degree. A polynomial function is a sum of terms where each variable is raised to a whole number power, multiplied by a real number coefficient. These functions allow us to model far more complex, undulating curves that rise and fall multiple times across the coordinate plane.

To understand how these grand curves behave, we must start with the simplest building blocks: power functions. A power function has the form of a real number multiplied by a variable raised to a positive integer power, such as five times x cubed. When we look at a full polynomial, the term with the highest power of x dominates everything else as x grows very large in either the positive or negative direction. This domination dictates what we call the end behavior of the graph. The end behavior simply describes whether the graph points upward or downward on the far left and far right sides of the plane.

Two key features determine this end behavior: the degree of the polynomial, which is the highest exponent present, and the leading coefficient, which is the multiplier attached to that highest power. Think about a positive leading coefficient multiplying an even degree power, like two times x to the fourth power. Because any number raised to an even power becomes positive, both the far left and the far right ends of this graph must rise upward, just like our familiar parabola. To see this numerically, let us test the function two times x to the fourth power at two and at negative two. Two times two to the fourth power is two times sixteen, which is thirty-two; and two times the quantity negative two to the fourth power is also two times sixteen, which is thirty-two. Both inputs yield a positive output of thirty-two, showing that both ends rise. If you change that leading coefficient to negative, the entire graph flips vertically, and both ends point downward toward negative infinity.

When the degree is odd, such as three times x to the fifth power, the ends behave differently. A negative number raised to an odd power stays negative, while a positive number stays positive. This means one end of an odd degree graph points down while the other points up. For instance, if we test three times x to the fifth power with two, we get three times thirty-two, which is ninety-six; with negative two, we get three times the quantity negative thirty-two, which is negative ninety-six. Specifically, if the leading coefficient is positive, the graph falls to the left and rises to the right, resembling the line y equals x. A negative leading coefficient reverses that trend, rising to the left and falling to the right.

Between those distant left and right ends, the graph wanders through the middle, turning around whenever it shifts from increasing to decreasing or vice versa. The maximum possible number of these turning points for any polynomial is always strictly less than its degree. A third-degree cubic function, for example, can have at most two turning points, though it might have none. To verify this, consider the cubic function defined by x cubed minus three times x. If we test inputs around zero, at negative two the output is negative two, at zero the output is zero, and at two the output is two, reflecting two turns where the curve changes direction between those points. Furthermore, polynomials can cross or touch the horizontal axis wherever the output of the function equals zero.

The way the graph interacts with the horizontal axis depends entirely on the multiplicity of each zero, which is the number of times its corresponding linear factor appears. Consider the function where f of x equals the square of the quantity x minus two multiplied by the quantity x plus one. At x equals two, the factor x minus two has an even multiplicity of two. Because an even power never changes sign, testing values like one point nine or two point one yields a positive output in both cases, meaning the graph dips down, touches the horizontal axis right at two, and turns back around without crossing it. But at x equals negative one, the factor x plus one has an odd multiplicity of one, so the graph crosses straight through the axis from one side to the other.

By paying attention to the leading term, counting the turning points, and checking the multiplicity at every zero, you can sketch the essential shape of any polynomial by hand. Next time, we will explore how to take these polynomials apart through division and use the factor theorem to find their zeros with absolute precision.

## Key ideas
- Polynomial functions are smooth, continuous curves whose overall shape and end behavior are completely dictated by their leading term, which we demonstrated by evaluating powers at specific positive and negative numbers.
- The degree of a polynomial determines the maximum possible number of turning points its graph can possess, which is always one less than the degree, as shown by cubic curves changing direction twice.
- The sign and value of the leading coefficient, combined with whether the degree is even or odd, dictate whether the ends of the graph point in the same direction or opposite directions.
- The multiplicity of a zero reveals whether the graph cleanly crosses the horizontal axis or merely touches it and turns back, which we verified by testing numbers on either side of the intercept.
