# Authorship and Dating Debates

Imagine standing before an ancient cathedral that has stood for a thousand years. Generations of masons worked on its stones. Some repaired the flying buttresses, others carved new saints into the limestone, and later bishops compiled records that changed how visitors understood the floor plan. If you asked who wrote the cathedral, you would quickly realize that a single name cannot capture the story. The five books of the Torah, and the historical books that follow them from Joshua through Kings, present a very similar puzzle. For centuries, tradition attributed these texts to solitary heroes like Moses or Samuel, but a close look at the text reveals layers of history, editorial hands, and long centuries of development.

In our last lesson, we traced how Israel experienced exile and the painstaking post-exilic return, a time when the community gathered its scattered heritage and re-read its sacred traditions. This context naturally raises the question of how those foundational texts came together. When we look at the Pentateuch, meaning the first five books of the Bible, traditional Jewish and Christian views historically assigned all of them to Moses. Passages within Exodus and Deuteronomy describe Moses writing down God's commands. Yet, as early as the medieval period, commentators noticed peculiar details. For instance, the final chapter of Deuteronomy describes the death and burial of Moses, noting that no man knows his sepulchre to this day. It takes little imagination to realize that Moses likely did not write an account of his own funeral.

During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, biblical scholarship developed a formal method for analyzing these texts, known as the documentary hypothesis. Scholars like Julius Wellhausen observed shifts in vocabulary, duplicate accounts of the same event, and differing theological emphases across Genesis through Deuteronomy. They proposed that the Pentateuch was woven together from at least four distinct source strands over several centuries. These strands are commonly labeled by scholars as the Jahwist, the Elohist, the Deuteronomist, and the Priestly sources. Each was thought to reflect a different period, from the early monarchy to the Babylonian exile. Today the classic four-source model is itself much debated. Many scholars, especially in Europe, doubt that separate J and E documents ever existed and prefer models of fragments and later supplements. Conservative scholars, including many evangelicals and Orthodox Jews, hold that Moses is the substantial author, allowing for later updates such as the account of his death.

For example, compare the two creation accounts in Genesis. The first chapter uses a majestic, formal style and refers to God as Elohim, focusing on cosmic order and the Sabbath. The second chapter adopts a more earthy, narrative tone, using the personal divine name represented as Yahweh, and focuses closely on human relationships and the soil. To a traditional reader, these differences show the rich texture of divine revelation viewed from varied angles. To a critical scholar, they suggest distinct originating communities whose voices were eventually edited into a single, cohesive masterpiece.

The historical books from Joshua to Kings underwent a similarly complex process. Modern scholarship often speaks of a Deuteronomistic History, suggesting that an anonymous historian or school of writers living during or after the exile shaped these accounts to explain why Israel lost its land. These editors wanted to show that the tragedies of conquest and exile were the fulfillment of warnings given in Deuteronomy. They gathered royal chronicles, ancient war poems, and prophetic legends, shaping them into a theological narrative that evaluated every king by his faithfulness to the covenant.

Navigating these debates does not require choosing between rigorous scholarship and deep reverence. Many contemporary theologians, including scholars across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions, view the composition of Scripture as a divine-human symphony. God worked through real human editors, scribes, and communities, using their historical struggles, literary skills, and editorial processes to preserve His word for generations. The final product is a rich tapestry of faith, shaped over centuries. As we transition from the foundational stories of Israel into the lyrical expressions of our next lesson, we will explore how Hebrew poets and wisdom writers gave voice to the inner life of this same covenant community in the Psalms.

## Key ideas
- Traditional views historically attributed the authorship of the Pentateuch to Moses and the historical books to various prophets and leaders.
- Modern academic scholarship often analyzes the Pentateuch through the documentary hypothesis, identifying multiple distinct source traditions woven together over centuries.
- The historical books from Joshua to Kings are frequently studied as a Deuteronomistic History, shaped by editorial work during the period of the exile.
- Recognizing human editing and historical context allows readers to appreciate Scripture as a divine-human work crafted through real communities over generations.
