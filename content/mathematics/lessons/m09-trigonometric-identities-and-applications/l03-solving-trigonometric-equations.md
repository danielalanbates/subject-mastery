# Solving Trigonometric Equations

Imagine you are watching a rhythmic wave, perhaps a buoy bobbing in the harbor or the pendulum of a grandfather clock. The position of that object changes over time, following a repeating pattern that we describe using sine and cosine functions. Today, we move from simplifying expressions to solving equations that contain them. When we solve a trigonometric equation, we are asking: at what specific input values does the wave reach a certain height?

To begin, consider the equation: two times the sine of theta equals one. Our goal is to isolate the variable, theta. Divide both sides by two, which gives us the sine of theta equals one over two. We know the sine function represents the vertical coordinate on a unit circle. This value is one over two at two distinct angles within the interval from zero to two pi. These angles are pi over six and five pi over six. Because the sine function repeats its values every two pi radians, these two angles represent the starting points for an infinite number of solutions. To account for every possible solution, we add two times k times pi to each, where k is any integer. This periodicity occurs because the unit circle returns to the same vertical height every full rotation. We check our work by substituting pi over six back into the original equation: two times the sine of pi over six is two times one over two, which equals one. This confirms our solution is correct. If we had a ratio like the sine of theta equals five, we would have no solution, because the sine function never exceeds one or falls below negative one.

Often, equations resemble the algebraic polynomials you have mastered. Suppose we have the equation: two times the cosine squared of theta minus the cosine of theta minus one equals zero. Let a variable, such as u, represent the cosine of theta. The equation becomes two times u squared minus u minus one equals zero. We can solve this by factoring. The expression factors into the quantity two times u plus one times the quantity u minus one equals zero. Setting each factor to zero, we find that u equals negative one over two or u equals one. If an equation does not factor easily, you can apply the quadratic formula to the variable u, which is negative b plus or minus the square root of the quantity b squared minus four times a times c, all divided by two times a.

Now, substitute the cosine of theta back in for u. First, where does the cosine of theta equal negative one over two? The horizontal coordinate is negative one over two at two pi over three and four pi over three. Second, where does the cosine of theta equal one? Within the interval from zero up to two pi, this happens at zero radians. We add two times k times pi to each to represent all solutions. Finally, we check the solution u equals one by substituting the cosine of zero back into the original: two times the square of one minus one minus one equals two minus one minus one, which is zero.

Sometimes, an equation contains multiple functions, such as the sine of theta plus the cosine of theta equals zero. If we subtract the cosine of theta from both sides, we get the sine of theta equals negative cosine of theta. We might be tempted to divide by the cosine of theta. However, we must first consider the case where the cosine of theta equals zero. If the cosine of theta is zero, the original equation becomes the sine of theta plus zero equals zero, which means the sine of theta must also be zero. Since the sine and cosine are never zero at the same angle, the case where the cosine of theta equals zero provides no solutions. Therefore, we can safely divide by the cosine of theta to get the tangent of theta equals negative one. The tangent is negative one at three pi over four and seven pi over four. Since the tangent function has a period of pi, we can write the general solution as three pi over four plus k times pi. Always check your candidate solutions in the original equation to ensure no extraneous solutions were introduced during your algebraic steps.

## Key ideas

- Solving a trigonometric equation requires isolating the trigonometric ratio and identifying all angles that satisfy that ratio within the unit circle.
- When an equation reduces to a simple ratio, such as the sine of theta equals d, there are no solutions if the absolute value of d is greater than one.
- Because trigonometric functions are periodic, the general solution is found by adding integer multiples of the function's period to the primary solutions.
- When dividing by a trigonometric function during the solving process, you must separately analyze the case where that function equals zero to avoid losing potential solutions or introducing errors.
- If factoring is not possible for quadratic-form trigonometric equations, you must use the quadratic formula on the temporary variable to find the roots.
- Always substitute your candidate solutions back into the original equation to verify they are valid and to identify any extraneous solutions.
