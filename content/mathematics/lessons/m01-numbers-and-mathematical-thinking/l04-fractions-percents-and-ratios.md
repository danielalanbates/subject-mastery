# Fractions, Decimals, Percents and Ratios

Imagine baking a loaf of bread and trying to explain to a friend how much flour you used when the bag is only partly full. Whole numbers alone cannot capture the pieces left behind. In our last lesson, we explored the properties of arithmetic and how numbers behave under addition and multiplication. Now, we expand that view to parts of a whole, translating quantities between fractions, decimals, percents, and ratios. Each of these four forms is simply a different lens for viewing the exact same proportional relationship.

A fraction is an indicated division of two integers, written as a numerator over a denominator. Before we combine or compare fractions, we often need to simplify them to lowest terms. We do this by finding the greatest common factor of the numerator and denominator and dividing both by it. For example, six over eight simplifies to three over four because both six and eight can be reduced by dividing the top and bottom by two. When a fraction has a numerator larger than its denominator, like five over four, we call it an improper fraction. We can also write it as a mixed number, such as one and one over four, which combines a whole number with a proper fraction.

When we add two fractions, such as two over five plus one over three, we cannot add them directly because fifths and thirds are different-sized slices. To combine them, we must find a common denominator, which is a shared multiple of both denominators. Multiplying five by three gives fifteen. We convert two over five by multiplying its top and bottom by three, yielding six over fifteen. We convert one over three by multiplying its top and bottom by five, yielding five over fifteen. Now that the slices are the same size, we add the numerators: six plus five is eleven, giving an answer of eleven over fifteen.

Multiplying fractions is remarkably direct. To multiply two over three by four over five, we multiply straight across. The numerators give two times four, which is eight, and the denominators give three times five, which is fifteen, resulting in eight over fifteen. This works because a fraction represents a division, and scaling by a fraction means taking a proportional part of a proportional part. Division of fractions builds directly on this through the rule of multiplying by the reciprocal. Why does dividing by a fraction mean multiplying by its reciprocal? Because division asks how many times the divisor fits into the dividend, which is algebraically equivalent to multiplying by the inverted divisor. To divide three over four by two over five, we multiply three over four by the reciprocal of two over five, which is five over two. Multiplying three times five gives fifteen, and four times two gives eight, yielding fifteen over eight.

Moving between fractions, decimals, and percents is a matter of division and scaling by one hundred. A terminating decimal is a fraction whose denominator is a power of ten. To change three over four into a decimal, we divide three by four, which results in seventy-five hundredths, or zero point seven five. Not all decimals terminate, however. Some decimals repeat infinitely, such as one over three, which equals zero point three repeating, where the three goes on forever without ending. A percent is simply out of one hundred, so seventy-five hundredths means seventy-five percent. To convert a percent back to a decimal, we divide by one hundred by moving the decimal point two places to the left, turning seventy-five percent into zero point seven five.

Percent increase and decrease measure how a quantity grows or shrinks relative to its original size. Suppose an item costs twenty dollars and increases in price by twenty percent. We find twenty percent of twenty by multiplying twenty by zero point two zero, which gives four dollars. Adding that increase to the original twenty dollars yields a new price of twenty-four dollars. We could also achieve this in one step by multiplying the original twenty dollars by one point two zero, representing one hundred percent plus twenty percent. Percent decrease works the same way in reverse. If an item drops in price by twenty percent from twenty dollars, we subtract four dollars, leaving sixteen dollars. Sometimes we know the final amount after a percent change and need to find the original amount. Suppose a shirt costs thirty-six dollars after a twenty percent increase. Because the new price represents one hundred twenty percent of the original, we divide thirty-six by one point two zero, which gives an original price of thirty dollars.

Ratios compare two quantities by division, such as a ratio of three to two for blue marbles to red marbles. This means for every three blue marbles, there are two red marbles. Proportions state that two ratios are equal, allowing us to find a missing value. If the ratio of flour to water is three to two, and we use nine cups of flour, we set up a proportion: three over two equals nine over the unknown quantity. When we cross-multiply, we get three times the unknown equals eighteen, which works because we are multiplying both sides of the equation by the common denominator. Dividing both sides by three tells us we need six cups of water.

Mastering these forms of proportional reasoning allows us to handle scaling and measurement with absolute precision. As we move forward to our next lesson, we will see how multiplying numbers by themselves many times leads us into the powerful shorthand of exponents and scientific notation.

## Key ideas
- Fractions represent division, can be simplified to lowest terms, and convert between improper fractions and mixed numbers.
- Adding fractions requires a common denominator, while multiplying them involves multiplying numerators and denominators straight across, and division uses the reciprocal.
- Terminating decimals correspond to fractions with powers of ten in the denominator, whereas repeating decimals continue infinitely without stopping.
- Percents, percent increase, percent decrease, and finding original amounts from changes connect directly to decimal scaling.
- Ratios compare quantities by division, and proportions allow us to solve for unknown values.
