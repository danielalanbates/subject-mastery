# The Coordinate Plane

Imagine sitting in a vast, darkened theater with every seat numbered by its row and its distance from the center aisle. If someone tells you row twelve and seat five, you can walk directly to that spot in the dark without bumping into anything. That is the exact magic of the coordinate plane, a mathematical map that turns geometry into algebra. In our last module, we solved linear equations and inequalities. Today, we build that map with absolute precision, turning every geometric curve into an exact collection of numbers that we can study, measure, and transform.

To build this plane, we start with two perpendicular number lines that intersect at a single point called the origin, whose coordinates are zero comma zero. The horizontal line is named the x-axis, and the vertical line is named the y-axis. These two axes cross at right angles, dividing the entire flat surface into four regions known as quadrants. We number these quadrants using Roman numerals, starting with quadrant one in the upper right, where both horizontal and vertical directions are positive. Moving counterclockwise, quadrant two sits in the upper left, quadrant three in the lower left, and quadrant four in the lower right. Points that lie directly on either the x-axis or the y-axis do not belong to any quadrant at all. Every point on this map is described by an ordered pair of numbers, enclosed in the grouping words the ordered pair, such as the ordered pair three comma four. The first number gives the horizontal displacement from the origin, telling you how far to move left or right. The second number gives the vertical displacement, telling you how far to move up or down.

René Descartes is often credited with wedding algebra and geometry together through this system, creating what we call Cartesian coordinates, though earlier thinkers also played with the idea. When we write an equation with two variables, say, the equation y equals two times x minus four, something wonderful happens. An equation like this places a strict condition on numbers. A pair of coordinates satisfies the equation if, when you plug the first number in for x and the second number in for y, the statement is completely true. For instance, if you choose x to be three, then two times three is six, and six minus four is two, so y equals two. The ordered pair three comma two satisfies the equation. The graph of an equation is simply the complete set of every single ordered pair that satisfies it. When you plot all those infinitely many points, they form a solid shape, which in this case is a straight line sloping upward.

To understand a graph deeply, mathematicians always look for where it anchors itself against the grid, which means finding its intercepts. An intercept is a point where the graph crosses one of the axes. To find where a graph crosses the y-axis, you look at the vertical line where the horizontal position is zero. You substitute zero for x into the equation and solve for y. For our equation of y equals two times x minus four, replacing x with zero gives y equals two times zero minus four, which simplifies to negative four. So the y-intercept is the point zero comma negative four.

To find where the graph crosses the horizontal x-axis, you do the reverse. The x-axis is the line where the vertical position is zero, so you substitute zero for y and solve for x. For our same equation, setting zero equal to two times x minus four means you add four to both sides, leaving four equals two times x. Dividing both sides by two gives x equals two. So the x-intercept is the point two comma zero. Knowing these two crossing points alone gives you enough structure to sketch the entire line accurately.

Now that we can locate any point on our map and find where lines anchor to the axes, we are ready in our next module to measure the direct stretch between points and find the exact halfway mark between them.

## Key ideas
- The coordinate plane consists of a horizontal x-axis and a vertical y-axis intersecting at the origin, with coordinates zero comma zero, dividing the surface into four quadrants while points on the axes belong to no quadrant.
- Every location on the plane is given by an ordered pair of numbers, representing horizontal and vertical displacements.
- The graph of an equation is the complete set of all ordered pairs that satisfy that equation when substituted.
- Intercepts are the specific points where a graph crosses the axes, found by setting one variable to zero and solving for the other.
