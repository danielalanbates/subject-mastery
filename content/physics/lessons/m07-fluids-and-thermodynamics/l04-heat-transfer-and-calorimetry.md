# Heat Transfer and Calorimetry

Imagine pulling a heavy cast iron skillet off a hot stove. Even after you turn off the burner, the metal remains scorching for a long time, while a thin aluminum foil sheet cools down in mere seconds. Both objects absorb heat from the same flame, yet their temperatures change at drastically different rates. In our last lesson, we explored how temperature relates to the average kinetic energy of ideal gas particles. Today, we investigate what happens when that thermal energy actually moves between substances, or when it triggers a physical transformation from solid to liquid and beyond.

To understand why the skillet and the foil behave so differently, we must consider both the mass of each object and a property of its material called specific heat capacity. The heavy skillet has far more mass, so it stores far more thermal energy for each degree of temperature. Specific heat capacity tells us how much thermal energy is required to raise the temperature of a specific mass of a substance by one degree Celsius. Water, for instance, has a famously high specific heat, which is why oceans absorb vast amounts of solar energy during the summer without boiling away, moderating our entire global climate. Metals, by contrast, have low specific heats, meaning they heat up and cool down rapidly under the same conditions. The energy required to change a substance's temperature equals its mass multiplied by its specific heat capacity and by the change in temperature.

Let us walk through a quick calculation to see this principle in action. Suppose you have two kilograms of water, and you want to raise its temperature by ten degrees Celsius. The specific heat capacity of water is roughly four thousand one hundred eighty-four joules per kilogram per degree Celsius. Multiplying the mass of two kilograms by that specific heat, and then by the temperature change of ten degrees, gives us eighty-three thousand six hundred eighty joules of energy. That is a substantial amount of thermal energy, roughly equivalent to the kinetic energy of a one thousand kilogram car driving at about forty-five kilometers per hour. If you performed the exact same calculation for two kilograms of copper, which has a much lower specific heat, the required energy would be barely a tenth of that amount.

Thermal energy does not just change temperature; it can also alter the phase of matter. When you heat ice at zero degrees Celsius, the added energy does not make the ice any hotter. Instead, it breaks the intermolecular bonds holding the solid crystal lattice together, turning the ice into liquid water at that same zero degrees Celsius. This hidden energy is called latent heat. The energy required to melt a solid or vaporize a liquid equals the mass of the substance multiplied by its specific latent heat of fusion or vaporization.

Consider making a cool summer drink by dropping ice into a glass of warm tea. As the ice melts, it absorbs heat from the tea without changing its own temperature during the phase transition. We can measure these thermal exchanges using a device called a calorimeter, a well-insulated container that traps heat. By applying the principle of conservation of energy, we know that any heat lost by the warm tea must equal the heat gained by the melting ice and the resulting water. By setting up an energy balance equation, we can determine final equilibrium temperatures with remarkable precision in a closed system.

As thermal energy moves through materials via conduction, convection, and radiation, it constantly drives the dynamic processes of our natural world. Conduction transfers energy through direct physical contact between neighboring atoms, while convection relies on the bulk movement of fluids driven by temperature differences and buoyancy. Radiation, on the other hand, requires no medium at all, moving through the vacuum of space via electromagnetic waves. In our next lesson, we will tie these ideas together by exploring the overarching laws of thermodynamics, which dictate the direction of heat flow and the ultimate fate of all energy in the universe.

## Key ideas
- Specific heat capacity is the amount of thermal energy required to raise the temperature of a unit mass of a substance by one degree Celsius.
- The energy needed for a temperature change is calculated by multiplying mass, specific heat capacity, and the change in temperature.
- Latent heat is the thermal energy absorbed or released during a phase change without any accompanying change in temperature.
- Calorimetry applies the conservation of energy to measure heat exchanges and find final equilibrium temperatures in isolated systems.
