# Solving Linear and Rational Equations

Imagine you run a bakery and know that two identical batches of muffins plus five extra ones for the display case equal thirty-five muffins in total. How many muffins are in one batch? That everyday puzzle is an equation in disguise, asking you to find an unknown quantity hidden behind the operations of arithmetic. Building on our work with algebraic expressions, we now turn to equations, which are mathematical balance scales. An equation states that two expressions have the exact same value. Solving an equation means finding every number that makes that statement true, transforming a tangled expression into a clear, transparent statement of truth.

Our first major tool is the linear equation in one variable, where the unknown appears only to the first power. Think of it as a see-saw. To keep the balance, whatever you do to one side, you must instantly do to the other. Suppose we want to solve three times the quantity x plus two equals fifteen. We want to isolate the variable, stripping away everything around it layer by layer. Before we do, let us see the distributive property in action on actual numbers: three times the quantity four plus five is three times nine, which is twenty-seven; three times four plus three times five is twelve plus fifteen, also twenty-seven. Applying that property here, we expand the left side by multiplying three into the quantity x plus two, giving three x plus six equals fifteen. Next, subtract six from both sides to keep the balance, leaving three x equals nine. Finally, divide both sides by three, resulting in x equals three. We check our work by substituting three back into the original statement: three times the quantity three plus two is three times five, which is fifteen. The balance holds.

As we solve linear equations, we encounter three possible outcomes. Most linear equations have exactly one solution, where the variable is isolated to a single value like x equals three. Sometimes, after simplifying, variables vanish and leave a false statement, such as five equals zero, which means there is no solution because no number can ever make that statement true. In other cases, simplification yields a true identity, such as seven equals seven, which means there are infinitely many solutions because every real number satisfies the equation.

Sometimes, equations arrive cluttered with fractions, which can make our see-saw feel heavy and awkward. Just as we clear away clutter in a room, we can clear fractions from an equation in a single stroke by multiplying every term on both sides by the lowest common denominator. Suppose we are solving one half x plus one third equals four. The denominators are two and three, and their least common multiple is six. We multiply every single term by six. Six times one half x becomes three x, six times one third becomes two, and six times four becomes twenty-four. Now our equation is three x plus two equals twenty-four, completely free of fractions. Subtracting two gives three x equals twenty-two, and dividing by three gives x equals twenty-two over three.

This strategy of multiplying by denominators leads directly to rational equations, where the unknown variable itself lurks inside a denominator. Consider the equation two over the quantity x minus one equals three over x. To clear the denominators, we multiply both sides by the quantity x times the quantity x minus one. On the left side, the factor of x minus one cancels out, leaving two times x. On the right side, the factor of x cancels out, leaving three times the quantity x minus one. Expanding that gives two x equals three x minus three. Subtracting three x from both sides gives negative x equals negative three, so x equals three.

There is a vital trap here that every mathematician must watch for, known as an extraneous solution. Because division by zero is undefined, any value of the variable that makes a denominator zero in the original equation is strictly forbidden from entering our world. Suppose a rational equation leads us to an answer of x equals four, but substituting four back into the original denominator gives four minus four, which is zero. That proposed answer instantly self-destructs. We must reject it, leaving us with no solution at all for that problem. Always check your final answers against the original denominators before you celebrate. With these balance techniques mastered, we are ready to step beyond single lines and fractions to tackle how real-world stories are translated directly into equations.

## Key ideas
- An equation is a mathematical balance, and solving it requires performing the exact same operation on both sides to isolate the variable.
- Linear equations in one variable may yield one unique solution, no solution, or infinitely many solutions.
- Fractions can be cleared from an equation entirely by multiplying every term on both sides by the lowest common denominator.
- Rational equations involve variables in denominators, requiring us to multiply through by the common denominator to clear them.
- Any proposed solution that results in a denominator of zero in the original equation must be rejected as invalid.
