# Radical and Absolute Value Equations

Imagine you are designing a ramp where the length of the support beam must be calculated using a square root, or you are tracking a sensor whose voltage fluctuates symmetrically above and below a target value. In both cases, standard linear or quadratic methods fall short because the variable is trapped inside a radical or wrapped in absolute value bars. Building on our work with the quadratic formula, today we explore radical and absolute value equations, learning how to unlock these expressions and solve them with precision.

A radical equation has a variable inside a square root or another root. To solve it, your main goal is isolation. You must get the radical expression completely by itself on one side of the equation before you do anything else. Once the root is isolated, you eliminate it by applying the power rule, usually squaring both sides. For instance, suppose you want to solve the equation where the square root of the quantity x plus five is equal to x minus one. The square root is already isolated on the left. By squaring both sides, the radical vanishes, leaving x plus five on the left, and the square of the quantity x minus one on the right.

Expanding the right side gives x squared minus two x plus one. Now you have a standard quadratic equation: x plus five equals x squared minus two x plus one. Moving all terms to one side yields x squared minus three x minus four equals zero. Factoring this quadratic gives the quantities x minus four and x plus one, suggesting two possible answers: x equals four or x equals negative one. But here lies a crucial trap. Squaring both sides of an equation is not always a reversible operation, and it can introduce false answers known as extraneous solutions.

You must always check every candidate back in the original equation. Let us test x equals four. The left side becomes the square root of four plus five, which is the square root of nine, or three. The right side is four minus one, which is also three. That works. Now let us test x equals negative one. The left side is the square root of negative one plus five, which is the square root of four, or two. The right side is negative one minus one, which is negative two. Since two does not equal negative two, negative one is an extraneous solution created when we squared a negative number. We discard it, leaving only x equals four.

Sometimes equations are not purely radical at first glance, but are quadratic in form. Consider an expression with fourth powers and second powers, or fractional powers like the one-half power, which is simply a square root in disguise. If you have x to the one-half power minus five times x to the one fourth power plus six equals zero, you can use substitution. Let a temporary helper variable, say m, equal x to the one-fourth power. The equation rewrites as m squared minus five m plus six equals zero. Factoring this yields m minus two times m minus three equals zero, giving m equal to two or m equal to three. Substituting back x to the one-fourth power for m and raising both sides to the fourth power gives x equal to sixteen or x equal to eighty-one.

Absolute value equations require a completely different strategy based on distance from zero. The absolute value of a number is its distance on the number line, meaning it is always positive or zero. If the absolute value of the quantity x minus three is equal to five, it means the expression inside the bars is either five units to the right of zero or five units to the left. Therefore, we split the single absolute value equation into two separate linear cases: x minus three equals five, or x minus three equals negative five. Solving the first case gives x equals eight. Solving the second gives x equals negative two. Both must be checked, and both are valid. Knowing how to split these cases and manage radicals prepares us nicely for our next lesson, where we will extend these ideas into linear and absolute value inequalities.

## Key ideas
- Radical equations are solved by isolating the radical expression on one side and raising both sides to the appropriate power.
- Squaring both sides of an equation can introduce extraneous solutions, making it mandatory to check every candidate in the original equation.
- Equations with fractional exponents or repeating powers can often be solved by substituting a temporary variable to reveal a quadratic form.
- Absolute value equations represent distance and must be split into two distinct cases to account for both positive and negative directions.
