# Series and Parallel Circuits

Imagine walking through an older house where turning on the kitchen blender suddenly dims every living room lamp. That frustrating flicker is not just bad wiring; it is a clue to how electric currents behave when they share a single pathway versus when they have choices. In our previous lesson, we explored how components respond to electrical pressure. The voltage applied across a conductor divided by the resistance of that conductor equals the current flowing through it, a principle known as Ohm's Law. But real electrical systems almost always contain multiple components. How those components are wired together changes everything about the total resistance they present to the circuit and how the current flows through them.

There are two fundamental ways to connect resistors or appliances in a direct current circuit. The first is a series circuit, where components are linked end to end in a single continuous loop. Think of old-fashioned holiday string lights where every bulb is part of one path; if a single filament breaks, the whole strand goes dark. Because there is only one route for the charge to travel, the current passing through every component in a series circuit is exactly the same. However, the total resistance of the circuit is simply the sum of each individual resistance added together. If you add more resistors in series, you make it harder for the charge to flow, increasing the total resistance and decreasing the overall current.

Let us walk through a quick example of a series circuit. Suppose we have a twelve volt battery connected in series to two resistors, one with a resistance of two ohms and another with a resistance of four ohms. To find the total resistance, we add the two values together, giving us a total resistance of six ohms. Now, applying Ohm's Law, we divide the total voltage of twelve volts by the total resistance of six ohms. This tells us that a current of two amperes flows through the entire circuit, which is a moderate current similar to what a small kitchen appliance might draw. That same current of two amperes passes through the two ohm resistor, creating a voltage drop of four volts across it because two amperes multiplied by two ohms equals four volts. The same current then passes through the four ohm resistor, creating a voltage drop of eight volts across it because two amperes multiplied by four ohms equals eight volts. Notice that the sum of these voltage drops, four volts plus eight volts, equals the total battery voltage of twelve volts.

The second fundamental arrangement is a parallel circuit, where components are connected across the same voltage source along multiple independent branches. This is how modern household wiring is designed, allowing you to turn on a lamp without affecting your refrigerator. In a parallel arrangement, each branch experiences the exact same voltage. But unlike a series circuit, the total current from the power source splits up, with different amounts flowing through different branches depending on each branch's resistance. A path with lower resistance draws more current, while a path with higher resistance draws less current.

Calculating resistance in a parallel circuit works quite differently. As you add more parallel branches, you provide more pathways for the charge to travel. This actually lowers the total resistance of the circuit rather than increasing it. The general rule for finding the total resistance in parallel is that the reciprocal of the total resistance equals the sum of the reciprocals of each individual branch resistance. For instance, if you connect two identical resistors, each with a resistance of six ohms, in parallel, the total resistance is not twelve ohms. Instead, the combined resistance drops to three ohms because the current has twice as many paths to follow.

Let us walk through an example of calculating branch currents in a parallel circuit with different resistances. Suppose a twelve volt battery is connected across two parallel branches, where the first branch has a resistance of four ohms and the second branch has a resistance of six ohms. To find the current in the first branch, we divide the twelve volt voltage by the four ohm resistance, which gives three amperes. To find the current in the second branch, we divide the twelve volt voltage by the six ohm resistance, which gives two amperes. The total current supplied by the battery is the sum of these branch currents, three amperes plus two amperes, giving a total current of five amperes. That total current of five amperes is larger than either individual branch current, which makes sense because adding parallel pathways gives the charge more total room to flow, much like opening a second cash register line during a busy store rush.

Understanding these wiring configurations allows engineers to control both current and voltage precisely within electronic devices. As we move ahead to our next lesson, we will look at how these principles of current and resistance combine with voltage to determine electrical power consumption.

## Key ideas
- In a series circuit, components are connected along a single pathway, meaning the current is the same through each component and total resistance is the sum of individual resistances.
- Adding resistors in series increases total resistance and decreases the overall current flowing from the voltage source.
- In a parallel circuit, components share the same voltage across multiple branches, and the reciprocal of the total resistance equals the sum of the reciprocals of each branch resistance.
- Adding branches in parallel provides more routes for charge to flow, which decreases the total resistance of the circuit and increases total current.
- The total current in a parallel circuit equals the sum of the individual currents flowing through each separate branch.
