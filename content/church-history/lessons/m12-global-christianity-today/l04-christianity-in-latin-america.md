# Christianity in Latin America

For centuries, the sound of church bells was the heartbeat of every village in Latin America. The Catholic faith was woven into the very fabric of social and political life. Today it is home to the largest share of the world's Catholics. Yet, the twentieth century brought a transformation as profound as any in church history. The church in Latin America had to reinvent itself in the face of extreme poverty, political upheaval, and a rapidly changing religious landscape.

The Second Vatican Council, which concluded in 1965, acted as a catalyst for this change. It encouraged the church to engage with the modern world and pay closer attention to the struggles of the poor. This shift was formalized during the 1968 Medellín Conference of Latin American bishops. There, the hierarchy applied the teachings of the council to the regional context, famously calling the church to stand with the poor, a commitment that the bishops' next general meeting, at Puebla, in Mexico, in 1979, named the preferential option for the poor. Out of this movement grew liberation theology, which argued that the gospel mandate for justice required the church to actively dismantle structures of oppression. A key grassroots expression of this movement was the development of Base Ecclesial Communities, or small neighborhood groups where laypeople studied the Bible and addressed local social needs.

This movement sparked intense debate. By the 1980s, the Vatican expressed significant concern. In 1984, the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith issued an instruction on certain aspects of the theology of liberation. It warned that some versions of the movement relied too heavily on Marxist analysis, which risked turning the gospel into a political ideology. A second instruction in 1986 took a more positive tone, and that same year Pope John Paul the Second told the bishops of Brazil that a theology of liberation rooted in the church's teaching was useful and even necessary, while insisting that the church's social mission remain grounded in traditional doctrine and the authority of the hierarchy.

A different kind of revolution was happening simultaneously. Starting in the mid-twentieth century, evangelical Protestantism began to grow at an astonishing rate. This movement included a wide range of groups, but it was dominated by Pentecostalism, which emphasizes the immediate power of the Holy Spirit, speaking in tongues, and personal conversion. These churches were often led by laypeople and centered in the poorest neighborhoods. They offered an experiential connection to God that resonated with people seeking hope amidst economic instability. These churches also began to exert significant social and political influence across the region.

The Catholic Church responded to this changing landscape in several ways. One significant internal development was the Catholic Charismatic Renewal, which brought elements of Pentecostal spirituality, such as exuberant worship and healing, into the heart of Catholic parish life. Despite the rapid growth of evangelical movements, Catholicism remains the largest religious tradition in the region. Surveys in the early 2020s still found Catholics to be the majority, though estimates range from a little over half to about two thirds of Latin Americans, down from about 90 percent through the 1960s.

The global significance of the region was fully recognized in March 2013. Following the resignation of Pope Benedict the Sixteenth, the College of Cardinals elected Cardinal Jorge Mario Bergoglio of Argentina. Taking the name Francis, he became the first pope from the Americas and the first from the Southern Hemisphere. His election signaled that the center of gravity in the Catholic Church had shifted toward the global south. As the first non-European pope in over a millennium, Francis brought a perspective shaped by the realities of Latin American life, prioritizing the marginalized and asking the church to live simply and stand with the poor. Francis died in April 2025. His successor, Leo the Fourteenth, elected in May 2025, was born in the United States but spent many years as a missionary and bishop in Peru.

Today, Latin American Christianity remains a dynamic, complex landscape. It is a region where the ancient traditions of the Catholic Church continue to hold deep cultural power, even as they engage in a constant, competitive dialogue with the vibrant world of evangelical Protestantism. The story of the church here is a testament to the fact that Christianity is a living faith that responds to the hopes and hurts of the people. As we look across the ocean to the vast and diverse continent of Asia, we will see how the church navigates a very different set of historical and cultural challenges.

## Key ideas

- The 1968 Medellín Conference and the rise of liberation theology emphasized a preferential option for the poor, supported by grassroots Base Ecclesial Communities.
- The Vatican issued formal instructions in 1984 and 1986 to ensure that social justice movements within the church remained grounded in traditional doctrine rather than political ideology.
- The rapid growth of evangelical Protestantism and Pentecostalism has significantly altered the religious landscape, though Catholics are still a majority of the population.
- The Catholic Charismatic Renewal emerged as a major movement within the church, incorporating experiential spirituality to address the changing needs of the laity.
- The 2013 election of Pope Francis highlighted the shift of the Catholic Church’s focus toward the Global South and the concerns of the developing world.
