# Displacement and Velocity

Imagine you're planning a trip. You might look at a map and see that your destination is 100 kilometers away. But that number alone doesn't tell you how long it will take to get there, or even which direction you'll need to travel. To truly understand motion, we need to be more precise. This precision begins with clearly defining fundamental quantities like distance and displacement, and how they relate to speed and velocity.

In our previous lesson, we explored the difference between scalar quantities, which have only magnitude, and vector quantities, which have both magnitude and direction. This distinction is crucial for understanding motion. When an object moves, we can describe its path in two main ways: by its total distance traveled, or by its displacement.

Distance is a scalar quantity that measures the total length of the path an object takes, regardless of its direction. If you walk 5 meters north, then 3 meters south, your total distance traveled is 8 meters. It's simply the odometer reading in your car.

Displacement, on the other hand, is a vector quantity that measures the change in an object's position. It's the straight-line distance and direction from the starting point to the ending point. Using our walking example, if you walk 5 meters north and then 3 meters south, your displacement is 2 meters north. You started at one point, moved, and ended up 2 meters north of where you began. In one-dimensional motion, we often use positive and negative signs to indicate direction, for example, positive for movement to the right or north, and negative for movement to the left or south. A negative displacement, therefore, indicates motion in the direction opposite to the chosen positive direction.

Let's consider an example to clarify this. Suppose a car travels 100 kilometers east from a town, then turns around and drives 40 kilometers west.
The total distance traveled by the car is 100 kilometers plus 40 kilometers, which equals 140 kilometers.
However, its displacement is 100 kilometers east minus 40 kilometers west, resulting in a displacement of 60 kilometers east of its starting point. The car ended up 60 kilometers east of where it began.

Just as we distinguish between distance and displacement, we also differentiate between speed and velocity. Average speed is a scalar quantity, defined as the total distance traveled divided by the total time taken. It tells you how fast an object is moving on average, over its entire path. Because distance is always a non-negative value, average speed is always non-negative.

Average velocity, however, is a vector quantity. It's defined as the object's total displacement divided by the total time taken. Since displacement is a vector, average velocity also has both magnitude and direction. We can express average velocity as the change in position divided by the change in time. If we consider the initial position and the final position, the change in position is the final position minus the initial position. Similarly, the change in time is the final time minus the initial time. The standard unit for both speed and velocity is meters per second.

Crucially, speed is the magnitude of velocity. This means that if you know an object's velocity, its speed is simply the numerical value of that velocity, without the direction.

Let's revisit our car example. The car traveled 100 kilometers east, then 40 kilometers west. We'll say the entire trip took two hours.
The average speed would be the total distance, 140 kilometers, divided by the total time, two hours, which equals 70 kilometers per hour.
The average velocity, however, would be the total displacement, 60 kilometers east, divided by the total time, two hours. This gives us an average velocity of 30 kilometers per hour, east. Notice how the magnitude of average velocity can be very different from average speed, and it always includes a direction. While average speed is always non-negative, average velocity can be zero if an object returns to its starting point, because its net displacement would be zero.

While average velocity tells us the overall change in position over a time interval, it doesn't describe the velocity at any specific moment. For that, we use instantaneous velocity. Instantaneous velocity is the velocity of an object at a particular instant in time. Think of your car's speedometer: it shows your instantaneous speed. Instantaneous speed is the magnitude of the instantaneous velocity. To get instantaneous velocity, we'd also need to know the direction you're traveling at that precise moment. If you're moving along a straight line, this direction is simply forward or backward. Mathematically, instantaneous velocity is defined as the limit of the average velocity as the time interval approaches zero. In practice, you can estimate it by measuring the change in position over a very short time, such as a hundredth of a second.

Understanding displacement and velocity is the first step in describing how objects move. But what happens when an object's velocity isn't constant? What if it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction? That's where the concept of acceleration comes in, which we'll explore in our next lesson.

## Key ideas
- Distance is a scalar quantity representing the total length of the path traveled, while displacement is a vector quantity representing the change in position from a starting point to an ending point.
- Average speed is the total distance traveled divided by the total time taken, and it is a non-negative scalar quantity.
- Average velocity is the total displacement divided by the total time taken, and it is a vector quantity that includes both magnitude and direction, and can be zero.
- Speed is the magnitude of velocity, and instantaneous speed is the magnitude of instantaneous velocity.
- Instantaneous velocity describes an object's velocity, including its direction, at a specific moment in time, and is the value the average velocity approaches over shorter and shorter time intervals.
