# Mystics and Lay Devotion

Imagine standing in a churchyard in the year 1350. The air is heavy with anxiety. The Black Death, a devastating plague, has swept across Europe, claiming about one-third of the population. People are watching their neighbors, families, and friends fall ill and die within days. In this climate of fear and sudden mortality, the institutional church often feels distant. The papacy is embroiled in political turmoil, and the structures of society feel fragile. For the average person, the question is no longer just about theology; it is about how to find a direct, personal connection to God in a world that feels as though it is coming to an end.

This environment gave rise to a powerful movement known as mysticism. Mystics were individuals who sought an immediate, experiential encounter with the divine. They did not necessarily want to leave the church, but they felt that the external rituals and complex academic debates of the universities were not enough to satisfy the hunger of the soul. They wanted to know God not just through a priest or a textbook, but through personal prayer and inward reflection.

Consider Julian of Norwich, an English anchoress who lived in the late fourteenth century. An anchoress was someone who chose to live a life of prayer in a small, enclosed room attached to a church. After experiencing a series of intense visions of Jesus during a life-threatening illness, she wrote Sixteen Revelations of Divine Love. She is perhaps best known for the words she recorded from her visions, as found in her book: that all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well. Julian’s focus was on the deep, motherly love of God. She encouraged her readers to look past their own sin and fear to see the welcoming embrace of Christ.

In Italy, Catherine of Siena offered a different kind of witness. She was a laywoman, not a nun, who became deeply involved in the political and religious life of her day. She famously wrote letters to popes and kings, urging them to end the divisions within the church and to return the papacy from its exile in Avignon to Rome. For Catherine, mysticism was not about retreating from the world, but about engaging with it. She believed that the love of God must inevitably lead to the service of the neighbor. She spent her life nursing the sick and the marginalized, seeing in their suffering the very face of Christ.

While these mystics sought personal experience, another movement began to take root among the laity, which we call the Modern Devotion, or Devotio Moderna. This movement was founded by a Dutch preacher named Geert Groote. He inspired the Brothers of the Common Life, a group of ordinary people who wanted to live a life of holiness without necessarily taking monastic vows. These communities prioritized a life defined by simplicity and humility, and they placed a high value on the reading of scripture. They believed that true religion was found in the quiet, daily imitation of Jesus rather than in grand displays or complex philosophy.

The most famous product of this movement is a small book titled The Imitation of Christ, traditionally attributed to Thomas à Kempis. This book became a bestseller across Europe. It teaches that the heart of the Christian life is not found in intellectual mastery, but in a humble, constant turning of the heart toward God. In The Imitation of Christ, the author writes that it is better to feel compunction than to know how to define it. The book encourages readers to strip away their own ego and pride, making room for the presence of Christ to grow within them.

These movements showed that faith was becoming increasingly personal. The church was no longer just a massive, external institution that one belonged to by birth; it was a relationship that one cultivated through prayer and imitation. This shift toward the individual’s direct experience of God laid important groundwork for the future. As people began to prioritize their own reading of scripture and their direct relationship with the divine, they naturally became more critical of the corruption and intellectual dryness they perceived in the established church. The same desire for a more authentic, scripture-centered faith drove their contemporaries John Wycliffe and Jan Hus, whom we met in the last lesson, to challenge the established hierarchy.

The stage was being set for a new way of thinking. As the focus shifted toward the inner life and the simple, human example of Jesus, scholars and thinkers began to look back to the ancient world with fresh eyes. This growing interest in the wisdom of the past, coupled with a new confidence in the human capacity for learning, would soon blossom into the intellectual movement known as Renaissance Humanism.

## Key ideas

- The Black Death and the crises of the papacy created a climate of fear and uncertainty that drove many Christians to seek a more personal and experiential faith.
- Mystics like Julian of Norwich and Catherine of Siena emphasized direct communion with God, whether through quiet contemplation or active service to the suffering.
- Geert Groote and the Brothers of the Common Life founded the Modern Devotion, which encouraged laypeople to pursue holiness through daily, humble reflection on the life of Jesus.
- These movements toward individual piety helped shift the focus of religious life away from rigid institutional structures and toward the personal, internal experience of the believer.
