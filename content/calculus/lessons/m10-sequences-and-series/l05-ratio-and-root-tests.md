# Ratio and Root Tests

Imagine packing an infinite set of nesting Russian dolls into a single trunk, where each successive doll shrinks by a factor of one half. Because the size of the dolls decreases so rapidly, you know instinctively that the total height will not shoot off to infinity. When we study infinite series, we often face terms involving factorials or high powers, where comparing them to a simple geometric series feels clumsy or impossible. In our last lesson, we used direct and limit comparison tests alongside alternating series tests, which work wonderfully for algebraic fractions. But when terms grow or shrink exponentially, or involve factorials, we need a sharper tool that measures the ultimate scaling factor of the sequence.

That tool is the ratio test, and it gives us a way to examine the limit of the absolute value of the ratio of consecutive terms as index n grows without bound. Think about what happens when you look far out into a series. If each term is roughly a constant fraction of the previous term, the series behaves just like a geometric series. Let us call the limit of the absolute value of the term b sub n plus one divided by b sub n, as n approaches infinity, by the letter r. If that limit r is strictly less than one, the terms shrink fast enough, and the series is absolutely convergent. If r is strictly greater than one, the terms grow, and the series diverges by the divergence test. If r equals one, the test fails completely, and we must try another method because the series might either converge or diverge.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Consider the series whose n-th term is n factorial divided by two raised to the quantity n plus one. We want to test this for convergence using the ratio test. First, we write out the expression for the n plus one term, which replaces every n with the quantity n plus one, giving us the quantity n plus one, all factorial, divided by two raised to the quantity n plus two. Next, we divide this by the original term, which is equivalent to multiplying by its reciprocal, two raised to the quantity n plus one, divided by n factorial. When we simplify this product, the n factorial in the denominator cancels most of the numerator, leaving just the quantity n plus one in the numerator. The two raised to the quantity n plus one in the numerator cancels most of the denominator, leaving just the number two in the denominator.

Now we take the limit of the absolute value of this simplified result, which is the quantity n plus one divided by two, as n approaches infinity. As n grows larger, this limit clearly goes to infinity. Because infinity is strictly greater than one, the ratio test tells us immediately that this series diverges. The factorial growth in the numerator vastly outpaces the exponential growth in the denominator.

Sometimes, when terms are raised entirely to the power n, the ratio test becomes messy, but a sister method called the root test makes quick work of them. The root test looks at the limit of the n-th root of the absolute value of b sub n as n approaches infinity. Formally, when limits of roots fluctuate or do not settle down smoothly, the root test uses the limit superior of the n-th root to handle cases where the standard limit does not exist. Let us call this limit r as well. Just like before, if this limit r is less than one, the series converges absolutely. If r is greater than one, or goes to infinity, the series diverges, and if r equals one, the test is inconclusive. The root test is essentially a direct comparison against a geometric series, measuring the compound scaling factor of the terms over many steps.

Consider a series where the n-th term is the quantity three n plus two divided by four n minus one, all raised to the power n. Applying the root test, we take the n-th root of this expression, which simply strips away the exponent n, leaving the absolute value of the quantity three n plus two divided by four n minus one. We now take the limit of this fraction as n approaches infinity. By dividing every term by n, we see the limit evaluates to three fourths. Since three fourths is strictly less than one, the root test confirms that this series converges.

What happens when these tests yield a limit of one? For instance, consider the harmonic series, whose n-th term is one over n. If we apply the ratio test, the limit of the absolute value of the term b sub n plus one divided by b sub n evaluates to one, leaving the test completely inconclusive. To resolve this, we can turn to the integral test, which shows that the improper integral of one over x from one to infinity diverges, meaning the harmonic series diverges as well. These powerful tools pave the way for our next journey, where we will build entire functions out of infinite polynomial sums using power series.

## Key ideas
- The ratio test evaluates the limit of the absolute value of consecutive term ratios to determine convergence by comparison to a geometric series.
- A ratio limit strictly less than one guarantees absolute convergence, a limit greater than one ensures divergence, and a limit of one yields no conclusion.
- The root test examines the limit superior of the n-th root of the absolute value of the n-th term, making it especially useful for expressions raised entirely to the power n.
- When either test yields a limit of one and becomes inconclusive, alternative methods such as the integral test or limit comparison test can resolve the behavior of the series.
