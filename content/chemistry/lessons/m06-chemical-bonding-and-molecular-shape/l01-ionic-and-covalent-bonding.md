# Ionic and Covalent Bonding

Imagine you are holding a pinch of common table salt in one hand and a clear plastic bottle of water in the other. Both substances are essential to life, yet they are held together by two fundamentally different types of glue. In our previous look at the periodic table, we saw how elements organize themselves by their electron configurations. Now, we explore how those same electrons allow atoms to stick together. Whether atoms trade electrons or share them, the driving force is almost always the same: achieving a stable, low-energy state, usually by filling their outermost electron shells to mimic the noble gases.

When a metal atom, like sodium, meets a nonmetal atom, like chlorine, they often engage in a complete transfer of electrons. Sodium has one lone electron in its outer shell that it is quite eager to lose, while chlorine is just one electron short of a full, stable set. Sodium hands that electron over to chlorine. This transfer leaves the sodium with a charge of positive one and the chlorine with a charge of negative one. Because opposite charges attract, these two ions snap together like magnets. This is an ionic bond. These ions do not just form simple pairs; they arrange themselves into vast, repeating, three-dimensional geometric grids we call ionic solids.

This lattice structure is why salt crystals are brittle and have high melting points. To understand the strength of these bonds, we look at lattice energy. Lattice energy is the quantitative measure of the energy released when gaseous ions combine to form an ionic solid. It is also the amount of energy required to pull that lattice apart into individual ions. Because the electrostatic attraction is so strong across the entire grid, the lattice energy is quite high, which explains why ionic solids are rigid and difficult to melt. Furthermore, these solids are generally poor conductors of electricity in their solid form because the ions are locked in place. However, when we melt an ionic solid or dissolve it in water, the ions become free to move. This mobility allows the liquid or solution to conduct electricity, as the charged ions act as carriers for the electrical current.

Not every atom is willing to give away its electrons so easily. When two nonmetals meet, they often find themselves in a standoff. Neither atom is strong enough to steal an electron from the other, so they compromise by sharing. This is a covalent bond. Think of it like two people holding onto opposite sides of the same rope. By sharing a pair of electrons, both atoms can count those electrons as part of their own stable outer shell. This shared pair acts as the glue that holds the two nuclei together.

How do we predict whether a bond will be ionic or covalent? We look at electronegativity, which is a measure of how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons. If two atoms have very similar electronegativity values, they share electrons equally, creating a nonpolar covalent bond. If one atom is significantly more electronegative than the other, it will hog the shared electrons, pulling them closer to its own nucleus. This creates a polar covalent bond, where the bond has a slight positive end and a slight negative end. If the difference in electronegativity is extreme, the electron is pulled away entirely, and we get an ionic bond. While some textbooks suggest a specific numerical cutoff for this difference, such as one point seven, many chemists view this as a continuous spectrum rather than a hard boundary.

Think of the bond between hydrogen and chlorine in hydrogen chloride gas. Chlorine is much better at pulling electrons than hydrogen is. Consequently, the shared electron pair spends most of its time orbiting the chlorine, giving it a partial negative charge, while the hydrogen is left with a partial positive charge. This polarity is a sliding scale. Chemists generally agree that a large difference in electronegativity leads to ionic character, while smaller differences lead to varying degrees of covalent polarity.

The strength of these bonds is a major factor in how matter behaves. Covalent bonds are generally very strong because they involve the direct, dense overlap of electron clouds between specific atoms. Ionic bonds are also incredibly strong, but they are defined by the total cumulative attraction across the entire crystal lattice. When we look at bond energy, or the energy required to break a bond, we see that stronger bonds release more energy when they form and require more energy to pull apart. Understanding these interactions is the first step toward predicting how molecules will react and what shapes they will eventually take in space. We have seen how atoms bond, and soon we will look at how to map these connections using specific structural representations.

## Key ideas

- Ionic bonds form when atoms with very different electronegativities transfer electrons, creating charged ions that attract each other into rigid crystal lattices.
- Lattice energy serves as the quantitative measure of the strength of an ionic bond, representing the energy required to break the crystal structure into gaseous ions.
- Ionic solids are generally poor conductors of electricity, but they become highly conductive when melted or dissolved in water because the ions are free to move.
- Covalent bonds occur when atoms share pairs of electrons to achieve stability, with the nature of the sharing determined by the electronegativity difference between the atoms.
- Electronegativity acts as a spectrum where a small difference leads to nonpolar covalent bonds, a moderate difference leads to polar covalent bonds, and a large difference leads to ionic bonds.
