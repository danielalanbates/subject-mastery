# Information, Risk and Insurance

Imagine you are trying to buy health insurance, and the company has no idea whether you run marathons every weekend or spend your evenings eating potato chips on the couch. Because you know your own health habits much better than the insurer does, a hidden imbalance of knowledge is at play. In our previous lessons, we saw how market failures can stem from physical spillovers like pollution or public goods like national defense. Today, we turn to a different kind of market failure, one born not of smoke or steel, but of ignorance and hidden truths. We are looking at how information, risk, and insurance interact to distort the marketplace.

To understand this friction, economists use the concept of asymmetric information. This simply means that one party in a transaction possesses more or better knowledge than the other party. In a frictionless textbook market, buyers and sellers share equal awareness of product quality and risk. But in the real world, information is costly and unevenly distributed. When an insurance company tries to protect people against uncertainty, this information gap creates two major hazards. The first is adverse selection, which occurs before a contract is signed. The second is moral hazard, which happens after the agreement is in place.

Let us look closely at adverse selection. Suppose an auto insurance company offers a standard policy for one thousand dollars a year. Who is most likely to buy this policy? Safe drivers who rarely leave their driveways might decide the price is too high and skip coverage. Meanwhile, drivers who know they are reckless, or who frequently commute through congested city streets, will gladly pay the thousand dollars because they know they will likely cost the company far more in payouts. The insurance pool thus becomes adversely selected. It skews heavily toward high-risk individuals. As the average risk in the pool goes up, the insurer must raise prices to cover the mounting claims. This price hike drives away even more of the moderately safe drivers, triggering a vicious cycle that can cause the entire market to collapse under its own weight.

To combat adverse selection, insurance companies spend immense resources on screening and risk assessment. They ask detailed medical history questions, check driving records, and install tracking devices in cars to monitor actual behavior. By forcing individuals to reveal their private information, insurers can tailor prices more accurately to individual risk profiles, pulling the market back from total breakdown. Employers also help mitigate this in group health insurance markets by pooling healthy and unhealthy workers together under a single workplace plan.

Once an insurance policy is successfully purchased, a second problem arises: moral hazard. This occurs when an insured person changes their behavior in a risky direction because they no longer bear the full cost of their actions. Imagine a homeowner who insures their house against theft for its full market value. Before getting the policy, they might have locked every door, installed motion-activated lights, and bought a sophisticated alarm system. After getting comprehensive insurance, they might become careless, leaving doors unlocked or windows open because they know the insurance company will replace any stolen items anyway. The presence of the safety net alters incentives, leading to more reckless behavior and higher overall costs for society.

Insurers use clever mechanisms to fight moral hazard and align incentives once again. They introduce deductibles, which require the policyholder to pay the first few hundred dollars of any claim out of pocket. They also use copayments and coinsurance clauses, meaning the insured person shares a portion of every loss, though these features are not universal to every single policy or claim type. By keeping a portion of the financial risk squarely on the shoulders of the consumer, insurers ensure that people stay vigilant, lock their doors, and drive carefully.

Market economists note that government intervention often steps in where private information markets falter or exclude vulnerable populations. Compulsory public programs, such as social security or national health insurance schemes, force everyone into the risk pool, largely removing adverse selection by ensuring that healthy and sick people alike share the burden. Yet government programs face their own incentive challenges, proving that information asymmetries shape public policy just as much as private contracts. As we move forward, we will see how similar regulatory challenges emerge when governments try to police corporate market power and prevent monopolies from exploiting consumers.

## Key ideas
- Asymmetric information occurs when one party in an economic transaction possesses more or better knowledge than the other party.
- Adverse selection happens before a contract is signed, when high-risk individuals are more likely to seek coverage, driving up prices and potentially collapsing the market.
- Moral hazard arises after a contract is signed, when insured individuals take greater risks because they are shielded from the full consequences of their actions.
- Insurers use screening tools and risk classification to combat adverse selection, and employ deductibles and copayments to reduce moral hazard.
