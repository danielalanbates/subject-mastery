# The Church of the East in Asia

Picture yourself standing on a quiet road in the heart of modern-day China, looking at a stone monument that stands nearly ten feet tall. It is topped with a cross rising from a stylized cloud, with lotus petals carved at the base. If you were to read the ancient Chinese characters carved into its face, you would encounter a story that challenges the common assumption that Christianity was exclusively a Western faith during the Middle Ages. This is the Xi'an stele, erected in 781 CE. It tells the story of missionaries who traveled thousands of miles from Persia across the Silk Road to bring the gospel to the Tang Dynasty.

While we often focus on the Church in Rome or Byzantium, there was a vibrant, distinct tradition known as the Church of the East. Its members preferred this title, though they were often referred to historically as the Nestorian Church. They operated outside the boundaries of the Roman Empire, flourishing in the Sassanid Persian Empire. After the Council of Ephesus in 431 CE, which condemned the teachings of Nestorius, this church increasingly distanced itself from the theological definitions favored in Constantinople. They emphasized the distinction between the humanity and divinity of Christ, a position that allowed them to thrive in cultures where the imperial theology of the West was viewed with suspicion.

From their base in Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq, these Christians were remarkably mobile. They were not merely passive observers of history; they were merchants, scholars, and physicians who followed the trade routes. By the sixth century, they had established thriving communities across Persia and Central Asia. They moved into the regions we now know as Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan, and along the sea lanes of the Indian Ocean they kept close ties with the Christians of India. In southern India, the Saint Thomas Christians claim a heritage dating back to the apostle Thomas himself, and they maintained strong ties with the Church of the East for many centuries.

Their reach into China is perhaps the most striking chapter of their mission. In 635 CE, a monk named Alopen arrived at the Tang capital of Chang'an, modern-day Xi'an. The Tang Dynasty was a cosmopolitan era, characterized by an openness to foreign ideas and religions. The emperor welcomed Alopen and even granted him permission to build monasteries. The Xi'an stele, which we mentioned earlier, serves as a formal record of this encounter. It describes the Christian faith in terms that would be understandable to a Chinese audience, using philosophical language to explain the nature of God and the redemption offered through Christ.

The life of these communities was one of adaptation and resilience. Their scriptures and liturgy were in Syriac, but they also translated Christian texts into local languages such as Sogdian and Chinese. In China, they functioned within a society dominated by Buddhism, Taoism, and Confucianism. They were often viewed as a Persian religion, which provided a level of protection but also made them vulnerable to changes in imperial policy. When the political winds shifted, as they did in the mid-ninth century during a period of suppression against foreign influences, these communities struggled to survive.

Despite these challenges, the Church of the East maintained a robust structure. They were led by a Catholicos, or patriarch, based in the city of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, near modern Baghdad. This leadership provided a sense of unity across vast distances. Their bishops oversaw a network of schools and monasteries that preserved ancient knowledge, including Greek philosophy and medicine, which they would eventually pass on to the Islamic world. Their legacy is not found in grand cathedrals that still stand today, but in the enduring witness of ancient communities that spanned the breadth of the Asian continent.

This story of expansion and theological independence provides a necessary contrast to the developments happening in the Mediterranean world. While the Church of the East was navigating the complexities of the Tang court and the Silk Road, the Christians of the Byzantine Empire were becoming increasingly embroiled in a fierce debate over the use of sacred images in worship. This tension would soon lead to a period of intense religious crisis known as iconoclasm, which would define the relationship between the state and the church in the East for generations to come.

## Key ideas
- The Church of the East, often historically called the Nestorian Church, established a vast network of faith that stretched from Persia across Central Asia and into India and China.
- Unlike the churches within the Roman or Byzantine empires, the Church of the East developed its own distinct theological vocabulary and hierarchical structure centered in Mesopotamia.
- The Xi'an stele of 781 CE provides primary evidence of the official reception and state-sanctioned growth of Christianity during the Tang Dynasty in China.
- These missionaries were highly adaptive, using trade routes and scholarly engagement to integrate into diverse cultures while maintaining their unique liturgical identity in the Syriac language.
