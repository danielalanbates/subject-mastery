# Sleep Disorders

Imagine lying in bed, exhausted, yet your mind is racing. You check the clock, only to find another hour has passed in total wakefulness. This is a common experience, but for about ten percent of adults, chronic insomnia is a recurring battle. In our last session, we explored how our brain cycles through stages of rest. Today, we turn to what happens when those biological systems go off track. We are looking at sleep disorders, which are conditions that consistently impair our ability to get the restorative rest our bodies require.

The most common of these is insomnia. It is defined by a persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep that interferes with daily functioning. Clinicians typically use the criteria found in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders to diagnose it. While it is tempting to reach for sleep aids, multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses show that cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is the most effective first-line treatment. This approach uses stimulus control to associate the bed only with sleep, sleep restriction to consolidate rest, and cognitive restructuring to address worries about sleep. The evidence for this therapy is strong and consistent across many studies.

A very different, and potentially dangerous, condition is sleep apnea. This is a disorder where a person repeatedly stops breathing during the night. Risk factors include obesity, older age, and male sex. Untreated sleep apnea is associated with a higher risk of hypertension, stroke, and coronary artery disease. There are two main types. Obstructive sleep apnea involves a physical blockage of the airway, often treated with a continuous positive airway pressure machine, or CPAP, which uses a mask to keep the airway open. Central sleep apnea, however, involves a failure of the brain to signal the muscles to breathe. Because the causes differ, central sleep apnea requires different medical interventions, such as adaptive servo-ventilation. Specialists often identify these disorders through polysomnography, a comprehensive sleep study, or actigraphy, which uses wearable sensors to monitor rest patterns.

Then there is narcolepsy, a neurological disorder often appearing in adolescence or young adulthood. It involves the loss of hypocretin-producing neurons in the lateral hypothalamus, a condition currently hypothesized to be autoimmune in nature. A person with narcolepsy may experience sudden, uncontrollable sleep attacks. Some also experience cataplexy, a sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by strong emotions. While there is no cure, clinical evidence supports the use of stimulants to maintain alertness and other medications to regulate sleep cycles.

We should also mention parasomnias, which are unwanted physical events occurring during sleep, such as sleepwalking or night terrors. Unlike nightmares, which occur during rapid eye movement sleep, night terrors happen during deep, non-rapid eye movement sleep. The person may scream, but they usually have no memory of the event. These are most common in children and often fade as the nervous system matures.

It is important to understand the relationship between sleep and health. Research consistently shows a correlation between chronic sleep deprivation and impaired concentration, as well as weakened immune function. Studies also show a strong correlation between poor sleep and symptoms of anxiety and depression. While these associations are well-documented, it is important to distinguish correlation from causation; sleep deprivation may exacerbate mental health symptoms, but those symptoms can also cause sleep loss, creating a bidirectional link. If you or someone you know is struggling with severe distress, help is available. In the United States, you can call or text nine eight eight to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

As we move forward, many people try to alter their sleep patterns through substances. In our next lesson, we will examine how psychoactive drugs interact with brain chemistry and the science behind how they affect our consciousness.

## Key ideas

- Insomnia affects about ten percent of adults, and randomized controlled trials show that cognitive behavioral therapy is the most effective evidence-based treatment.
- Sleep apnea, which is associated with serious cardiovascular risks, requires different medical treatments depending on whether the cause is physical obstruction or a failure of the brain's breathing signals.
- Narcolepsy is a neurological disorder linked to the loss of hypocretin-producing neurons in the hypothalamus, often managed with stimulant medications.
- Sleep deprivation is strongly correlated with impaired cognitive and immune function, as well as higher levels of anxiety and depression, though the direction of these links is often bidirectional.
