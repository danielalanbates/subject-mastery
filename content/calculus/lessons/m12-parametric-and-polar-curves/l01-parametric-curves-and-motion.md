# Parametric Curves and Motion

Imagine tracking a bird flying through the mist. If you only look at its horizontal position on the ground, or only at its height, you miss the true shape of its flight. In standard single variable calculus, we are used to functions where every input value of x produces a single output value of y. But many paths in nature and physics double back on themselves or loop around. To capture motion that moves both left and right and up and down at the exact same time, we need to free both coordinates from each other. We introduce a third hidden variable, time, which mathematicians usually call t, and let both x and y depend upon it separately.

Welcome to the start of our exploration into parametric and polar curves. In our recent work, we approximated complex functions by building infinite strings of polynomial terms using Taylor series. Now, we leave that linear expansion behind and turn toward curves defined by parametric equations. A parametric curve describes a path where both the horizontal position, given by x of t, and the vertical position, given by y of t, are functions of a common parameter t, often representing time. As t flows forward from a starting value to an ending value, the point formed by the coordinate pair x of t and y of t traces out a path in the coordinate plane.

To see how this works in practice, let us watch a particle move along a curved path. Suppose the horizontal position is given by the function x of t equals t cubed minus three t, and the vertical position is given by the function y of t equals t squared minus two. As t varies from negative two to positive two, this particle travels along a more complicated cubic-quadratic curve rather than a simple sideways parabola. When t is zero, the position is the coordinate point zero on the horizontal axis and negative two on the vertical axis. As t increases from zero to one, the particle first drifts left and upward to the point negative two, negative one. It then swings back to the right while still rising, crosses the horizontal axis, passes through the point zero, one, where its path crosses itself to close a loop, and ends at the point two, two.

Sometimes we want to connect these parametric equations directly to a standard Cartesian equation by eliminating the parameter t. For instance, we can solve one equation for the parameter or use algebraic identities to combine them into an equation relating only x and y, reinforcing the relationship between parametric and explicit forms.

In calculus, our first major question about any curve is how to find its slope at any given moment. Since both position coordinates depend on our parameter t, how do we find the derivative of y with respect to x? We rely on a clever application of the chain rule. The rate of change of y with respect to x is the rate of change of y with respect to t, divided by the rate of change of x with respect to t, provided that the denominator is not zero. In our notation, the derivative of y with respect to x equals the derivative of y with respect to t divided by the derivative of x with respect to t. If the derivative of x with respect to t is zero while the derivative of y with respect to t is not zero, the curve has a vertical tangent line.

Let us calculate the slope of the tangent line for our particle at the specific moment when t equals two. First, we find the derivative of y with respect to t, which is two t. Evaluating this at t equals two gives four. Next, we find the derivative of x with respect to t, which is three t squared minus three. Evaluating this at t equals two gives three times four minus three, which is nine. Therefore, the slope of the tangent line at that instant is four ninths. This tells us the exact steepness of the trajectory as the particle speeds through that point in the plane.

Motion also involves velocity and speed. We define the velocity vector with horizontal component given by the derivative of x with respect to t and vertical component given by the derivative of y with respect to t. The magnitude of this velocity vector gives the speed of the particle at any time t, computed as the square root of the quantity the derivative of x with respect to t, all squared, plus the derivative of y with respect to t, all squared.

Sometimes, we need even more detailed information about the motion, such as how sharply the curve bends. Just as we defined higher derivatives in earlier modules, we can find the second derivative of y with respect to x. The explicit formula for the second derivative of y with respect to x in terms of t is found by taking the derivative with respect to t of the quantity the derivative of y with respect to t divided by the derivative of x with respect to t, all divided by the derivative of x with respect to t. This measures the acceleration of the turn, telling us whether the path is curving upward or downward at any place. By treating position as a pair of coupled equations rather than a single static curve, we unlock the mathematics of real world trajectories, orbits, and mechanical linkages.

As we continue our journey through parametric and polar coordinate systems, we will soon discover how to calculate the exact lengths of these winding paths and the areas they enclose.

## Key ideas
- Parametric equations define both horizontal and vertical coordinates as separate functions of a shared independent parameter, usually representing time.
- Tracing a parametric curve reveals paths, such as loops and intersecting lines, that standard single variable functions cannot represent.
- The velocity vector combines horizontal and vertical rates of change, and its magnitude yields the scalar speed of the moving point.
- The first derivative of a parametric curve with respect to spatial coordinates is found by dividing the derivative of the vertical function by the derivative of the horizontal function with respect to the parameter, noting that a zero in the denominator indicates a vertical tangent.
- Higher order derivatives follow a specific quotient structure with respect to the parameter divided by the derivative of the horizontal position, measuring the curvature and bending of the path.
