# Carbohydrates and Lipids

Carbohydrates and lipids are two of the most abundant families of biological molecules. Both are built from carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, yet the way those atoms are connected gives each class very different properties. Carbohydrates tend to dissolve easily in water and can serve as quick-acting fuel or as sturdy structural components. Lipids, by contrast, contain large non-polar regions that repel water, allowing them to store energy for long periods, insulate, waterproof, and form the flexible barriers that define every cell.

The simplest carbohydrates are single-sugar units called monosaccharides. Glucose, the sugar most cells use for energy, adopts a ring shape in water because its carbonyl group reacts with a neighboring hydroxyl group. The many hydroxyl groups on the ring make glucose highly polar, so it mixes readily with the watery interior of cells and travels easily in the bloodstream.

When two monosaccharides join, they lose a molecule of water and become a disaccharide. Table sugar, or sucrose, is a glucose-fructose pair linked in this way. Cells can keep adding sugars, creating long chains called polysaccharides. The type of bond between glucose units determines the polymer’s function: alpha-1,4 bonds produce starch in plants, a compact storage form that enzymes can break down quickly; a more highly branched version called glycogen stores glucose in animal liver and muscle; beta-1,4 bonds generate cellulose, whose straight chains line up to form rigid fibers that give plant cell walls strength. Because most animals lack the enzyme to cut beta-1,4 bonds, cellulose passes through us as dietary fiber.

Lipids are defined by the presence of sizable non-polar regions. Many lipids are amphipathic, meaning they possess both a water-loving (polar) head and a water-fearing (non-polar) tail. This dual nature is essential for building cellular membranes. The most familiar neutral lipids are triglycerides, which consist of a three-carbon glycerol backbone attached to three fatty-acid chains. Fatty acids may be saturated, with only single bonds between carbon atoms, or unsaturated, with one or more double bonds. Saturated tails are straight and pack tightly, making butter solid at room temperature; unsaturated tails have kinks that keep oils fluid. Because a fatty-acid chain contains roughly twice as many carbon-hydrogen bonds per gram as a carbohydrate, triglycerides store about twice as much energy per unit mass, explaining why fats serve as long-term energy reserves.

Beyond storage, lipids perform many other roles. Subcutaneous fat insulates the body, reducing heat loss, while waxes on plant leaves and animal fur provide waterproofing. In the nervous system, a lipid-rich sheath called myelin wraps around axons, dramatically speeding nerve impulse transmission. Certain fatty acids cannot be made by animals and must be obtained from the diet; these essential fatty acids include the omega-three family (for example, alpha-linolenic acid) and the omega-six family (for example, linoleic acid). Both serve as precursors for signaling molecules that regulate inflammation and blood clotting.

When a triglyceride is modified so that only two fatty-acid tails remain attached to glycerol and a phosphate group is added to the third position, the molecule becomes a phospholipid. The phosphate group is usually linked to a small polar head such as choline or serine. In water, the polar heads face outward while the tails cluster together, causing phospholipids to spontaneously arrange into a double-layered sheet, the phospholipid bilayer. This bilayer forms the core of every biological membrane, creating a sealed compartment that lets the cell maintain a distinct internal chemistry.

Other important membrane lipids include glycolipids, which attach carbohydrate chains to a lipid anchor for cell-cell recognition, and sphingolipids, which contain a long-chain amino alcohol called sphingosine and are abundant in nerve tissue. Steroids represent a third major lipid class; they consist of four fused carbon rings rather than long tails. Cholesterol, the best-known steroid, inserts among phospholipids in animal membranes, modulating fluidity and preventing the bilayer from becoming too rigid at low temperature. It also serves as the precursor for hormones such as estrogen, testosterone, and corticosteroids that regulate metabolism and immune responses.

Cells extract energy from lipids through fatty-acid beta-oxidation. In each cycle of this pathway, a two-carbon fragment is removed from the fatty-acid chain, forming acetyl-coenzyme A, which then enters the citric-acid cycle. Because each round generates reduced cofactors that feed the electron-transport chain, the oxidation of a single fatty acid yields many more molecules of ATP than the oxidation of a glucose molecule.

## Key ideas
- Carbohydrates range from simple, water-soluble sugars to large polymers that store energy or provide structural strength, depending on how their sugar units are linked.
- Lipids include neutral triglycerides for dense energy storage, amphipathic phospholipids that form cellular membranes, and specialized classes such as glycolipids, sphingolipids, and steroids.
- Essential fatty acids—omega-three and omega-six families—must be obtained from the diet because animals cannot synthesize them.
- Fatty-acid beta-oxidation breaks down fatty acids into acetyl-coenzyme A, producing large amounts of ATP for cellular work.
- The amphipathic nature of phospholipids drives spontaneous bilayer formation, establishing the compartmentalized environment required for life.
