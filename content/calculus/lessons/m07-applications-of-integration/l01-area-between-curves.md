# Area Between Curves

Imagine you are standing in a vast, rolling meadow, and you want to wrap a ribbon around a patch of ground shaped by two crossing hills. The lower hill dips down, the upper hill arches high overhead, and your job is to find the total flat acreage trapped between them. In our last journey through calculus, we learned how the definite integral acts as an accumulator, adding up thin slices of area under a single curve down to the horizontal axis. But what happens when we want the space between two curves, floating somewhere in the middle of the coordinate plane, untethered from the bottom baseline?

To solve this, we can return to the humble Riemann sum, named for the nineteenth-century mathematician Bernhard Riemann. It rests on the calculus of Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, whose seventeenth-century priority dispute was bitter. Newton used dots and fluxions while Leibniz gave us the long stretched letter s for summation and the differential notation d x that we still use today. When we build rectangles to estimate area, instead of stretching a single rectangle all the way down to the horizontal axis, we now pinch each rectangle between an upper function and a lower function.

Let us picture this precisely. Suppose we have two functions of x, where the first function sits above the second function across a specific horizontal span. We want to find the total area of the region bounded between these two curves from a starting x value on the left to an ending x value on the right. If we slice this region vertically into thin strips, the height of any individual vertical strip is simply the upper function value minus the lower function value. The width of that strip is our tiny change in x, which we call d x.

When we let the number of strips grow infinitely large, the sum turns into a definite integral. The rule for finding the area between two curves is wonderfully direct. You take the integral from your left starting point to your right ending point of the upper function minus the lower function, with respect to x. Every single area problem of this type boils down to three clear steps. First, find where the two curves intersect to establish your boundaries. Second, determine which function is larger on that interval. Third, set up and evaluate the definite integral of the upper function minus the lower function.

Let us walk through a concrete example. Suppose our upper curve is a downward opening parabola defined as four minus x squared, and our lower curve is a simple horizontal line defined as the constant zero, which is the horizontal axis itself. First, we find where they cross by setting four minus x squared equal to zero. This gives us intersection points at negative two and positive two. On the interval from negative two to positive two, the parabola sits entirely above the horizontal axis.

Now we set up our integral. We integrate the quantity four minus x squared, with respect to x, from negative two to positive two. To solve this, we find the antiderivative term by term. The antiderivative of four is four x, and the antiderivative of negative x squared is negative one third x cubed. We evaluate this entire expression at the upper limit of two, and then subtract the result evaluated at the lower limit of negative two.

Plugging in positive two gives us eight minus eight thirds, which simplifies to sixteen thirds. Plugging in negative two gives us negative eight plus eight thirds, which simplifies to negative sixteen thirds. When we subtract that lower result from the upper result, we get sixteen thirds minus negative sixteen thirds, which gives a final total area of thirty-two thirds. To check our work, we can take the derivative of our antiderivative expression, which brings us right back to our original integrand of four minus x squared.

Sometimes, functions are laid out more naturally as functions of y rather than x, where slicing horizontally makes the calculation much cleaner. As we move forward into our next lesson, we will take these very same slicing concepts and rotate our regions through space to discover how to calculate the volumes of three-dimensional solids.

## Key ideas
- The definite integral can be used to find the exact area of regions bounded between two or more curves by subtracting the lower function from the upper function.
- The boundaries of integration are typically found by setting the intersecting functions equal to each other to solve for the endpoints.
- Slicing a region vertically results in an integral with respect to x, while slicing horizontally uses an integral with respect to y.
- Evaluating the area requires finding the antiderivative of the difference between the functions and applying the fundamental theorem of calculus across the given limits.
