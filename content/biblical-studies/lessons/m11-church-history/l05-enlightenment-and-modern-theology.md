# The Enlightenment and Modern Theological Shifts

If you walk into a modern university laboratory, you expect to find instruments that measure the physical world, data that can be replicated, and conclusions based on observable evidence. This commitment to empirical proof is one of the lasting legacies of the Enlightenment, an intellectual movement that swept across Europe during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Building on the scientific revolution pioneered by figures like Galileo and Isaac Newton, many of whom were devout believers, thinkers began to champion human reason as the ultimate arbiter of truth. In our last lesson, we saw how the Reformation and Catholic Reform wrestled with questions of ecclesiastical authority and the interpretation of scripture. Now, as the modern era dawns, the church faces a fundamentally different kind of challenge: not a dispute over papal power or sacramental grace, but a sweeping cultural shift that questioned whether divine revelation was necessary at all.

To understand how Christian thought changed during this period, we have to look closely at the rise of rationalism and deism. René Descartes, who tried to rebuild knowledge on what could not be doubted, and John Locke, who grounded knowledge in experience, were both Christians, yet their methods encouraged people to test inherited traditions by reason alone. Soon, many intellectuals embraced deism, the view that God created the universe, set its natural laws in motion, and then stepped back, leaving the world to run like a giant clockwork mechanism without divine intervention. Deists rejected miracles, divine inspiration, and traditional dogmas, retaining only a belief in a distant creator and a basic moral code.

A sharp intellectual challenge to this worldview came from the Scottish philosopher David Hume. In his famous critique, Hume argued that human experience is our only guide to probability, and since uniform natural laws govern the universe, no testimony regarding a miracle can ever outweigh the vast empirical evidence of regular natural operations. This philosophical skepticism posed a severe difficulty for Christian apologetics, because traditional defenses of the faith relied heavily on the historical reliability of biblical miracles, especially the resurrection of Christ. Christian thinkers such as George Campbell replied that Hume's argument assumed what it set out to prove, and the debate continues today.

Shortly after Hume, the German philosopher Immanuel Kant reshaped the religious landscape from a different angle. In his work on religion within the limits of reason alone, Kant argued that human reason cannot attain theoretical knowledge of metaphysical realities like God, freedom, or immortality. Instead, he reduced religion primarily to morality, suggesting that dogmas and rituals have little inherent value unless they serve ethical duty. Kant's philosophy left a lasting mark on modern thought and heavily influenced the rise of liberal theology, which sought to anchor religious truth in human moral consciousness and subjective experience.

In response to these intellectual pressures, Christian thinkers charted various paths. Some attempted to harmonize Christian faith with modern philosophy, while others, like the Pietists and the Methodist revival led by John Wesley, stressed heartfelt personal faith, and still others reasserted traditional authority. A major turning point arrived in the nineteenth century with the rise of liberal theology, notably in Germany through scholars like Friedrich Schleiermacher. Often called the father of modern liberal theology, Schleiermacher argued that the essence of religion was not dogmatic belief or scientific fact, but an inner feeling of absolute dependence on God. This approach aimed to protect Christian faith from scientific criticism by shifting its ground from objective history to subjective religious experience.

At the same time, historical-critical methods began to examine the Bible not merely as sacred revelation, but as a collection of ancient human texts subject to the same literary scrutiny as any other historical document. Scholars scrutinized the authorship, dating, and composite nature of biblical books. While some believers feared these methods would destroy faith entirely, others saw them as a fruitful way to understand the historical context of the text more deeply. Alongside historical criticism, the nineteenth century also witnessed dramatic scientific developments, most notably Charles Darwin's theory of evolution. The idea that biological life developed over millions of years through natural selection challenged traditional creation accounts and sparked intense debates between science and theology that continue to echo in our own day, though some Christian thinkers, such as the American botanist Asa Gray, argued from the start that evolution was compatible with belief in a Creator.

These shifts deeply fractured Western Christianity. Many mainline Protestant denominations increasingly embraced modernism, adapting their doctrines to fit contemporary scientific and philosophical frameworks. Conversely, other groups reacted strongly against these trends. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the fundamentalist movement arose to defend what it viewed as non-negotiable core doctrines of the faith, such as the inerrancy of scripture and the bodily resurrection of Christ. Meanwhile, the Roman Catholic Church officially resisted modernism, particularly under Pope Pius the Tenth, who famously condemned it as a synthesis of all heresies, while still encouraging careful biblical scholarship within traditional boundaries.

The Enlightenment and the modern era forced the church to articulate its faith in a world that no longer automatically accepted Christian presuppositions. By testing old certainties against new methods of inquiry, this turbulent epoch reshaped the landscape of Christian theology, setting the stage for the global, pluralistic church we encounter in our own time.

## Key ideas
- The Enlightenment and the scientific revolution championed human reason and empirical observation, challenging traditional Christian reliance on divine revelation and ecclesiastical authority.
- Deism emerged as a popular intellectual view that pictured God as a distant creator who established natural laws but did not intervene in the world through miracles.
- David Hume critiqued miracles by arguing that uniform natural laws outweigh human testimony, posing a major challenge to traditional Christian apologetics.
- Immanuel Kant argued that religion must be understood within the limits of reason alone, reducing dogmas to moral duties and heavily influencing liberal theology.
- Nineteenth-century liberal theology, pioneered by figures like Friedrich Schleiermacher, sought to preserve the Christian faith by grounding it in human religious experience rather than objective historical dogmas.
- The development of historical-critical biblical scholarship and evolutionary science forced theologians to rethink their approaches to scripture and creation, dividing the church between modernizing adaptations and defensive fundamentalism.
