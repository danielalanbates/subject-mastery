# Fundamental Trigonometric Identities

Imagine you are looking at a map of a complex city. You have several different ways to describe how to get from the town square to the library. You could give directions using a straight-line distance and a compass heading, or you could describe the path as a series of movements along north-south and east-west streets. Even though these descriptions sound completely different, they refer to the exact same destination. In mathematics, trigonometric identities serve as these different ways of describing the same relationship. While our previous look at inverse trigonometric functions allowed us to find missing angles, identities allow us to rewrite complex expressions in simpler, more useful forms.

An identity is a mathematical statement that remains true for every possible value of the variable involved. Unlike a conditional equation, which might only be true for specific values of a variable, an identity is a universal truth within its domain. For example, the statement that the quantity x plus one squared equals x squared plus two x plus one is an identity because no matter what number you pick for x, both sides will always balance. If you pick three, the left side is the quantity three plus one squared, which is four squared, or sixteen. The right side is three squared plus two times three plus one, which is nine plus six plus one, also sixteen.

The most basic trigonometric identities come directly from the definitions of the six trigonometric functions on the unit circle. Since we defined cosecant as the reciprocal of sine, secant as the reciprocal of cosine, and cotangent as the reciprocal of tangent, we have our reciprocal identities. For any angle theta, the cosecant of theta is one divided by the sine of theta, the secant of theta is one divided by the cosine of theta, and the cotangent of theta is one divided by the tangent of theta. Similarly, we have quotient identities, which remind us that the tangent of theta is the sine of theta divided by the cosine of theta, and the cotangent of theta is the cosine of theta divided by the sine of theta.

These definitions lead us to the most famous set of relationships, the Pythagorean identities. On the unit circle, any point has coordinates of cosine of theta and sine of theta. Because the radius of the unit circle is one, the Pythagorean theorem tells us that the square of the x-coordinate plus the square of the y-coordinate must equal one squared. This gives us the fundamental identity: the square of the cosine of theta plus the square of the sine of theta equals one. If we divide this entire equation by the square of the cosine of theta, we discover a second identity: one plus the square of the tangent of theta equals the square of the secant of theta. Dividing by the square of the sine of theta gives us a third: the square of the cotangent of theta plus one equals the square of the cosecant of theta.

We also have even and odd properties that describe how functions behave when we plug in a negative angle. Because the cosine function represents an x-coordinate on the unit circle, the cosine of negative theta is exactly the same as the cosine of theta, making it an even function. However, the sine function represents a y-coordinate, which flips sign when you rotate in the opposite direction. Therefore, the sine of negative theta equals negative sine of theta, making it an odd function.

To verify an identity, you must prove that both sides are equivalent. The golden rule here is to work on only one side of the equation at a time. Do not move terms across the equals sign, as that would assume the identity is already true before you have proven it. Let us verify that the quantity one minus the square of the sine of theta, all divided by the cosine of theta, is equal to the cosine of theta. We start with the left side. We know that the square of the cosine of theta plus the square of the sine of theta equals one. By subtracting the square of the sine of theta from both sides, we see that one minus the square of the sine of theta is equal to the square of the cosine of theta. Substituting this into our expression, we get the square of the cosine of theta divided by the cosine of theta. When the cosine of theta is nonzero, this simplifies to just the cosine of theta, which matches our right side. The original fraction is undefined when the cosine is zero, so those angles remain excluded. To check this, let us use the angle zero. The sine of zero is zero, so the left side is the quantity one minus zero squared, divided by the cosine of zero, which is one divided by one, or one. The right side is the cosine of zero, which is also one. Since both sides equal one, the identity holds.

## Key ideas
- An identity is an equation that is true for every value of the variable within its domain.
- Reciprocal and quotient identities allow us to express all six trigonometric functions in terms of sine and cosine.
- Pythagorean identities are derived from the unit circle and describe the relationship between the squares of trigonometric functions.
- Even and odd properties define the symmetry of trigonometric functions relative to the origin or the y-axis.
- When verifying an identity, you must manipulate only one side of the equation until it matches the other side and then confirm the result with a specific value.
