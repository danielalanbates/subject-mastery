# Orthodoxy Under the Ottomans and Tsars

Imagine a vast empire where your religious identity determines your legal status, your taxes, and the very structure of your daily life. This was the reality for Christians living under the Ottoman Turks after the fall of Constantinople in 1453. While Protestant Reformers in the West were debating justification and church governance, their Orthodox counterparts in the East were navigating a different challenge: survival and preservation under an Islamic state. The Ottoman authorities organized non-Muslims into communities called millets. The Orthodox Church was granted the status of a recognized millet, which gave the patriarch in Constantinople significant authority over his people. He was responsible for collecting taxes and maintaining order, but this power came with the heavy burden of answering to a sultan who could depose him at any time.

This arrangement created a complex dynamic. The church was protected, yet it lived in a state of constant vulnerability. Life under the Ottomans meant that the church became not just a spiritual home, but an essential social and political institution. It held the community together amidst the pressures of a foreign administration. Despite these constraints, the Orthodox tradition remained deeply influential, preserving its liturgy and theology while serving as the primary advocate for its people within the Ottoman legal system.

Meanwhile, far to the north, the Russian Church was experiencing a dramatic transformation. As Constantinople declined, Moscow increasingly saw itself as the new center of the Orthodox world. In 1589, the Russian Church achieved a major milestone by establishing its own patriarchate. This was a bold move that signaled Russia’s emergence as a major power and a defender of the faith. After the upheaval of the Time of Troubles in the early 1600s, when foreign armies occupied Moscow, the church regained its footing. However, in the 1650s, Patriarch Nikon introduced significant liturgical reforms intended to align Russian practices more closely with Greek models. These changes sparked a fierce reaction from those who believed that the Russian way of worship was the only path to salvation and that the reforms were a betrayal of ancient custom.

These traditionalists, known as the Old Believers, refused to accept the changes. They maintained that the original Russian customs were essential to their faith. The resulting schism was profound and painful. It led to intense persecution, as the state and the official church sought to suppress the Old Believers, many of whom fled into the wilderness to preserve their way of life. This divide highlighted a recurring tension in Russian history: how much should the church adapt to external influences, and what must be held firm at all costs?

This tension reached a breaking point under Tsar Peter the Great in the early eighteenth century. Peter was determined to modernize Russia and bring it into line with European standards. He viewed the independent power of the church as a rival to his own authority. In 1721, he abolished the patriarchate, replacing it with a Holy Synod. This was effectively a government department, headed by a lay official appointed by the tsar. For the next two centuries, the Russian Church operated under the firm hand of the state. While this allowed the church to be integrated into the machinery of imperial administration, it also stripped it of its ability to act as an independent voice for the people.

These developments in the East contrast sharply with the movements we discussed in previous lessons. While the Puritans and Pietists were seeking to purify or deepen individual faith within a rapidly changing Protestant landscape, the Orthodox churches were preoccupied with maintaining stability in the face of imperial power, whether that power resided in the palace of a sultan or the throne of a tsar. The Orthodox Church’s experience during this era underscores the enduring challenge of maintaining the integrity of the faith when the state demands total allegiance.

As we look back at this period, we see a church that was often caught between the demands of earthly rulers and the call to spiritual fidelity. The resilience of the Orthodox communities under the Ottomans and the internal struggles within the Russian Church set the stage for the dramatic shifts that would follow. The intellectual and social landscape of Europe was about to undergo a seismic change, as new ideas about science, reason, and human progress began to challenge the traditional foundations of both church and society.

## Key ideas

- The Ottoman Empire managed its Christian subjects through the millet system, which granted the Orthodox Church limited self-governance in exchange for political loyalty and tax collection.
- The establishment of the Moscow patriarchate in 1589 marked the rise of Russia as a significant center for the Orthodox faith, independent of the older Mediterranean patriarchates.
- The Old Believer schism was a transformative conflict in Russia, triggered in the 1650s by resistance to liturgical reforms that traditionalists viewed as a departure from ancient, sacred customs.
- Peter the Great’s restructuring of the Russian Church in 1721 replaced the independent patriarch with a state-run Holy Synod, subordinating ecclesiastical authority to the interests of the imperial government.
